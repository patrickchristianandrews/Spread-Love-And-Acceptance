For teachers · Handout 7

How feelings work: naming what you feel

Treat a feeling as a signal, notice it in your body, and put a word to it, so you can choose what to do next.

Three key ideas

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict. It tells you something matters, but it can be wrong about the details, like a smoke alarm going off for burned toast.
  2. Feelings often start in the body: a racing heart, a hot face, a knot in the stomach. Hunger, tiredness and pain can feel a lot like being upset.
  3. Naming a feeling helps you hold it. A guess is fine (“something like worried”), and a more exact word than “bad” or “fine” points to what would help.

Talk it over

  1. Where in your body do you tend to notice a strong feeling first?
  2. “Is this the fire, or the toast?” When has a feeling been right about something mattering, but wrong about the details?
  3. Anger is often a cover for hurt or worry. Why might anger feel easier to show?
  4. What’s the difference between feeling angry and acting on it? What helps you wait before you act?
  5. Some families and cultures don’t talk much about feelings. How else do people show what they feel?

Try this week

Once a day, name one feeling, out loud or on paper. Try one word more exact than “bad” or “fine”: “left out,” “let down,” “nervous.” A guess still counts. You don’t have to share it with anyone.

Teens version

Before a test, your stomach knots. Is it fear, or did you skip breakfast? After a group chat goes quiet, you feel “annoyed.” Underneath, is it feeling left out? Try: “I think what I’m feeling is…” and finish it with one word. Nobody else needs to see it.

No one has to share, and passing is always fine.

A simple 50-minute lesson

One way to use How feelings work, with handout 7. Swap in any chapter.

  1. Opener (5 minutes). “Think of a time your body reacted before you knew why: a racing heart, a hot face. Just think, no need to share.”
  2. Read one idea together (10 minutes). Show one idea card, such as “Naming a feeling helps you hold it.” Read it aloud, including the example.
  3. Practice in pairs (15 minutes). One person describes a made-up situation. The other says back what they heard and guesses one feeling word: “So you felt left out?” Swap.
  4. Talk it over (15 minutes). Use two questions from handout 7. Anyone can pass.
  5. Exit line (5 minutes). Each student writes one feeling word they could use this week, privately.

Keeping it safe in a classroom

Talking about feelings and families can stir things up. A few habits help:

  • Passing is always fine. Nobody has to share anything personal. Made-up examples work just as well.
  • Follow your school’s safeguarding policy if a student tells you something worrying, and know who your safeguarding lead is before the lesson.
  • Shared computers: anything typed into the tools stays in that browser. What stays on this device shows how to clear it.
