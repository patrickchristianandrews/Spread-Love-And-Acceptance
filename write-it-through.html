Talk it through in writing: write it, wait, then send

Some things are easier to sort out on paper than face to face. Writing gives you time to settle, lets each person finish a thought without being cut off, and means both of you have the same words to look back on. This page has a short guide and a simple tool: write one thing, wait a little, then send it as a private link. It works for partners, co-parents and exes, family, friends, housemates, coworkers, and groups of more than two.

Why writing can help

  • Time to settle. You can write it, leave it, and read it again calmer. Big feelings fade a little in twenty minutes; written words wait.
  • No interrupting. Each person gets to finish a whole thought, and the other reads all of it before answering.
  • Words checked before they land. You see your words before they do, so you can take out the sharp ones you didn’t mean.
  • The same record. You both have the same words to look back on, so nobody has to argue about who said what.

When writing isn’t the right tool

  • If you’re afraid of the other person, a well-worded message isn’t the answer. Your safety is. See Not safe at home? for free, private people to talk to.
  • If you’re flooded right now (heart racing, can’t think straight), don’t write yet. Try Upset right now? first, then come back.
  • Big news and big apologies are often better said out loud, with a face and a voice. A short note can open the door: “Can we talk tonight? I owe you an apology.”
  • Never as a way to avoid ever talking. Writing is a bridge back to talking, not a wall. When things are calmer, switch back to talking.

House rules for written disagreements

  1. One topic per message. “And another thing” starts a second argument.
  2. Facts, not verdicts. “The rent was paid on the 5th” is a fact. “You’re useless with money” is a verdict.
  3. No always or never, no sarcasm, no ALL CAPS, and no “you” accusations (“you don’t care”, “you made me”). Say how it was for you instead.
  4. Say what you’re asking for. One clear thing the other person can say yes, no or not yet to.
  5. Give a reply-by time, and respect it. No chasing before then.
  6. No sending late at night. Tired words land harder. It will keep until morning.
  7. The reader replies to the ask, not the tone. If a word stings, you can still answer the request.
  8. It’s okay to say “I need until tomorrow.” Taking time is not ignoring someone.
  9. Switch back to talking when it’s calmer. Writing is for the hard part, not forever.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t writing colder than talking?

It can be, if it’s used to score points. Used this way, with one topic, how it landed for you and a clear ask, it is often warmer than a rushed talk, because you have time to choose kind words. Switch back to talking once things are calmer.

What if they don’t reply by the time I asked?

Give it a little grace. Then send one short, calm nudge, not ten: “Just checking you saw my message. I’d still like to sort it out.” If messages keep going unanswered, raise that on its own, at a calm time.

Who can see what I write here?

Only you, and the person you send the link to. The conversation is kept in this browser on this device. The link carries the message after the #, a part of a web address that browsers never send to any website. You can erase a conversation at any time.

Can I use it with more than one person?

Yes. Send the same link to each person. Each reply comes back to you on its own. When you’ve agreed something, share the “What we agreed” card with everyone.

What if they write something unkind back?

You can answer the ask and leave the tone. If it crosses a line, it’s fine to say: “I want to sort this out, and I’m not okay being spoken to like that. Let’s pick it up tomorrow.” If you feel afraid, see Not safe at home?
