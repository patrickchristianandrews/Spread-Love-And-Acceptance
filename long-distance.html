Maybe one of you works away, studies in another city, is waiting for a visa or is deployed, or you have never lived in the same place. Being apart takes away the small, easy moments that keep people close: the cup of tea, the passing hug, the look across the room. This page is about building those back on purpose. Some studies have found that couples living apart can feel as close as couples who live together, partly because they tend to share more when they do talk, and to make their time count. The research is small and mostly about students, so take it as hope, not a promise.

Time zones: find your overlap

  • Write down both days side by side. When each of you wakes, works, eats and sleeps, in your own time. Mark the hours when you are both awake and free. That overlap is precious, so protect it.
  • Say whose time you mean. “Call at eight, your time” saves a lot of missed calls. A second clock on your phone helps.
  • Take turns with the awkward hours. If one of you is always up late or early for the call, swap now and then, or at least say thank you out loud.
  • Watch for the tired call. The end of one person’s long day and the start of the other’s is not the time for a hard topic.

Check-ins on a video call

A check-in works over video too. A few changes help:

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Check-ins on a video call
  • One screen, or one each. Share your screen so you both look at the same page, or each open the page on your own phone or laptop. Either works.
  • Take turns reading the prompts. One of you reads a step out loud, then you swap. It keeps you both in it, and it slows things down.
  • Set up the room at both ends. Somewhere private, headphones if others are around, other notifications off.
  • Agree what happens if the call drops. A bad connection is nobody’s fault. “If we cut out, I’ll call back once, then text” saves a lot of worry.
  • Keep hard topics for calls, not texts. Faces and voices carry what text can’t.
  • End on purpose. Say when you’ll talk next before you hang up, so the call doesn’t just fade out.

Who calls, and how often: agree it

When you live apart, calls can quietly turn into a scorecard. One of you notices who always calls first, who cut the last call short, who forgot to text good morning. If one of you is doing most of the reaching out, that’s real information, not scorekeeping. And if one of you calls less, it’s usually about the hours, not the love.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Who calls, and how often: agree it

Agree a rhythm instead, so neither of you has to carry it alone, guess or keep score:

  • How often. A long call on set days and short check-ins in between, or whatever fits your lives.
  • Who starts. Take turns, or one of you always calls because their day is easier to plan. Either is fine once you have agreed it.
  • What counts. A voice note, a photo, a two-line text. People feel close through different things, so say what works for you.
  • What happens when life gets busy. “If I can’t make our call, I’ll text before it, with a new time.”

Words you could use

If you usually reach out: “I’ve been the one starting most of our calls, and I miss feeling chosen. Can we agree a rhythm, so it isn’t always on me?”

If you call less: “I need a bit less phone time in busy weeks. It isn’t you, and I don’t want you to feel you’re always the one reaching out. Could we do short calls on weekdays, a long one on Sunday, and I’ll start the Sunday one?”

If a call turns into an argument and one of you needs to hang up, agree a time to call back. When one of you wants to talk now and the other needs space has a pause plan you can fill in together.

Small bids across the distance

Psychologist John Gottman described “bids”: small reaches for attention, like “Look at this,” a sigh or a joke. In his studies, couples who stayed together turned toward each other’s bids far more often than couples who split up. Apart, the bids just change shape:

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Small bids across the distance
  • a photo of something that made you think of them,
  • a voice note from your walk,
  • “Thinking of you” before their big meeting, and “How did it go?” after,
  • watching the same show at the same time, or reading the same book,
  • a small thing in the mail.

And when they reach for you, answer when you can, even briefly: a heart, “Ha, love that,” or “Can’t talk, call you at seven.” A short reply still turns toward them. Turning toward has seven small ways to do this.

Bonding tips across the distance

Small bids keep you in touch day to day. Doing things together, even on two screens, is what makes the distance feel smaller.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Bonding tips across the distance
  • Do something “together, apart.” Cook the same recipe on a video call, play an online game, or start the same movie at the same time and text through it.
  • Leave the call on sometimes. No need to talk the whole time. Folding laundry or reading while the other is “in the room” feels a lot like an ordinary evening.
  • Send something they can hold. A letter, a postcard, a sweater that smells of you. It is still there after the call ends.
  • Keep a shared list of places to go, movies to watch and things to do on the next visit. It turns “one day” into a plan.
  • Read or listen together. A chapter of a book at bedtime, or a podcast you both play and then talk about.

Words you could use

“Want to cook the same thing on Sunday and eat together on video? I’ll send you the recipe.”

Reading short texts carefully

Over text, “Fine.” can mean “Fine, go ahead” or “I’m upset and I don’t want to say so.” “Ok” can be relaxed or cold. Without a face or a voice, each of you fills in the tone yourself, and a tired or lonely mind tends to fill it in badly.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Reading short texts carefully
  • Ask, don’t assume. “Fine as in good, or fine as in not really?” is a kind question.
  • Say your tone out loud when it matters: “Short reply because I’m on the bus. All good.”
  • Move hard things to a call. If a thread is getting tense: “Can we talk about this on the phone tonight?”

Talking about it: listening from far away

When you’re apart, every call carries a lot. There’s less time, so it can feel as if each one has to go well. A hard topic, a bad connection and two tired people can turn a small thing into a big one.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Talking about it: listening from far away
  • Take turns. Let the other person finish, even with the delay. Lag makes people talk over each other.
  • Say back what you heard before you answer. You can’t always see their face, so checking matters more.
  • Ask what they need: “Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?”
  • Try not to hang up angry. Pause, name a time to call back, and keep it.
  • After a fight over text, go over it once on a call, calmly, not line by line.

Words you could use

“I don’t want to hang up on a bad note. Can we stop here, and talk again tomorrow at eight, your time?”

“Do you want me to help fix it, or just listen?”

Visits and goodbyes

Visits carry a lot of hope, and the first and last days can be tender.

  • Expect a settling-in day. The first day together can feel oddly stiff, because you are switching from phone closeness to being in the same room. It usually passes.
  • Don’t fill every hour. Ordinary time, like cooking or running errands together, is part of what you have been missing.
  • Talk about the plan before you arrive: who you will see, time alone, and any hard topic you want to have face to face.
  • Plan the goodbye. Many people feel low or snappy the day before leaving. Say so, and plan something easy for that day.
  • Put the next visit on the calendar if you can. A date makes the gap easier to carry.
  • Talk about the end of the distance, if there is one. Knowing roughly when and how you will live in the same place can make the time apart lighter.
