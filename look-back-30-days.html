Look back over 30 days The 30-Day Look-Back · for 2 to 8 people

Put four weeks side by side: who did what, last-minute rescues, how much each person is carrying, and whether each job has an owner. You get the running imbalance, what shifted, what didn't, and one next step.

A place to start a conversation, not a verdict. It reads the setup, never a person. Only have a rough "then and now"? Try the quick look-back.

More about this look-back

One week can be a fluke: someone was ill, a deadline landed, a visit took over. Thirty days is long enough to see whether what you changed is changing anything. It is the look-back that week four of the 6-week program asks for: who did what, how many last-minute rescues, and the running imbalance.

CALC-02 puts Pillar I (“See the whole load”) and Pillar II (“Fix the setup, not the person”) to work over a month. In you: you see whether what you tried is helping. Between you and others: one calm picture of the month, never a score on anyone.

      How it's worked out

      Start and end

      Each part is read twice. Start is the average of weeks 1 and 2, using whichever of them you filled in. End is the average of weeks 3 and 4, the same way. A part needs at least one week at each end, or it isn't read. Averaging two weeks keeps one odd week from deciding the read.

      The cut-offs are round numbers chosen to be easy to read, not research findings. A move of 0.09 and a move of 0.10 are really the same month.

      The running imbalance

      gap(person, after week k) = Σ over weeks 1..k of ( their hours − their share × everyone's hours )
hours to change hands     = ½ × Σ |gap| over everyone

      Their share is an even share (1 ÷ people), or the split you agreed on. Weeks with no hours are skipped, so a blank week never counts as an even one. With two people, the hours to change hands is simply how far one person is over.

      Made-up example: Sam and Alex log 14 and 6 hours, then 13 and 7, then 11 and 9, then 10 and 10. Sam's running gap is +4, +7, +8, +8 hours. Over the month, 8 hours would have to change hands to even it out, and all of it built up in the first three weeks. The last week was even, which is the change working. The 8 hours already done stay done.

      The one next step

      • If a part moved the wrong way, the step goes to it. If two did, the one that moved furthest past its cut-off.
      • Otherwise, the first part still worth a look at the end, in this order: jobs without an owner, uneven hours, rescues, a high load. Ownership comes first because it is usually the easiest thing to change, and it often settles the other three.
      • If nothing needs a look, the step is to keep the same rhythm and look back again in 30 days.

      Reading this device

      “Fill in from this device” reads, without changing them: the long fair-split calculator's kept trend log, the Workpaper Suite draft and single worksheet drafts (Who did what, How much are you carrying?, the daily check-in), and the jobs your weekly check-in card was told slipped. It only fills boxes that are still empty, and says what it used. A “week beginning” date is placed by its middle day. Where two entries fall in the same week, the later one is used. Rescues taken from the weekly check-in are the jobs it was told slipped, which is close to, but not the same as, every rescue: check the number.

      What this look-back will never use. Tone of voice, messages, how fast someone replies, or anything about a person that they didn't help enter themselves. A stress-tank score is never filled in for someone else.

      What it is not

      • Not a verdict on who is the better partner, parent or roommate.
      • Not a tested clinical or statistical tool. It predicts nothing. It describes what you typed in.
      • Not a reason to decide a relationship's future. “The look-back says…” is a misuse. Its job is to give you a calm place to start.
