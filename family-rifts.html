This page is for grown brothers and sisters, and other adult family, who have stopped speaking or barely speak. Maybe it started after a parent died. Maybe it was the will, the house or the money. Maybe it has just been years of silence. It is written for both sides: the one who wants to reach out, and the one who isn’t ready.

How family rifts start

Most rifts don’t start with one big fight. They start with a hard time, and then a gap that grows because nobody closes it.

  • After a loss. When a parent dies, the person who held the family together is gone. Old tensions come back, and grief makes everyone shorter with each other.
  • A will, a house or money. It is rarely only about money. A will can feel like a final word on who was loved, trusted or seen.
  • Years of silence. A missed birthday becomes a missed year. After a while, the silence itself feels too big to mention.
  • Feeling like the less-favoured child. If you always felt second, a loss or a will can open that old wound. That hurt is real, even if your sibling saw it differently.

Usually both people have a fair point, and both carry a hurt the other can’t fully see. Nobody here is the villain.

Reaching out after a long silence

After a long gap, short and calm works best. A first message is not the place to settle everything. Its only job is to open a door.

A good first message does three things:

  1. Name the rift. Say plainly that it has been a long time. Pretending nothing happened can feel like brushing it aside.
  2. Own your part. Only yours. Not “I’m sorry if you were upset”, but “I’m sorry for my part in it.”
  3. Make it easy to decline. No deadline, no guilt. The easier it is to say no, the easier it is to say yes.

Words you could use

An invitation: “I know it’s been a long time, and I’m sorry for my part in it. Mia’s christening is on the 14th. No pressure to reply, but I’d really like you there.”

Just opening the door: “I’ve been thinking about you. I miss having my brother around. If you ever want to talk, I’m here. No need to answer now.”

After a parent’s death: “I keep thinking about Dad, and about us. I don’t want the house to be the last thing we ever talked about.”

Leave out of a first message: who was right, the details of the will, “but you also…”, and anything that needs a quick answer. Sarcasm is easy to slip into on either side when you’re hurt. A line like “So now you want to talk?” usually comes from real pain, and it’s fair to feel it. Written down, though, it tends to land harder than you mean. If that’s where you are, the “not yet” words below may say it more truly.

If there’s no answer

  • Give it time. Weeks, not days. Silence often means “I don’t know what to say yet”, not “no”.
  • One gentle follow-up, at most. “No need to reply. I just wanted you to know the door is open.” Then stop.
  • Don’t send a flood. Many messages can feel like pressure, even when they come from love.
  • Small, regular signs can help. A birthday card once a year, with no questions in it, keeps the door open without asking anything.

You can do your part well and still not get the answer you hoped for. That is not a failure. The rest is their choice to make.

“I’m not ready”, or “I don’t have to”

If your sibling has reached out and you feel angry, hurt or simply tired, that is allowed. You don’t owe anyone a quick reply, and you don’t owe anyone a reunion.

It helps to know that forgiving and reconciling are two separate choices. Forgiving is something that happens inside you: letting go of the wish to get even. Reconciling is rebuilding the relationship. You can do one without the other, or neither, or both, in your own time. The Library entry on forgiveness explains the difference.

You can also say “not yet”, and you can set terms: a short meeting, a topic that stays off the table, a third person in the room.

Words you could use

Not yet: “Thank you for writing. I’m not ready to talk yet. I’ll let you know when I am.”

Yes, with terms: “I’ll come to the christening. I’m not ready to talk about Dad’s house there. Can we keep that day about Mia?”

A small step: “I can do a short coffee. I don’t want to go over the will yet.”

No, for now: “I’m not going to reply for a while. Please don’t keep asking. I’ll reach out if that changes.”

Talking about it: the first real conversation

If you do meet or talk, the first conversation carries years of hurt. Each of you may arrive ready to defend yourself, and old roles come back fast. It helps to go in with a small goal: to listen, and to leave the door open.

  • Listen first. Let them say how it was for them, without correcting the facts. Then say back what you heard: “So it felt like I took Dad’s side.”
  • Own your part, and only yours. “I’m sorry I stopped calling” is an apology. “I’m sorry you felt that way” isn’t.
  • One topic. Not the will, the funeral and the last twenty years in one go.
  • Stop before it boils over. If voices rise, end kindly and set another time. Leaving calmly beats staying and saying something you can’t take back.
  • Don’t ask for forgiveness on the spot. They may need time, and so may you.

Words you could use

To open: “I’m not here to argue about who was right. I’d like to hear how it’s been for you.”

If it gets heated: “I don’t want this to go badly. Can we stop here and meet again in a couple of weeks?”

When grief and money get tangled

After a parent dies, you may feel grief and unfairness at the same time. That is common. Money and things can stand for love, trust and who was “the favourite”. So a fight about a house is often also a fight about being seen.

  • Separate the two conversations. One is about the money or the property. The other is about the relationship. Trying to solve both at once usually solves neither.
  • For legal questions, get proper advice. A will, a sale or a claim can have real legal rules and deadlines. A lawyer or solicitor where you live can tell you where you stand.
  • A mediator can help. A family mediator is a neutral person who helps you talk it through and agree next steps. Many people find it calmer than going back and forth alone.
  • Each person grieves differently. A brother who wants to sell quickly may not care less. He may just cope by getting things done. When your family grieves differently explains more.

Words you could use

“I think we both feel it wasn’t fair, in different ways. Can we get some help sorting out the money, so it doesn’t sit between us?”

“The will hurt me. That’s about how I felt growing up, not only about the house.”

Old roles at the table

When grown siblings meet, old roles come back fast: the responsible one, the baby, the peacemaker, the one who always gave in. A sibling can still sound like the old scorekeeper, even after twenty years.

It helps to remember: you can respond as the adult you are now, not the role you had growing up. Take a breath before you answer. Ask yourself what you would say to a friend in the same spot.

Words you could use

“I don’t want us to fall back into the old patterns. I’d like us to talk as two adults.”

If you both want it: rebuilding closeness slowly

This part is only for when both of you want to be back in touch. After a rift, closeness usually comes back in small, safe steps, not in one big talk about the past. Going slowly is not a failure. It is how trust grows back.

  • Keep early meetings short, with an easy end. A coffee or a walk with a set finish time is easier than a whole day.
  • Meet around something to do. A match, a garden centre, sorting a box of Mum’s photos. A shared task takes the pressure off talking.
  • Share a good memory before a hard one. “Do you remember the caravan holiday?” reminds you both there was more than the rift.
  • Leave the big topics for later, for a calm, agreed time, perhaps with a mediator. Not every meeting has to settle something.
  • Find a small, regular rhythm. A birthday call, a message every few weeks, the same family day each year. Light and regular beats rare and intense.

Words you could use

“It was good to see you. No pressure, but could we do another coffee next month?”

You don’t have to rebuild anything you don’t want to, and nobody can demand closeness from you.
