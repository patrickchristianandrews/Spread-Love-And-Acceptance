Start with these three

A pill can’t fix what is causing the stress: too much on your plate, a hard relationship, money, grief, or poor sleep. The things with the strongest evidence cost nothing. Try these first, in any order:

  1. 1

    Sleep

    Steady wake-up times and a wind-down hour do more than most pills. For long-term insomnia, CBT for insomnia is the recommended first treatment.

    CBT for insomniaGood evidence A regular scheduleEarly evidence

  2. 2

    Movement

    Hundreds of trials show regular activity eases anxiety and low mood. A brisk walk counts.

    See the researchGood evidence

  3. 3

    People

    A friend or someone in your family. Being heard is calming in itself.

    See the researchGood evidence

Two more places to start

  • Daylight and green space

    Get outside in daylight, especially in the morning and in winter.

    Daylight Time in nature

  • Food and drink

    Eat regularly, cut down on caffeine, and go easy on alcohol, which makes anxiety worse afterward.

    Steady meals Less caffeine Less alcohol

And when you need calm right now: breathe out slowly, longer than you breathe in. Take a one-minute Breathe break.

What the evidence labels mean
  • Good evidence many good studies agree.
  • Some evidence several decent studies, with limits.
  • Mixed evidence studies disagree, or it depends how it’s used.
  • Early evidence a few small studies: a maybe.
  • Weak evidence little or poor-quality research.
  • No good evidence tested, and no benefit found.

Therapy or approach

Good evidence

CBT for anxiety and panic (talking therapy)

A recommended first-line treatment for anxiety, panic and social anxiety.

Studied in
UK guidelines for generalized anxiety, panic and social anxiety
Forms
A structured talking therapy with a trained therapist; guided self-help versions exist
    Details

    Watch out This is a professional therapy, delivered by a trained therapist.

    What the research shows

    UK guidelines recommend CBT (or applied relaxation) for generalized anxiety, CBT for panic disorder, and individual CBT developed for social anxiety.

    What it is

    A structured talking therapy that helps you notice and change patterns of thinking and avoiding, often with guided practice. Guided self-help versions exist.

    Used for

    Generalized anxiety, panic, social anxiety and OCD.

    Cautions

    Side effects
    It can feel harder before it feels easier.

    Good to know

    How different neurotypes may respond

    Autistic: CBT adapted for autistic people uses clearer structure, more visual material and less reliance on naming feelings.

    Anxiety or OCD: For OCD, the recommended therapy is CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP) (Oxford Health NHS: OCD (opens a new tab)). For social anxiety, UK guidance recommends individual CBT designed for social anxiety.

    Listed under Anxiety and worry · Panic · Social anxiety · Restlessness and racing thoughts

    Sources: NICE anxiety and panic guideline (CG113) (opens a new tab) · NICE social anxiety guideline (CG159) (opens a new tab)

    CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)

    The recommended first treatment for long-term insomnia, ahead of sleeping pills.

    Studied in
    Guideline for adults with chronic insomnia (American College of Physicians, 2016)
    Forms
    With a therapist, in a group, or through an app or online program
      Details

      Watch out Sleep may get a bit worse in the first weeks before it improves.

      What the research shows

      The American College of Physicians recommends CBT-I as the first treatment for all adults with chronic insomnia (a strong recommendation, based on moderate-quality evidence).

      What it is

      A short course of talking therapy for sleep, with a therapist, in a group, or through an app or online program.

      Used for

      Long-term trouble sleeping.

      Cautions

      Side effects
      Tiredness at first, because part of it limits time in bed.
      Take care if
      you drive or use machines for work, or have epilepsy or bipolar disorder: sleep restriction may need adjusting.

      Good to know

      How different neurotypes may respond

      ADHD: A version that allows for a late body clock fits better.

      Anxiety or OCD: It includes ways to handle a racing mind at night.

        Everyday habit

        Good evidence

        Movement

        The strongest evidence of anything here for low mood, anxiety and stress.

        Studied in
        Hundreds of trials on depression, anxiety and distress
        Forms
        Walking, dancing, cycling, swimming, gardening, strength training
          Details

          Watch out Start gently if you have a heart condition.

          What the research shows

          Hundreds of trials show physical activity eases depression, anxiety and distress. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training did especially well.

          Which kinds of exercise helped most: Noetel and colleagues, 2024 (opens a new tab). For PMS, the NHS lists regular exercise and sleep among things that can help, though the trials are of poor quality (NHS: PMS (opens a new tab)).

          What it is

          Any physical activity: walking, dancing, cycling, swimming, gardening or strength training.

          Used for

          Low mood, anxiety and stress.

          Cautions

          Side effects
          Aches. Start gently if you have been inactive.
          Take care if
          you have a heart or other health condition, or a condition where activity makes you worse later: go gently and pace yourself.

          Good to know

          How different neurotypes may respond

          ADHD: A review of eight trials in children with ADHD found exercise, especially aerobic exercise, helped with attention and hyperactivity (Cerrillo-Urbina and colleagues, 2015 (opens a new tab)).

          Autistic: Predictable, familiar movement often works best: the same walk, swim or stretch at the same time. Sensory-friendly places (quiet times at the pool, headphones) count.

          Anxiety or OCD: A racing heart during exercise can feel like panic at first. Starting gently helps the body learn the feeling is safe.

          Chronic illness or low energy: With ME/CFS or similar conditions, “push through” plans can make you worse. UK guidance says not to use fixed, step-by-step increases. Stay within your energy limits (NICE ME/CFS guideline (NG206) (opens a new tab)).

          Listed under Low mood · Anxiety and worry · Stress · Sleep · Focus and attention · Nervous system regulation · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Burnout and fatigue · Grief and big stress · PMS and menstrual-cycle mood · Menopause-related sleep and mood · Tension and pain-related stress

          Source: Singh and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab)

          Everyday habit

          Good evidence

          People

          Strong social ties go with better health and survival. Being heard is calming.

          Studied in
          148 studies of social ties and survival
          Forms
          Friends, family, neighbors or a group
            Details

            Watch out Low risk.

            What the research shows

            Across 148 studies, people with stronger social ties were more likely to survive over the years studied. That shows a strong link, though it can’t prove cause.

            What it is

            Time with people who leave you calmer: friends, family, neighbors or a group.

            Used for

            Everything. Feeling less alone.

            Cautions

            Side effects
            Some people drain you. Choose the ones who leave you calmer.
            Take care if
            someone frightens or controls you.

            Good to know

            How different neurotypes may respond

            Autistic: Being with people who get you counts most. Many autistic people find time with other autistic people less tiring. Text or side-by-side activities count too.

            Anxiety or OCD: With social anxiety, small, low-pressure contact counts: a message, a walk side by side.

            Listed under Stress · Low mood · Anxiety and worry · Grief and big stress · Nervous system regulation · Social anxiety · Burnout and fatigue

            Source: Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, 2010 (opens a new tab)

            Practice or body-based

            Some evidence

            Expressive writing (journaling)

            Writing about what’s on your mind for a few short sessions has a small but real effect.

            Studied in
            146 studies
            Forms
            Writing freely for 15 to 20 minutes, a few days in a row
              Details

              Watch out It can feel worse for a short while. Stop if it overwhelms you.

              What the research shows

              A review of 146 studies found writing about difficult experiences had a small benefit on average for psychological and physical health. Later reviews disagree on how much it helps.

              What it is

              Writing freely about a stressful experience and your feelings about it, for 15 to 20 minutes, a few days in a row.

              Used for

              Stress, grief and racing thoughts.

              Cautions

              Side effects
              A short dip in mood right after writing is common.
              Take care if
              you are dealing with trauma: writing can bring up strong feelings, so keep sessions short and stop if it becomes too much.

              Good to know

              How different neurotypes may respond

              Autistic: Writing can be easier than saying feelings out loud, especially if naming feelings is hard.

              Dyslexic or dyspraxic: Voice notes or typing count. It’s about getting it out, not spelling.

              Listed under Stress · Grief and big stress · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Low mood

              Source: Frattaroli, 2006 (opens a new tab)

              Device or environment

              Some evidence

              Heart rate variability biofeedback

              Slow breathing guided by a heart-rate sensor. Trials found less stress and anxiety.

              Studied in
              24 studies with 484 people
              Forms
              A sensor on a finger, ear or chest strap, shown on a screen
                Details

                Watch out Apps vary a lot in quality. You don’t need a device to breathe slowly.

                What the research shows

                Across 24 studies (484 people), training with heart-rate biofeedback reduced stress and anxiety compared with control groups. The authors called it promising and asked for more well-controlled trials.

                What it is

                A sensor (on a finger, ear or chest strap) shows your heart rhythm on a screen, so you can learn the breathing pace that makes it smoothest, usually about six breaths a minute.

                Used for

                Stress and anxiety.

                Cautions

                Side effects
                Light-headedness.
                Take care if
                you have a heart rhythm condition: the readings can be unreliable, and this is not a treatment for it.

                Good to know

                How different neurotypes may respond

                ADHD: The live feedback can make slow breathing more engaging.

                Autistic: A screen showing your heart rhythm can make body signals easier to “see” when they are hard to feel.

                Listed under Stress · Anxiety and worry · Nervous system regulation

                Source: Goessl and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)

                  Good to know

                  Everyday habit

                  Some evidence

                  Less caffeine

                  In people with panic disorder, large amounts of caffeine set off panic attacks in about half.

                  Studied in
                  People with panic disorder, in lab studies
                  Forms
                  Cutting down coffee, tea, energy drinks, cola and pre-workout products
                  • Medicine interactions
                  Details

                  Watch out Cut down gradually: stopping suddenly can cause headaches for a few days.

                  What the research shows

                  In lab studies, about half of people with panic disorder had a panic attack after a large dose of caffeine (400 mg or more, about four cups of coffee), compared with very few people without it. Everyday amounts haven’t been studied as well.

                  The NHS self-help advice for anxiety includes cutting down on caffeine (NHS anxiety self-help (opens a new tab)).

                  What it is

                  Cutting down on coffee, tea, energy drinks, cola and pre-workout products, especially after midday.

                  Used for

                  Anxiety, panic and sleep.

                  Cautions

                  Side effects
                  Withdrawal headaches and tiredness for a few days.
                  Take care if
                  you rely on caffeine to stay safe at work or driving: cut down slowly.

                  Good to know

                  How different neurotypes may respond

                  ADHD: Caffeine and ADHD stimulant medicines both raise heart rate and blood pressure, so together they can add up: jitters, a racing heart, poor sleep (Drugs.com interaction checker (opens a new tab)).

                  Anxiety or OCD: People with panic disorder are much more sensitive to caffeine.

                  Listed under Anxiety and worry · Panic · Sleep · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Nervous system regulation

                  Source: Klevebrant and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab)

                  Some evidence

                  Light therapy (light box)

                  A bright light box in the morning eases winter depression more than dim light, in pooled trials.

                  Studied in
                  Trials in seasonal depression; small and varied
                  Forms
                  A bright light box, usually in the morning
                  • Medicine interactions
                  Details

                  Watch out Buy one made for this purpose.

                  What the research shows

                  A review of trials found bright light eased seasonal depression more than dim or fake light, with more people improving. The studies were small and varied.

                  What it is

                  Sitting near a bright light box for a set time each day, usually in the morning.

                  Used for

                  Winter low mood (seasonal affective disorder).

                  Cautions

                  Side effects
                  Eye strain, headache, feeling wired.
                  Take care if

                    Practice or body-based

                    Some evidence

                    Mindfulness and meditation

                    Small improvements in anxiety, low mood and pain. MBCT helps prevent depression coming back.

                    Studied in
                    47 trials; plus a course tested to prevent depression coming back
                    Forms
                    Short apps to eight-week courses
                      Details

                      Watch out Some people feel worse at first. A gentle start helps.

                      What the research shows

                      Across 47 trials, mindfulness programs gave small improvements in anxiety, low mood and pain. They were not better than other active choices, such as exercise. For people who have had depression several times, an eight-week course called MBCT cut the risk of it coming back by about a third.

                      What it is

                      Practices that train attention on the present moment, from short apps to eight-week courses.

                      Used for

                      Anxiety, stress, low mood and pain.

                      Cautions

                      Side effects
                      Some people feel more anxious at first.
                      Take care if
                      you have trauma or severe depression: meditation can stir up difficult feelings, so keep sessions short.

                      Good to know

                      How different neurotypes may respond

                      ADHD: Shorter, moving or guided practices (walking meditation) are easier for many people.

                      Autistic: Body scans can be hard if body signals are faint or overwhelming. Focusing on sounds or an object outside you may suit better.

                      Anxiety or OCD: If meditation stirs up intrusive thoughts, it is fine to stop and try a different approach.

                      Listed under Anxiety and worry · Stress · Low mood · Tension and pain-related stress · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Grief and big stress

                      Sources: Goyal and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab) · Kuyken and colleagues, 2016 (opens a new tab)

                      Practice or body-based

                      Some evidence

                      Progressive muscle relaxation

                      Tensing and then relaxing each muscle group. Relaxation training eases anxiety in many studies.

                      Studied in
                      27 studies of relaxation training
                      Forms
                      Tense each muscle group, then let go
                        Details

                        Watch out Skip any muscle that hurts.

                        What the research shows

                        A review of 27 studies of relaxation training, including progressive muscle relaxation, applied relaxation and meditation, found a medium-sized drop in anxiety. Many studies were not randomized.

                        What it is

                        Tensing one muscle group (hands, shoulders, face…) for a few seconds, then letting go, working through the body.

                        Used for

                        Anxiety, tension and sleep.

                        Cautions

                        Side effects
                        Cramp if you tense too hard.
                        Take care if
                        you have an injury: skip that area.

                        Good to know

                        How different neurotypes may respond

                        ADHD: Short guided recordings keep it moving.

                        Autistic: Tensing and releasing one part at a time can make body signals easier to notice than “just relax.”

                        Listed under Anxiety and worry · Stress · Sleep · Tension and pain-related stress · Restlessness and racing thoughts

                        Source: Manzoni and colleagues, 2008 (opens a new tab)

                        Practice or body-based

                        Some evidence

                        Slow breathing (about six breaths a minute)

                        Breathing out slowly, longer than you breathe in, is linked with feeling calmer. Free and safe.

                        Studied in
                        A review of studies of slow breathing (under ten breaths a minute)
                        Forms
                        About six breaths a minute, with a longer out-breath
                          Details

                          Watch out If you feel dizzy, breathe normally for a moment.

                          What the research shows

                          A review of studies found slow breathing (under ten breaths a minute, often about six) is linked with calmer body signals and feeling less wound up. Try the Breathe break.

                          What it is

                          Slowing your breathing, usually to about six breaths a minute (in for 4 or 5, out for 5 or 6), with a longer out-breath.

                          Used for

                          Stress, worry and panic.

                          Cautions

                          Side effects
                          Light-headedness if you breathe too deeply.
                          Take care if
                          you have a lung or heart condition: go gently.

                          Good to know

                          How different neurotypes may respond

                          ADHD: Short is fine: one minute, with something to watch.

                          Autistic: Focusing on body signals can be hard or uncomfortable. A visual pacer (like the Breathe break) or counting may work better than “notice your breath.”

                          Anxiety or OCD: If watching your breath makes you more anxious, try counting steps, humming, or a longer out-breath without counting.

                          Listed under Anxiety and worry · Stress · Panic · Nervous system regulation · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Sleep · Social anxiety · Tension and pain-related stress · Sensory overload

                          Source: Zaccaro and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)

                                Device or environment

                                Mixed evidence

                                Weighted blanket

                                One adult trial found less insomnia. A trial in autistic children found no sleep benefit.

                                Studied in
                                120 adults with insomnia (Swedish trial); a trial in autistic children
                                Forms
                                Blankets of about 6 to 8 kg in the adult trial
                                  Details

                                  Watch out Never for babies or small children who can’t move it off themselves.

                                  What the research shows

                                  In a Swedish trial, 120 adults with insomnia and depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety or ADHD slept better after four weeks with a weighted chain blanket than with a light one. A trial in autistic children found no extra sleep.

                                  What it is

                                  A heavy blanket filled with beads or chains. The adult trial used blankets of about 6 to 8 kg.

                                  Used for

                                  Sleep, anxiety and deep-pressure comfort.

                                  Cautions

                                  Side effects
                                  Feeling too hot or trapped.
                                  Take care if
                                  you have breathing, circulation or mobility problems, or for any child: a heavy blanket can be unsafe. Never for babies or toddlers.

                                  Good to know

                                  How different neurotypes may respond

                                  ADHD: The adult trial that found better sleep included people with ADHD.

                                  Autistic: In a careful trial of 67 autistic children, a weighted blanket did not help them sleep longer than a normal blanket, but children and parents liked it more (Gringras and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab)). Liking it is a fair reason. It just isn’t a sleep treatment.

                                  Sensory sensitivity: Deep pressure is soothing for many people with sensory sensitivity.

                                  Listed under Sleep · Anxiety and worry · Sensory overload · Nervous system regulation

                                  Sources: Ekholm and colleagues, 2020 (opens a new tab) · Gringras and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab)

                                  Everyday habit

                                  Early evidence

                                  A regular sleep schedule

                                  Steadier sleep times go with better next-day mood in a large study.

                                  Studied in
                                  2,115 first-year doctors wearing sleep trackers
                                  Forms
                                  Going to bed and getting up at about the same time each day
                                    Details

                                    Watch out Low risk.

                                    What the research shows

                                    In a study of 2,115 first-year doctors wearing sleep trackers, more day-to-day variation in sleep went with more depressive symptoms, and steadier sleep with better mood the next day. It shows a link, not proof.

                                    What it is

                                    Going to bed and, especially, getting up at roughly the same time each day.

                                    Used for

                                    Sleep, mood and feeling steady.

                                    Cautions

                                    Side effects
                                    None.
                                    Take care if
                                    you work shifts or care for someone at night: do what you can, and be kind to yourself about the rest.

                                    Good to know

                                    How different neurotypes may respond

                                    ADHD: Sleep timing is often a struggle with ADHD. Aim for steadier wake times first. Medicine timing can affect sleep (NHS: side effects of methylphenidate (opens a new tab)).

                                    Autistic: A predictable wind-down routine (same order, same time) can make sleep easier.

                                    Listed under Sleep · Low mood · Nervous system regulation · Burnout and fatigue

                                    Source: Fang and colleagues, 2021 (opens a new tab)

                                    Practice or body-based

                                    Early evidence

                                    Cold water on your face (the dive reflex)

                                    Cold on the face slows the heart through a reflex. Studies are in healthy volunteers, not in people with anxiety.

                                    Studied in
                                    61 healthy adults; not tested as a treatment for anxiety
                                    Forms
                                    Cold water, or a cold wet cloth, on the face for up to 30 seconds
                                    • Condition caution
                                    Details

                                    Watch out Don’t use ice directly on skin. Don’t hold your breath if you feel faint.

                                    What the research shows

                                    Cold on the face sets off the “dive reflex,” which slows the heart. In a trial of 61 healthy adults, cold on the neck slowed the heart rate and raised heart rate variability, a sign of the calming branch of the nervous system. It hasn’t been tested as a treatment for anxiety.

                                    What it is

                                    Splashing cold water on your face, or holding a cold, wet cloth or cool pack against your cheeks and forehead for up to 30 seconds while you hold your breath briefly or breathe slowly.

                                    Used for

                                    A quick reset when you are wired or panicky.

                                    Cautions

                                    Side effects
                                    Feeling cold. Light-headedness if you hold your breath.
                                    Take care if
                                    you have a heart condition, a slow heart rate, low blood pressure or an eating disorder: skip this one, because cold on the face can slow the heart rate.

                                    Good to know

                                    How different neurotypes may respond

                                    Anxiety or OCD: Some people use it as a quick reset in a surge of panic. It slows the heart. It doesn’t fix what set the panic off.

                                    Sensory sensitivity: Strong cold can be too much. Start with a cool cloth on the cheeks and forehead.

                                    Chronic illness or low energy: Cold on the face can slow the heart rate, so this may not suit a heart condition, low blood pressure or fainting.

                                    Listed under Panic · Nervous system regulation · Restlessness and racing thoughts · Anxiety and worry · Sensory overload

                                    Source: Jungmann and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)

                                    Practice or body-based

                                    Early evidence

                                    Cyclic sighing (the double sigh)

                                    Little known: five minutes a day of long, sighing out-breaths lifted mood in one month-long trial.

                                    Studied in
                                    One one-month remote trial

                                      Details

                                      What the research shows

                                      In a one-month remote trial, people who did five minutes of cyclic sighing a day had a bigger lift in mood and slower breathing at rest than people who did mindfulness meditation. It is one study.

                                      What it is

                                      Breathing in through the nose, then a second short sip of air, then a long, slow breath out through the mouth. Repeat for a few minutes.

                                      Used for

                                      Cautions

                                      Side effects
                                      Light-headedness.
                                      Take care if
                                      you have a lung condition: go gently.

                                      Good to know

                                      Device or environment

                                      Early evidence

                                      Heat (sauna, warm baths)

                                      One small trial found raising body temperature lifted depression for weeks. Much more research is needed.

                                      Studied in
                                      30 adults with depression (one trial, with a clinic device)
                                      Forms
                                      A sauna, a hot bath or a medical heat treatment
                                      • Pregnancy caution
                                      • Condition caution
                                      Details

                                      Watch out Drink water, and never use a sauna after drinking alcohol.

                                      What the research shows

                                      In one trial of 30 adults with depression, a single session of medical whole-body heating lifted mood more than a fake treatment for six weeks. It is one small study, and it used a clinic device, not a home sauna.

                                      What it is

                                      Warming the whole body, in a sauna, a hot bath or a medical heat treatment.

                                      Used for

                                      Cautions

                                      Take care if
                                      you are pregnant, have a heart condition or low blood pressure, or have been drinking: avoid saunas.

                                      Practice or body-based

                                      Early evidence

                                      Humming and singing

                                      Humming and singing slow your out-breath. Group singing shows promise for wellbeing.

                                      Studied in
                                      A review of group singing in people with mental health conditions
                                      Forms
                                      Humming, singing alone, or singing in a group or choir
                                        Details

                                        Watch out Claims that humming “tones your vagus nerve” go further than the research.

                                        What the research shows

                                        Humming and singing make you breathe out slowly, like slow breathing does. Humming also raises nitric oxide in the nose about 15-fold, which is a fact about sinuses, not proof of calm. A review of group singing for people with mental health conditions found improvements, but the studies had a moderate to high risk of bias.

                                        What it is

                                        Humming, singing on your own, or singing in a group or choir.

                                        Used for

                                        Stress, low mood and connection.

                                        Cautions

                                        Side effects
                                        None.
                                        Take care if
                                        singing in front of others feels too much: start alone, or hum.

                                        Good to know

                                        How different neurotypes may respond

                                        Autistic: Humming can be a comforting, self-made sound and can also block out other noise.

                                        Anxiety or OCD: With social anxiety, a choir gives company without having to make small talk.

                                        Listed under Stress · Low mood · Nervous system regulation · Social anxiety

                                        Sources: Williams and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab) · Weitzberg and Lundberg, 2002 (opens a new tab)

                                          Everyday habit

                                          Early evidence

                                          Pacing (energy management)

                                          Planning activity within your energy limits. Recommended by UK guidance for ME/CFS.

                                          Studied in
                                          UK guideline for ME/CFS; not large trials
                                          Forms
                                          Spreading activity across the day and week, resting before you crash
                                            Details

                                            Watch out Low risk.

                                            What the research shows

                                            The UK’s NICE guideline for ME/CFS recommends person-centered energy management, and says not to offer fixed, step-by-step increases in exercise. It is a guideline recommendation rather than the result of large trials.

                                            What it is

                                            Spreading activity across the day and week, resting before you crash, and noticing what drains you.

                                            Used for

                                            Burnout, long-term illness and fatigue.

                                            Cautions

                                            Side effects
                                            None.
                                            Take care if
                                            you have new or worsening fatigue: it can have causes that pacing will not fix.

                                            Good to know

                                            How different neurotypes may respond

                                            Autistic: Many autistic people use pacing to avoid burnout: counting social and sensory demands as “energy spent,” not just physical effort.

                                            Device or environment

                                            Early evidence

                                            Sensory tools (headphones, fidgets, quiet spaces)

                                            Noise-reducing headphones and quieter spaces can lower stress for people who are sound-sensitive. Research is small.

                                            Studied in
                                            Six autistic children (a small study)
                                            Forms
                                            Headphones, earplugs, sunglasses, fidgets, chewables, a quiet space
                                              Details

                                              Watch out Keep volume low if headphones play sound.

                                              What the research shows

                                              A small study of six autistic children found noise-reducing headphones lowered a skin-based stress measure. Research on fidgets and other tools is limited.

                                              What it is

                                              Tools that turn down sensory input or give the body something steady: noise-canceling headphones or earplugs, sunglasses, fidget toys, chewables, deep pressure, a quiet space.

                                              Used for

                                              Sensory overload and feeling overwhelmed.

                                              Cautions

                                              Side effects
                                              None.
                                              Take care if
                                              a child uses chewables: check they are made for chewing.

                                              Good to know

                                              How different neurotypes may respond

                                              ADHD: Fidget tools are popular. Research is limited, but they are cheap and low risk.

                                              Autistic: In a small study of six autistic children, noise-reducing headphones lowered a skin-based measure of stress during the day (Pfeiffer and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)).

                                              Sensory sensitivity: Earplugs, sunglasses, soft fabrics and a quiet corner are “reasonable adjustments,” not fussiness.

                                              Highly sensitive: Dimmer lights, fewer sounds and planned quiet time can lower the background load.

                                              Everyday habit

                                              Early evidence

                                                Details

                                                Watch out Low risk.

                                                What the research shows

                                                In a three-week study, 64 adults rated their hunger and mood five times a day. When people were hungrier, they felt more angry and irritable and less pleasure. It shows a link, not proof.

                                                What it is

                                                Eating regularly so you don’t run on empty.

                                                Used for

                                                Irritability, low mood and feeling wired.

                                                Cautions

                                                Side effects

                                                        What this page leaves out

                                                        A note on supplements, herbs and drugs

                                                        This site does not cover supplements, herbs or drugs in depth. The evidence for them is mixed and keeps changing, and some of them interact with prescribed medicines. “Natural” doesn’t mean safe: plants contain active chemicals. St John’s wort interacts with many medicines. Kava, ashwagandha, skullcap and black cohosh have liver warnings. Supplements aren’t checked like medicines before they are sold in the US, and what is in the bottle can differ from the label.

                                                        A prescribed medicine should never be stopped suddenly, and some need to be reduced slowly. No product or dose information is given on this page.

                                                        Sources: FDA: supplements Q&A (opens a new tab) · NCCIH: herb-drug interactions (opens a new tab)
