Early days: dating and new relationships

Simple versionRead the full guide

The start of something new can feel exciting, wobbly, or both at once. This page is for the first weeks and months: texting, pace, what you each want, a first disagreement, meeting each other’s people, moving in, and ending it kindly if it isn’t right. It is for any age and any orientation. Dating in your twenties, again after a divorce, or after losing a partner at sixty or eighty. Dating as a parent. And dating when you’re neurodivergent and would like the unwritten rules written down.

In short:

  • Different texting styles are normal. Say what you like, and ask what they like.
  • Notice how you feel around them. More like yourself, or less?
  • Most differences are just differences. Pressure, checking up on you, and a “no” that doesn’t count are not.
  • Talk about what you each want, a little at a time, early on.
  • A first disagreement is a first look at how you both repair.
  • Keep your own life: friends, plans and your own pace.
  • Moving in is a decision, not something to slide into.
  • If it isn’t right, end it clearly and kindly.

Texting styles and pace: different is normal

Some people text all day. Some send one long message at night. Some reply in a minute, some in a day. Early on, it’s easy to read a slow reply as “not interested” and a fast one as “too keen”. Usually it’s just habit, work, or how someone’s brain works.

Pace differs too: how soon you want to meet again, say “I like you,” or meet friends. Neither pace is wrong. What helps is saying yours out loud, and asking about theirs.

For example (made up): Priya texts in bursts at lunchtime. Tom works nights and replies at 3am in one long message. For two weeks, each worried the other was cooling off. Then Tom said, “I’m a once-a-day texter, not a not-interested one.” They both relaxed.

Words you could use

“I’m not a big texter during the day. It’s not you. I’ll usually message in the evening.”

“I’d like to take this a bit slowly. I’m enjoying it, and I want to keep enjoying it.”

“Do you like a call or a text better? I want to get it right.”

Just different, or a real concern?

Most early worries are ordinary differences. They’re quieter than you. They plan less, text less, or are closer to their family. These are things to talk about, and often to live with. One thing on its own rarely tells you much about a person.

Saying no, or asking for something out of care or respect for the relationship, is not control. That’s true for partners, family and friends alike. “Text me when you’re home safe” or “I’d rather you didn’t flirt with your ex” is a request you can talk through.

Some patterns are different. Take them seriously, especially when more than one shows up, and even if everything else is lovely:

  • Pushing the pace: pressing you to commit or move in fast, or going cold when you want to slow down.
  • Checking up on you: demanding your passwords or location, reading your messages without agreement, or getting angry when you see friends.
  • Pressure about sex, money, drinking, or anything else after you’ve said no.
  • Your no doesn’t count: they sulk, punish you or keep pushing.
  • You start to feel afraid of their mood, or plan your words around it.
  • Someone you’ve only met online asks for money, for any reason.

Control takes away someone’s freedom more broadly: cutting them off from people, watching them without agreement, controlling their money, threats, or leaving them afraid. A quick test: around them, do you feel more like yourself, or less? Ordinary differences can be talked about. Control can’t be talked away.

Words you could use

“I’m not going to share my location. It isn’t about trust. It’s just how I like things.”

“I said no, and I need that to be okay.”

If it isn’t okay with them, that tells you something important.

Try this: For a first date with someone new, meet somewhere public, get yourself there and back, and tell a friend where you’ll be.

Talking about what you each want

Early on, big questions can feel too soon. But a few gentle ones save a lot of heartache later. You don’t need every answer on the second date. Ask a little more as things get closer.

  • Seeing other people: are you exclusive? Don’t assume either way. If you’re both open to more than one relationship, say how that works for you (More than two has more).
  • Children: wants them, has them, doesn’t want them, not sure.
  • Faith and culture: how much it shapes daily life and family. See Two faiths or cultures.
  • Money: saver or spender, and who pays for what when you go out. See Money together.
  • Sex and closeness: the pace that feels right for each of you. Either of you can say “not yet” or “stop” at any point, every time.
  • Where it’s heading: something casual, something serious, or not sure yet. All are fine if you’re both honest.

Words you could use

“I’m enjoying this. Can I ask where you see it going? There’s no right answer. I just like to know.”

“Are we seeing other people? I’m happy to say where I am first.”

“I’d like to go slowly physically. I’ll tell you when I’m ready, and I’d like you to do the same.”

“Kids are a big one for me. Could we talk about it sometime soon?”

Try this: Each of you writes three things that really matter to you and three you could flex on. Swap, and talk about one of them.

Your first disagreement, done kindly

Everyone disagrees sooner or later. A first disagreement isn’t a sign it’s doomed. It’s your first look at how the two of you repair.

  • Start gently. “I felt let down” lands better than “You always…”
  • One thing at a time. Not a list of everything so far.
  • Listen first, and say back what you heard.
  • Pause if it gets hot, and say when you’ll come back to it.
  • Repair: a small sorry, a joke, a hand on the arm. It counts.

For example (made up): Sam and Alex mixed up a dinner booking. Sam said, “You never plan anything.” Then stopped. “Sorry, that wasn’t fair. I was looking forward to it, and I felt let down. Can we pick another night?” They did.

Words you could use

“Can we start that again? I said it badly.”

“I’m getting upset. Can we take twenty minutes and come back to this?”

“I want to understand. What was that like for you?”

Try this: If one of you wants to talk it out now and the other needs space, make a pause plan together on a calm day.

Meeting friends and family

  • There’s no set timetable. Some people meet friends in week two and family after months. Go at the pace of whoever is slower.
  • Give each other a who’s who before a big meeting, and anything to avoid.
  • Agree a quiet signal that means “I’m ready to go”.
  • Not out to your family, or two different faiths? Talk first. See Coming out: with a partner and Two faiths or cultures.
  • If someone’s family or friends aren’t keen, listen, but don’t ask your partner to choose between you.

Words you could use

“My family can be a lot. Want the who’s who before Sunday?”

“Let’s have a word that means ‘I’m ready to go’.”

“I’d love you to meet my friends. No rush. Whenever you feel ready.”

Dating as a parent

  • Keep dating separate from the kids at first. Introduce someone once it feels steady. Many parents wait months.
  • Make the first meeting short and easy, somewhere neutral, as “a friend.”
  • If you share parenting, tell the other parent before the kids meet someone new. See Separated co-parents.
  • Never ask children to keep a secret from their other parent.
  • Kids may be upset, curious, or not interested at all. All of these are normal.
  • Getting serious? Read Blended families and stepfamilies before you move in together.

Words you could use

To your kids: “I’ve got a friend called Jo I’d like you to meet. We’ll just get pizza.”

To someone new: “My kids come first on timing. It isn’t a sign I care less.”

To your co-parent: “I wanted you to hear it from me. I’m seeing someone. They won’t meet the kids for a while yet.”

When to move in together

There’s no right number of months. But many couples slide into living together without ever deciding: a toothbrush, then a drawer, then a lease runs out. Researchers call this “sliding versus deciding.” Talking it through on purpose tends to go better.

  • Why now, and what does it mean to each of you?
  • Your place, theirs, or somewhere new?
  • How will you split the bills and the jobs?
  • Whose name is on the lease, and what happens if it doesn’t work out?

Words you could use

“I’d love to live together. Can we talk about why and how before we decide?”

“Let’s each write down how we’d split the bills and the jobs, then compare.”

  • Money when you move inSplitting bills in a way you both call fair, and one owner per bill.
  • Who does what?Who does which job at home, as a plain picture with no blame.
  • House rules for usWrite down the few rules you agree, so nobody has to argue them again.

Keeping your own life

A new relationship can’t fill every gap, and it shouldn’t have to. Keep your friends, your hobbies and your routines. Notice if you’ve dropped everything else, and gently pick some of it back up.

Words you could use

“I love seeing you. Thursday is my football night, and I want to keep it.”

To a friend: “I’ve been a ghost since I met Ana. Can I buy you a coffee and hear everything?”

Ending it kindly

  • If you know, say so soon. Clear is kinder than slow fading.
  • After a date or two, a short message is fine. After a while together, a call or meeting is kinder.
  • Don’t list their faults. “It isn’t right for me” is enough.
  • If it ends for you, it’s allowed to hurt. You don’t need a full reason to move on. On my own can help.

Words you could use

After a date or two: “Thank you for the evenings. I don’t feel it’s a match for me, and I wanted to tell you rather than go quiet. I wish you well.”

After longer: “I’ve thought about this a lot. I don’t want to go on with this relationship. I care about you, and I’m sorry this hurts.”

When it ends for you: “Thanks for telling me straight. I’m sad, but I’d rather know.”

Your situation

After a divorce or a breakup

Take dating at your own pace. It’s fine to want friends first. Old hurts can echo in a new relationship, so name them: “That’s an old sore spot for me, not something you did.” See On my own after a breakup or divorce.

After losing a partner, and later in life

Dating again doesn’t erase the person you lost. Families, and grown-up children especially, may need time. Later-life dating often brings practical questions too: health, homes, wills, and whether to live together at all. Many couples choose to live apart.

“I loved Rob for forty years, and I always will. I’m also enjoying getting to know you.”

To a grown-up child: “Nobody is replacing your mum. I’d like you to meet someone who makes me happy.”

Dating when you’re neurodivergent

If you’re autistic, have ADHD or both, dating’s unwritten rules can be tiring. You can write them down. Say how you communicate and what helps, early, and ask the same of them.

“I take things literally, so please just tell me directly. I’ll do the same.”

“I forget to reply, not because I don’t care. A quick nudge is fine.”

“Loud bars wear me out. Could we try a walk or a quiet café?”

Questions people often ask

How soon should we talk about being exclusive?

When you’d like to know, ask. There’s no rule about timing. Saying where you are first makes it easier for them: “I’m not seeing anyone else. Where are you?”

Is it a bad sign if they text less than I do?

On its own, no. Texting styles differ a lot. Ask what’s normal for them, and say what’s normal for you. It’s a concern when someone uses messages to check up on you or punish you.

When should I introduce my children to someone I’m dating?

Once the relationship feels steady, and many parents wait months. Keep the first meeting short and easy. If you share parenting, tell the other parent first.

How long should we date before moving in?

There’s no right number. What matters more is deciding on purpose, not sliding into it. Talk about why, how you’ll share bills and jobs, and what happens if it doesn’t work out.

Is it okay to end things by text?

After a date or two, a short, kind message is fine. After longer, a call or a meeting is kinder. If you’re worried about how they’ll react, a message from somewhere safe is the right choice.
