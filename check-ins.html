Check-ins

A check-in is not a fight with a time on the calendar. It is a short, agreed conversation in a room that can hold it: bodies settled enough to listen, outside noise turned down, and a speaking order that puts acknowledgement before any rebuttal. The hard topic can wait for that room. It cannot survive without it.

The daily version already lives in WP-13: ninety seconds, one status each, no debate. This page is the other kind. One topic that has been waiting. Twenty to forty minutes. A stop if either body leaves the conversation.

The room has to be safe before the topic is

Challenging conversations fail less from the words than from the conditions around the words. Four things have to be true at the same time. If one is missing, postpone. Postponing with a return time is not avoidance. Walking in anyway is how a check-in becomes the next spike.

A safe time

  • Not in the spike, and not in the first minutes after one. A flooded body often needs at least twenty minutes before it can hear again, and the return should not slip past the next day (Gottman Institute).
  • Not when one person is about to leave for work, pick up a child, or fall asleep. A hard topic with no exit time trains people to rush or to hide.
  • Named in advance. "Can we use twenty minutes at 8, after dinner, for the kitchen thing?" is a check-in. "While you're walking out the door" is an ambush.
  • One topic. A second topic is a different meeting.

A safe environment

  • Private. No audience, including children. Children borrow the alarm even when the words are careful.
  • Phones face down. Notifications are a third person in the room.
  • Both people seated, or both standing. One person standing over the other is already a power difference.
  • A door that can close, and a way for either person to leave the room without leaving the relationship. Say the return before anyone uses it.
  • Light, noise and temperature low enough that a sensitive nervous system is not already spending its budget on the room. If one of you is highly sensitive, lower the volume of the house before you lower the volume of the talk. Wired Differently covers why.

External factors, regulated on purpose

Hunger, thirst, pain, a full bladder, alcohol, a deadline in ten minutes, a messy room that is the topic and also the setting: these are not character. They are load. Handle the ones you can before you open the topic. Eat if someone is sharp because they have not eaten. Move the conversation out of the pile of dishes if the dishes are what you came to discuss. A body that is already spending itself on the environment will spend the check-in defending, not listening.

Substances are a stop, not a style. If either person has been drinking or is otherwise altered, the check-in waits. Insight from an altered state rarely survives the morning, and harm from one often does.

A stable connection, before the content

Some people cannot hear a hard fact until they know the bond is still there. Silence, for them, is the start of being left. Other people cannot think until they have a little space. Both can be true in the same pair. Before the topic, offer a short signal if it is welcome: "We are okay. This is one topic, not a verdict on us. I am not leaving this." A hug or a hand is optional, never owed, and off if anyone does not want it, if you are not in private, or if anyone is afraid of the other person. The Signal Translator builds that bridge from the situation, the room, and what each person needs.

Then check states. Thirty seconds on the Nervous System Ladder, each person naming their own rung, not the other person's. If either of you is mobilized or shut down, this is not the meeting. Use WP-11, name a return, and keep it.

When a check-in is the wrong tool

Fear of losing someone is not the same as fear of someone. If you are afraid of the other person, do not schedule a closer conversation in order to feel safer. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or thehotline.org. If you are in crisis, call or text 988. This page assumes both people want a fair exchange and can leave the room.

The order, and why it is this order

The sequence below is not a new therapy. It puts three published methods in one order, then slots this program's tools into the steps they already belong to.

  • Speaker-Listener. One person speaks in short pieces. The other paraphrases before adding anything, asks whether they got it, and only then do the roles reverse. The listener is not preparing a rebuttal while the other person is still talking (The Family Institute at Northwestern, describing the technique in Markman, Stanley and Blumberg).
  • Imago dialogue. Mirror first, without analyzing or answering. Then validate that the other person's experience can make sense on its own terms. Empathy, if you can name it without inventing a feeling they did not claim. Then the process reverses (Imago Dialogue steps).
  • Gottman's six skills. Soften the start: complain without blaming, describe without judging, speak from "I." Accept influence. Repair if it spikes. Soothe yourself and, if welcome, the other person. Compromise (Gottman Institute).

Skip the mirror and the rebuttal arrives as a cross-examination. Skip the state check and you will do the mirror in a body that cannot hear. Skip the both-sides close and you will have a beautiful conversation with no owner for tomorrow.

  1. Name the container. Time, place, one topic, the stop rule, the return.
  2. Regulate. Ladder, food, phones, door, bond signal if needed.
  3. Speaker states one fact and one impact. Not a history. Not a character.
  4. Listener acknowledges, all the way through, before any rebuttal.
  5. Roles reverse. Same rules.
  6. Only then, a rebuttal if something was inaccurate. Own your part first.
  7. A solution that meets the minimum of both needs. One owner. One time. A review.
  8. Turn the miss into a repeatable positive. Not a speech. One action.
  9. Close. One specific appreciation. Next check-in, even if it is the ordinary ninety-second one.

Acknowledgement, before any answer

Acknowledgement is proof of receipt. It is not agreement. It is not therapy voice. It is not "I hear you, but." The "but" deletes the sentence in front of it. If you need a second sentence, use "and."

LISTENER
"What I heard is: the dishes were still there this morning, and you felt like the evening had been left on you. Did I get that?"

IF THEY CORRECT YOU
Mirror the correction. Do not defend the first version. "Got it. Not the dishes in general. Last night's, still in the sink at 7. Did I get that now?"

THEN, STILL BEFORE YOUR VIEW
"That can make sense. If I came down to a job I thought was done, I would be frustrated too. You don't have to be wrong for me to have a different picture. I'm not answering yet."

Stay in acknowledgement until they say you have it. Two or three short turns is normal. A speech is not a mirror. Speaker-Listener practice uses pieces of about fifteen to thirty seconds, then a check, on purpose, so neither person stockpiles a case (Family Institute).

Validation is the next inch, and it is easy to fake. "That can make sense" means their landing is a possible reading of the facts. It does not mean you intended the harm, and it does not mean their reading is the only one. Say that out loud if you need to, after the mirror has landed, not instead of it.

Empathy is optional, and it has a limit. Do not assign a feeling they did not name. Someone with alexithymia may not have a feeling-word, and being handed one can feel like a correction. "You seem hurt" is a guess. "You said it landed as being left with it. Did I get that?" is a mirror. Wired Differently has the longer version.

Acknowledgement across wirings

  • Autistic listener or speaker. Mirror the words, not a vibe you inferred. A literal repeat is respect, not coldness. Vague comfort ("it's fine, we're good") can sound like the topic was cancelled.
  • ADHD. Write the mirror in one line if either of you will lose it. A spoken "I got you" with no content is not receipt.
  • Anxious, or afraid of losing the person. Add the bond line once, and mean it. Repeating "we are okay" after every sentence starts to sound like a script.
  • Highly sensitive. Lower your volume before you lower your standards. A correct mirror at a sharp volume still lands as an attack.
  • Trauma-wired. A fast mirror can feel like mimicry or a trap. Slow down. Ask "Was that close?" instead of performing perfect recall.

If you cannot mirror without preparing your defense, you are not in the listener role yet. Say so. "I want to get this, and my answer is already crowding it. Give me a minute, then ask me to mirror again." That is regulation, not refusal.

How to rebut without erasing what you just heard

A rebuttal is allowed. Accuracy matters. What is not allowed is a rebuttal that pretends the acknowledgement did not happen, or that uses the other person's feeling as the thing to disprove.

Gottman's softened start is the shape: complain, don't blame. Describe what happened, don't judge the person. Start from "I," not "you always" (Gottman Institute). Run that sentence through the Signal Translator before the meeting, with their wiring selected, the relationship selected, and the situation set to repair. If the read is heavy static, the rebuttal is not ready.

THIS ERASES THE MIRROR
"I hear you, but you never notice what I did do, and if you weren't so sensitive this wouldn't be a thing."

THIS KEEPS IT
"I got the part about last night's dishes still there at 7, and that it landed as the evening being left on you. My part: I said I'd do them and I didn't. What I want on the record is that I did the lunch boxes and the trash before bed, and I treated those as the close. I was wrong to leave the sink unspoken. I'm not asking you to feel differently about the sink."

A proper rebuttal has four moves, in this order.

  1. Restate their fact in one line, so they can hear that it survived.
  2. Own the part that is yours, without a discount. "I said I would" is an ownership. "I would have if you had reminded me nicely" is a transfer.
  3. Add one fact they may not have, not a character contrast. One event. A time. No always, never, or "everyone else."
  4. Separate impact from intent, then stop. "I did not mean to leave it on you. I can see that it landed that way. I'm not asking you to cancel the landing."

Then be quiet. Their turn is another acknowledgement of your fact, not an instant verdict on whether your intent counts. Intent can be true and still not be the point. The point is what happens tomorrow.

If the rebuttal needs a label ("lazy," "crazy," "too much," "cold"), it is not a rebuttal. It is a verdict. Take it back to WP-09: one fact, one feeling, one ask. Paste that ask into the translator. The facts box is for the event. The phrase box is for the sentence. Do not mix them, or the listener will argue the verdict and never hear the event.

A solution that works for both

Understanding is not the close. A check-in that ends on "I feel heard" and no next action will have the same conversation next week. Gottman names compromise, and accepting influence, as skills: the other person's picture is data, not an attack to repel (Gottman Institute).

Write both needs before either solution. Use the same split the translator already asks for. One person may need the bond confirmed. The other may need a written plan, quiet, or space before they can think. A solution that meets only the faster person's need will be called peace and felt as a loss.

BOTH NEEDS, THEN ONE MOVE
"You need the kitchen not to be a surprise in the morning. I need the close of the night not to be a test I can fail without knowing the list. The move: a ten-minute close together, or a note on the fridge that says what is still open. Owner this week: me, for writing the note if we skip the ten minutes. We look at it Sunday."

A both-sides solution has five parts. If one is missing, you have a wish.

  1. Need A, in their words, mirrored.
  2. Need B, in their words, mirrored.
  3. One action that covers the minimum of each. Not the maximum of the louder one.
  4. A named owner, and a clock time, not "when you get a chance."
  5. A review. Sunday, or the next weekly close in Chapter V. If it fails, you change the system, not the verdict on the person.

Some topics will not resolve in one sitting. That is not failure if you leave with a temporary experiment and a date. Forcing a permanent answer because one person fears an open loop will produce a promise the other person cannot keep. Name the experiment as an experiment. "For seven days, not forever."

If the real fix is ownership of a recurring task, do not leave it inside the check-in. Promote it to WP-03. A spoken agreement with no owner returns as resentment, which is how the deficit audit describes a fluke becoming a pattern.

Using the time to turn a negative into a positive

This is not looking on the bright side. The pile of dishes does not become a gift because you rename it. Mood Arbitrage is explicit about that: shifting a negative interaction is not toxic positivity. It is changing the carrier so the actual point can land. The negative stays visible. What changes is what you do with it.

A miss is information about the system. Used well, the check-in spends its last minutes converting that information into one repeatable action, and into one thing that is already working. Used badly, it spends those minutes prosecuting the person who missed.

  1. Keep the cost in plain words. "The sink was full at 7, and the morning started behind." Do not sand it down into "a little miscommunication."
  2. Ask what the miss was pointing at. A need, a missing owner, a time of day when one of you is already empty, a sentence that was a hint instead of an ask. The translator's facts box is the right place to write this before you say it.
  3. Convert the need into one positive action you can repeat, not a speech about growth. The positive is behavioral. "A written close of the kitchen" is a positive. "Try to be more considerate" is a mood.
  4. Name one specific thing the other person already does that is the positive version of this. Specific, recent, observable. "You did the lunch boxes without being asked" gives the nervous system a true counterweight. "You're a great partner" does not, because it cannot be checked.
  5. Put the action where the program can see it. A repeated friction belongs in the weekly resync of WP-13, then in WP-03 or WP-04, not in a hotter speech next Tuesday.

The leverage is this: the negative paid for a clearer system. If you leave with only the sting, you wasted the cost. If you leave with only the compliment, you hid the cost and it will bill you again.

CLOSE
"The negative was a sink with no owner after 9. The positive we are keeping is a ten-minute close, or a note. Thank you for saying it as a fact instead of as a verdict on me. I'll write tonight's note before I sit down."

A card you can read from

Print this block, or keep it on the table. Reading a card is not childish. It is how you keep the order when your body wants to skip to the rebuttal.

OPEN
"Twenty minutes. One topic: ___. Either of us can pause. If we pause, we come back at ___. We are okay. This is not a verdict."

STATE
"I'm at ___ on the ladder. You?"

SPEAKER
"The fact is ___. It landed as ___. The ask is ___."

LISTENER, BEFORE ANY ANSWER
"What I heard is ___. Did I get that?" Then: "That can make sense." Stop.

SWITCH
Same two lines, the other way.

REBUTTAL, ONLY IF NEEDED
"Your fact, as I heard it: ___. My part: ___. One fact I want added: ___. I'm not asking you to cancel how it landed."

BOTH
"You need ___. I need ___. The move is ___. Owner: ___. By: ___. We look again on ___."

LEVERAGE
"The miss showed us ___. The positive we are keeping is ___. One thing you already did that counts: ___."

Practice the card once on a small topic before you use it on a loaded one. The Family Institute notes that the speaker-listener order is easier in a tense talk if you have already used it when nothing is at stake. A first performance, on the worst topic, is how good protocols get abandoned.

What this page can't do

It can't replace a clinician, a mediator, or a safety plan. It can't make two unwilling people fair. It scores no one. If a check-in becomes a cross-examination with better manners, stop and return to the mirror, or stop for the day. The Signal Translator can show how a sentence may land. It cannot hear the room, and it should not be used to win one.
