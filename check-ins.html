Check-ins: a kind weekly check-in for couples, families and housemates

Simple versionRead the full guide

A check-in is a short, agreed talk about one hard topic, at a time that works for everyone taking part: a couple, a family, housemates or a team. The topic can wait for that time, and it goes much better when it gets one.

For relationships where everyone feels safe. If anyone is afraid or controlled, see Not safe at home?

Wednesday, 10:50 p.m.

You start the conversation because it is finally quiet. It goes sideways, because it is 10:50 p.m. and you are both running on empty. The topic was fine. The timing just wasn’t.

  1. One topic, and you’re on the same side.

    Say it once before you start: “I am not against you. I do not think you meant harm. I want to sort out this one thing.” Talk about what happened, not about who the other person is. If a second topic comes up, jot it down for another day.

    Dig deeper: the ground rules

  2. Pick a good moment first.

    Choose a time you all agreed on, not the middle of a stressful moment. Private, phones down, nobody hungry or drinking. If either of you feels too wound up or too flat, that’s okay. Pick another time and say when.

    If you’re apart: a video call works. Each of you finds a private spot with other notifications off. Share one screen, or each open this page on your own, and take turns reading the steps out loud. Long-distance and apart has more.

    Dig deeper: a safe time and place

  3. Hear it back before you answer.

    One person says one fact and how it landed. The other says back what they heard and asks, “Did I get that?” Then you swap. If something needs clearing up, wait until you have both been heard.

    Dig deeper: the order, and why

  4. A pause is fine. Come back to the same topic.

    If a voice goes up or someone goes quiet, take a break and say when you’ll come back. When you do, it’s a fresh start, not round two: “I’m back. The topic is still the sink. I am not against you.”

    Dig deeper: noticing it early, and how to regroup

  5. Turn a rough moment into one small habit.

    Say how it affected you, in plain, kind words. Then agree on one small thing you can keep doing, and name one thing the other person already does well. That way a hard moment helps you build something better.

    Dig deeper: turning a negative into a positive

  6. Write your sentences down before you go in.

    List everything you want to raise, then keep just the one that would make the rest feel lighter. The Signal Translator, a tool that shows how a sentence may land, can hold your notes and save them as one page. You don’t have to bring the whole list.

When you work different hours

Nights, rotating shifts, or one of you at home all day while the other sleeps. A weekly check-in still works. It just needs a time that fits both body clocks.

  • Check in at the shift change. Two minutes as one of you comes home: how was it, and what does today need? If you are apart, a voice note does the job.
  • Never start one in protected sleep hours, or just before a night shift.
  • Keep one fixed together time a week, and hold it like a shift.
