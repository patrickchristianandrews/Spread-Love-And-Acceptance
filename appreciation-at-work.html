Appreciation at work: a menu, not a label

People like to be appreciated in different ways at work too. Gary Chapman and Paul White adapted the five love languages for the workplace. This guide keeps the useful idea (ask what appreciation looks like for each person) and leaves out the boxes.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice what makes you feel valued at work. Between you and others: ask, and offer the kind that fits.

An honest word about the evidence

The five languages of appreciation in the workplace (Chapman and White, 2012) are popular in workplaces and have been tested very little. The sturdier finding is simpler: people tend to want appreciation that is specific, timely, sincere and delivered in a way that fits them. So use the five as prompts for a question, not as a way to label coworkers.

  1. Words: specific thanks, in the way they like.

    Say what they did and what it made possible. Some people like it said out loud in front of others; many prefer it private.

    Healthy

    • “Thank you for catching that error. It saved us a day.”
    • Asking: “Do you like thanks in public, or one-to-one?”
    • Putting it in writing for the record.

    When it goes sideways

    • Generic praise (“good job”) said to everyone.
    • Public praise for someone who dislikes it.
    • Praise instead of fair pay or workload.

  2. Quality time: real attention.

    A proper conversation, a walk, a coffee, someone listening without looking at a screen.

    Healthy

    • A short, regular one-to-one.
    • Listening to the idea, not just the status.
    • Keeping the time you set.

    When it goes sideways

    • Cancelling it again and again.
    • A “catch-up” that is really a status check.
    • Expecting after-hours time.

  3. Acts of service: help that takes something off a plate.

    Lending a hand at a busy time, doing a tedious task, clearing a blocker.

    Healthy

    • Asking what would help most.
    • Taking a job fully, including the remembering.
    • Noticing a need before it becomes a request.

    When it goes sideways

    • Helping in a way that makes extra work.
    • Taking credit for the help.
    • Doing it to be seen.

  4. Small gifts: a thoughtful token.

    A favourite snack, a book, a handwritten card, a note about something you remembered.

    Healthy

    • Small, thoughtful and fits the person.
    • A gift that costs more thought than money.
    • Giving without strings.

    When it goes sideways

    • Gifts in place of fair pay or credit.
    • Expensive gifts that make others uncomfortable.
    • Giving to one person and visibly skipping another.

  5. Appropriate touch: a handshake, a high-five.

    At work this means something very small and always welcome: a handshake or a high-five if the person likes it. Many people prefer none.

    Healthy

    • Following their lead.
    • Offering a wave or a nod as an alternative.
    • Respecting “no thanks” with no fuss.

    When it goes sideways

    • Touch from someone with more power.
    • Pressing someone who stepped back.
    • Assuming everyone is comfortable.

  6. How to practice: ask for specifics.

    1. Ask. “When something goes well, what’s a way of saying thanks that you actually like?”
    2. Remember a moment. Think of a time you felt really valued at work. What happened?
    3. Be specific. Say what they did and why it mattered.
    4. Be fair. Appreciation does not replace fair pay, workload or credit.
    5. Review. Once a month: “Whom have I not thanked in a while?”

For managers: appreciation that is the same for everyone feels cheap. Appreciation that is fitted to the person, and goes with fair treatment, feels real. Never use appreciation to avoid a harder conversation about pay, workload or boundaries.
