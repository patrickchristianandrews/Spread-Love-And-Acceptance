# WP-04 — Unbilled Deficit Audit

*A Monthly Structural Reconciliation*

**Purpose:** a monthly reconciliation, done together, that looks across four weeks of Field Audit logs (WP-01) and the RACI Treaty (WP-03) to find tasks that keep becoming deficits — not to tally what either person "owes," but to find where the household's structure needs a fix rather than another one-off patch.

**Cadence: monthly.** Not weekly — this audit is specifically for patterns that are invisible at the weekly scale.

The work is separating a gap in the structure from a bad month, so that recurring failures get fixed at the level they actually live at — without anyone being assigned moral fault for them.

## Part A — Pull the month's raw data

> **Before you start, run WP-02.** This is a monthly retrospective on four weeks of dropped balls, done together — structurally the most likely session in the suite to go badly. If either person's battery is high, move the audit to another day. A month-old pattern will keep.

List every task that was logged as noticed-but-undone, or done only after being raised more than once, in **any** week this month. Pull from whichever record you actually keep:

- **Four weekly Field Audits (WP-01)** — if you're logging a full audit week each week, that's the richest source.
- **The WP-13 daily loop log** — if you're on the quarterly Field Audit rhythm instead, the daily loop's friction line is your month's record. It runs continuously, which is what this audit actually needs.

Either works. You do not need to keep a permanent Field Audit to run this — permanent logging turns a diagnostic into a chore, which is where this all started.

**Structural gap** — no clear owner exists, or the named owner isn't the person actually handling it in practice. A recurring dropped ball is almost never an attitude problem — it's an unowned task living in the common space. If something reliably produces resentment, check whether anyone's name was ever on it.
*Fix:* update the RACI Treaty (WP-03) this session. Putting a single name on it removes the ambient friction.

**Capacity issue** — has a named owner, but that person has been consistently unable to keep up with it.
*Fix:* a direct conversation using a neutral refusal or reassignment (WP-01, WP-09) — not a private resolution to just try harder.
*If it traces to a bad month* — illness, a work crunch, broken sleep — the answer is rest and time, not a treaty meeting. Depletion needs sleep and quiet, and revising the workload while someone is flat produces a treaty written by exhaustion.
*If it has recurred for months*, independent of how depleted anyone was, stop retrying. Trade the task permanently, or change the conditions around it — a different time, a different place, a different shape. One person finding something consistently harder is information about the assignment, not a verdict on them.

**Standard in question** — nobody has done it in four weeks and nothing broke. The task may reflect a standard only one person holds, rather than something the household actually requires.
*Fix:* the conversation is about whether the task is needed, and at what frequency — not about who failed to do it. If it is needed, it becomes a structural gap and gets an owner. If it isn't, remove it from the treaty and stop counting it as a deficit.

**One-off, no action** — flagged more than once but tied to an identifiable temporary cause (illness, travel, a busy work month).
*Fix:* note it and move on; don't let a temporary cause justify a permanent structural change.

A recurring fix usually means the owner was assigned but the *conditions* weren't — no trigger, no time, no realistic slot in the week. Reassigning it to the other person rarely helps. Ask what would have to be true for it to happen, not who should have made it happen.

### Then: close out the month

1. Update the RACI Treaty (WP-03) for every task classified as a structural gap.
2. Schedule the capacity conversation for anything classified as a capacity issue — this audit surfaces it, it doesn't replace the actual conversation.
3. For anything classified as a standard in question, decide it now: either it gets an owner and a frequency, or it comes off the treaty. Leaving it undecided means re-litigating it next month.

## What this audit is not for

- **Not a retroactive bill.** Hours already passed don't get tallied up and "charged" to anyone. The audit only looks backward to find the pattern, and only acts forward on the structure.
- **Not a performance review.** A task with no owner is a gap in the treaty, not a verdict on the person who happened to keep covering it.
- **Not a substitute for the weekly closing.** This audit works because the weekly rhythm (Lemonade Stand, RACI glance) keeps feeding it real data. Skipping the weekly checks makes this audit far less accurate.

> **Expect a well-run audit to lower the score first.** Ownership clarity is tasks-with-an-owner divided by total tasks listed. Adding five genuinely invisible tasks to the treaty adds five rows to the denominator — so the month you finally surface hidden work, the number can drop even though nothing got worse.
>
> That's the score behaving correctly. It was measuring a treaty that didn't know about those tasks. A lower, honest number beats a higher one computed from an incomplete list, and it should recover over the following month or two as the new rows get owners.
>
> The same rule as WP-03 applies: never raise the score by deleting rows. Removing a task because the household genuinely doesn't need it is a Part B decision, made deliberately and written down — not a way to improve the arithmetic.
