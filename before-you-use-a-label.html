Guide · Words that get used too fast

Before you use a label

Try this in 60 seconds

Think of a word you have used about someone. Write one plain sentence about what they did and what it did to you, with the word left out.

See how this page works

Words like “controlling,” “gaslighting” and “toxic” get used for ordinary friction all the time. When the word does not fit, it hurts the person who hears it and it ends the conversation. When it does fit, it deserves to be said with care. This page sets out what has to be true before a word applies, what is not enough on its own, and how to say what you mean without it.

The short version:

  • Describe what happened and what it did to you. That is almost always more accurate and more useful than a label.
  • A label needs a pattern over time, not one moment, and it needs more than a feeling that it fits.
  • Many things that look alike on the outside come from stress, overload, a different way of thinking or care shown clumsily.
  • Everyone has been on both sides of these words. Looking at both views is part of getting it right.

How to use this page

Each entry below has the same parts, so you can compare them:

  • What has to be there. The things that need to be true together, over time.
  • What is not enough on its own. Things that look like it from the outside.
  • Questions that tell them apart. Short checks you can ask yourself or each other.
  • Both views. What it can look like from each chair.
  • Saying it without the label. Words you could use instead.

This page does not diagnose anyone, and it does not decide what is happening in your relationship. It cannot be used to label a person from a story or a text message. The entries are plain-language checklists. Where research exists, each one says how strong it is, using the same labels as the research page.

A made-up example: the phone

Jordan says to Sam, “You’re so controlling. You checked my phone.” Sam hears an attack and says, “I was worried. You didn’t answer for four hours.” They argue about the word for an hour and never reach the phone.

Try again with a description. Jordan says, “When you look at my phone without asking, I feel watched. Can we agree on one check-in a day?” Sam says, “I get anxious when I don’t hear anything. A check-in would help me, too.” Nothing was labeled, and both of them were heard. Whether this was a one-time worry or a pattern is still worth asking, and the entry on monitoring has the questions.

Power, truth and character

Words that describe a whole pattern in a relationship. They fit rarely and hurt a lot when they do not.

Controlling, or something that can look like it?

Controlling behavior is one person limiting another person’s choices, freedom or contact with others, and making it cost something to say no.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • It is a pattern over weeks or months, not one tense moment.
  • Real choices are taken away: money, friends, clothes, where you go, who you talk to.
  • The rules apply to one person and not the other.
  • There is a price for not going along, such as anger, sulking, cold treatment, threats or having things taken away.
  • Watching or checking happens that the other person never agreed to.
  • The effect is that one person’s life gets smaller so as to avoid trouble.

What is not enough on its own

  • Strong preferences said out loud (“I really don’t like surprises”).
  • Asking for a plan or routine because unpredictability is hard, which is common with anxiety, autism and ADHD.
  • Asking someone to text when they get home, when both of you do it.
  • One sharp request on a bad day.
  • A limit on your own behavior (“I won’t stay in the room while we’re shouting”).
  • Rules that both people helped make and both follow.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Can I say no and nothing bad follows?
  • Do the rules go both ways?
  • Is this about my choices, or about what they need in order to feel settled?
  • Has it been going on for months, or is it new this week?
  • Did we agree to it?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Describe what happens and what it does to your days (“I stopped seeing my sister because the evening ended in a fight every time”). Notice whether you have been able to push back without a cost.

If someone says it about you: Ask what it felt like when you asked, and whether you would accept the same rule for yourself. A request that leaves room for “no” is not control. A request that comes with a penalty is worth rethinking.

Saying it without the label: “When you ask where I am every hour, I feel watched. Could we agree on one check-in a day?” And from the other side: “I ask because not knowing the plan makes me anxious. Can we settle the plan the night before?”

Evidence: A plain-language checklist built from how the word is used. It is not a validated test.

Manipulative, or just trying to influence you?

Manipulation is influencing someone through hidden, unfair or pressuring means, such as guilt, flattery, threats or deception, to get something they would not freely agree to.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • A request is hidden behind something else, or the information given turns out to be false.
  • It works by pressure or deceit, not by honest asking.
  • It serves one person at the other’s cost.
  • It happens more than once.

What is not enough on its own

  • Saying how you feel (“I’m hurt”), even if it changes what the other person does.
  • Crying during a hard talk.
  • Asking for what you want directly, even persistently.
  • Persuading with honest reasons.
  • A clumsy attempt to avoid a fight.
  • Everyday influence. Every close relationship has some.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Could I say no openly, and would that be okay?
  • Is the real reason being told to me?
  • Does it keep happening, and does it keep costing me?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Name the move and its effect, not the person: “I feel pushed when this comes with ‘after all I do for you.’”

If someone says it about you: Ask yourself whether you would be comfortable saying the real reason out loud. If you would, say it. If you would not, that is worth noticing.

Saying it without the label: “Can you just ask me? I’d rather hear what you want than have to guess.”

Evidence: A plain-language checklist. Researchers study specific tactics, such as guilt-tripping, more than the word itself.

Gaslighting, or two different memories?

Gaslighting is repeatedly denying or twisting things another person saw, heard or knows happened, until they come to doubt their own memory or judgment. The name comes from a 1938 play and the 1944 movie Gaslight.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • It happens again and again. One argument is not enough.
  • The same person denies things you saw or heard, again and again, even when something can be checked, such as a message, a photo or someone who was there, or it is always about things nobody else could check.
  • You end up doubting your own memory or judgment in general, not just about one event.
  • It usually gives the person doing it an advantage, such as control, avoiding blame or getting their way.

What is not enough on its own

  • Two people remembering the same event differently. Memory rebuilds events each time we recall them.
  • Forgetting or misremembering.
  • Disagreeing about what something meant.
  • One heated denial that was taken back later.
  • A tired or stressed person getting a detail wrong.

Questions that tell them apart

  • When there is a message, a calendar or someone else who was there, does the denial keep going even after you look?
  • Do I find myself trusting my own memory less and less?
  • Do they ever say “you’re right, I remember that now”?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Keep it concrete and kind to yourself: write down dates and plans in a shared place, and say what you remember (“I remember you saying you would pick her up”).

If someone says it about you: If someone says your version does not match theirs, check before defending. You may simply remember it differently. If it is a real memory slip, say so and fix it together.

Saying it without the label: “I remember it differently. Can we put plans in a shared note from now on, so neither of us has to rely on memory?”

Evidence: Moderate evidence for the memory point: Loftus and Palmer, 1974 (opens a new tab) showed that wording can reshape what people remember. The word “gaslighting” itself has little formal research, so treat the checklist as a plain-language guide.

Narcissistic, or just self-focused right now?

In psychology, narcissism is a set of traits, such as a strong need for admiration, a sense of being special or entitled, and difficulty taking other people’s view. Everyone has some of these to a degree.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • It is a long-lasting, inflexible pattern across many areas of life, not a bad week.
  • It causes real harm to the person and the people around them.
  • The traits show up at work, with friends and with family, not only in one conflict.
  • A formal diagnosis (narcissistic personality disorder) is rare and cannot be made from a story, a text message or a breakup.

What is not enough on its own

  • One selfish act.
  • Confidence.
  • Wanting praise for good work.
  • Being self-focused during stress, grief or exhaustion.
  • Talking about yourself a lot when you are nervous.
  • Being hurt and defensive in an argument.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is this a pattern over years and places, or about this conflict?
  • Can they ever hear how they affected someone, even later?
  • Would I describe a friend’s behavior this way, or only my partner’s in this fight?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Describe what happened and its effect (“You talked over me three times tonight”) instead of naming what kind of person they are.

If someone says it about you: If someone says this about you, ask for the specific moment. You can take the moment seriously without accepting a label for your whole self.

Saying it without the label: “When the plans changed without a word, I felt like my time didn’t matter. Can we check with each other first?”

Evidence: Moderate evidence that the traits exist on a spectrum. The clinical diagnosis is much narrower than the way the word is used online.

Toxic, or hard?

“Toxic” is a popular word for a relationship or behavior that does ongoing harm. It has no fixed meaning in psychology, which is why it is used for everything from cruelty to one bad day.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • A lasting pattern that leaves one or both people feeling worse most of the time.
  • Frequent contempt, put-downs, dishonesty, threats or control.
  • Repair that never happens, or that is promised and not kept.
  • A pattern that continues after it has been named clearly and calmly.

What is not enough on its own

  • One big fight.
  • A hard season such as a new baby, a move or a job loss.
  • Different needs.
  • A person you simply do not click with.
  • Someone being honest in a way that is hard to hear.

Questions that tell them apart

  • How often does this happen?
  • Does it get repaired afterward?
  • Is it the situation that is hard, or the way we treat each other in it?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say what the behavior is and how often (“Most of our talks this month ended in yelling”). That is clearer and easier to work with than a verdict on the whole relationship.

If someone says it about you: A word that judges your whole person will make anyone defensive. Ask “which part?” and listen for the specific thing.

Saying it without the label: “I’m not saying you’re a bad person. I’m saying our last four talks ended badly, and I want that to change.”

Evidence: The word is not a research term. The pattern of contempt and criticism is studied. See Gottman and Levenson, 1992 (moderate evidence).

Codependent, or caring a lot?

Codependency is a popular term for a pattern where someone’s sense of worth and safety depends heavily on taking care of, pleasing or controlling another person, often at a serious cost to their own needs. It began in addiction support groups in the 1970s and 1980s.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • It is a lasting pattern, not one caring act.
  • Neglecting your own needs and health to manage someone else.
  • Feeling responsible for their feelings and choices.
  • Covering for their behavior.
  • Struggling to say no.
  • Feeling empty or anxious when you are not needed.

What is not enough on its own

  • Caring deeply.
  • Relying on each other.
  • Wanting to help a partner who is ill.
  • Enjoying doing things for someone.
  • Doing more for a while during a crisis.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is there room for both of our needs?
  • Can I stop helping and still feel okay about myself?
  • Is this care freely given, or is it fear of what happens if I stop?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Talk about the specific pattern (“I notice I cancel my plans whenever you’re upset”) and one small change you could make.

If someone says it about you: Wanting to help is not a flaw. If you notice you have stopped looking after yourself, that is worth gentle attention, and it is not a verdict on your love.

Saying it without the label: “I want to be there for you, and I also need my Tuesday class. Can I call you after?”

Evidence: Weak evidence. It is not a formal diagnosis and has no agreed definition, so researchers use it carefully.

Silence and distance

Going quiet or pulling back can mean very different things.

Stonewalling, or a body that has had too much?

Stonewalling is Gottman’s word for going blank and unresponsive in an argument. A shutdown is the quiet response to overwhelm: going still, losing words, feeling numb or far away.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For “stonewalling” to describe a choice, the silence has to be aimed at the other person, to end the talk or win it.
  • It happens in many arguments, not once.
  • The person could speak if they chose and does not.
  • There is no way back offered.

What is not enough on its own

  • Going quiet when flooded. Gottman’s lab found that people who withdrew often had a racing heart, and pulling away was an attempt to calm down.
  • Sensory or emotional overload.
  • Needing time to find words.
  • Looking away or looking at a phone to cope.
  • A slower way of processing than yours.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Could they speak right now if they wanted to, or have the words gone?
  • Do they come back to the topic later?
  • Did the silence start when the voices got louder or the topic got heavier?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Leave with a sentence instead of silence: “I’m getting overloaded. I need 20 minutes and then I’ll come back.” Then come back.

If someone says it about you: If the other person goes quiet, lower the volume, ask fewer questions and name a time to pick it up: “Let’s stop here and try again after dinner.”

Saying it without the label: “I can see this is a lot. I’m not going anywhere. Let’s take a break and talk at 8.”

Evidence: Gottman and Levenson, 1992 (moderate evidence) measured arousal alongside behavior. Phung and colleagues, 2021 (early evidence) interviewed autistic children and young people about what shutdown feels like.

The silent treatment, or needing space?

The silent treatment is deliberately refusing to speak to or acknowledge someone, to punish them or pressure them into giving in.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • The silence is chosen.
  • It is aimed at the other person, to make them feel bad, anxious or shut out.
  • It goes on with no word about when it will end.
  • It usually ends only when the other person apologizes or gives in.
  • It is often a pattern, and the person acts normally with everyone else.

What is not enough on its own

  • Needing quiet to calm down, especially with a return time (“I need an hour, then let’s talk”).
  • A shutdown from overwhelm, where the person cannot find words.
  • Slow processing before speaking.
  • Being busy and missing messages.
  • One quiet evening.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Was there a way back offered?
  • Does the quiet end when the person has calmed down, or only when I give in?
  • Is it being used to win?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Talk about the effect (“When I don’t know if or when we’ll talk, I panic”) and ask for a return time.

If someone says it about you: Tell the other person you are settling and when you will be back, even if it is only “an hour.” That one sentence turns a wall into a door.

Saying it without the label: “I need some quiet to settle. It isn’t about you. I’ll come find you at 7.”

Evidence: Moderate evidence for the underlying pattern: Schrodt and colleagues, 2014 pooled 74 studies of demand and withdraw patterns and found links with poorer relationship outcomes, whichever partner was the one demanding.

Avoiding, or overwhelmed?

Avoidance is steering clear of a task or topic. Overwhelm is having more coming at you than your mind and body can handle at the moment.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For avoidance as a habit: the same topics or tasks are dodged for weeks, even when there is time and energy.
  • Other people are left to carry what is dodged.
  • There is no plan to come back to it.

What is not enough on its own

  • Needing a break from a heavy topic.
  • Putting something off when you are exhausted, sick or short on sleep.
  • Freezing in front of a task with too many steps.
  • Demand overload, when every request feels like one too many.
  • Sensory overload at the wrong moment for a serious talk.

Questions that tell them apart

  • If I picked a smaller piece or a better time, would it get easier?
  • Is it the topic that is hard, or is it everything today?
  • Do I get through other things fine and dodge only this one?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Ask for a time instead of an answer now: “Can we talk about the bills Thursday after dinner?” Name the first small step.

If someone says it about you: Say what is making it hard and offer a time: “I’m too wiped out tonight. Can we look at it Saturday morning?” Then show up on Saturday.

Saying it without the label: “I’m not dodging you. I’m full today. Can we pick a time and I’ll start by opening the folder?”

Evidence: Phung and colleagues, 2021 (early evidence) interviewed autistic children and young people about overload, inertia and shutdown. Neff and Karney, 2009 (moderate evidence) found stress outside a relationship makes everyday annoyances land harder.

Cold, or a different way of showing feeling?

Cold means withholding warmth on purpose. Flat affect means feelings that do not show on the face or in the voice the way other people expect, whatever is felt inside.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For “cold” to fit: warmth is held back as a way of punishing or pushing someone away.
  • It changes with the person. They are warm with others and cold with you.
  • It lasts, and it follows hurt or conflict.

What is not enough on its own

  • A quiet voice or a still face.
  • Not saying “I love you” in the way you do.
  • Finding it hard to name feelings. About half of autistic people in one review scored high on alexithymia, compared with about 5% of others.
  • Showing care by doing things instead of saying things.
  • Tiredness, low mood or medicine that dulls expression.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Does this person show care in other ways, such as fixing, remembering or showing up?
  • Is the face the same with everyone?
  • Would they tell me if I asked what they feel?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Ask directly: “I can’t always tell what you feel from your face. Could you tell me in words when something matters?”

If someone says it about you: You can offer a small sign that helps, such as a sentence, a text or a thumbs up: “I may not look excited, but I really am.”

Saying it without the label: “Your face is hard for me to read, and I don’t want to guess wrong. Can we use words for the big things?”

Evidence: Kinnaird and colleagues, 2019 (moderate evidence) reviewed alexithymia in autism. Barrett and colleagues, 2019 (debated) argues that faces alone are a weak guide to feelings.

Closeness, worry and limits

Wanting closeness, wanting to know someone is safe, and drawing a line can all look alike from outside.

Jealous, or insecure?

Jealousy is the painful feeling that someone you love might be drawn to someone else. Insecurity is a more general doubt about whether you are enough or the relationship is safe.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For jealousy to become a problem: it turns into actions, such as checking a partner’s phone, demanding passwords, limiting who they see or punishing normal friendships.
  • It happens repeatedly.
  • The other person changes their life to keep the peace.

What is not enough on its own

  • Feeling jealous now and then. It is human.
  • Saying “I felt left out tonight” and asking for reassurance.
  • Wanting to know about someone’s evening.
  • Anxiety after a past betrayal.
  • Insecurity that you handle by talking about it.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is the feeling being shared, or acted on at the other person’s expense?
  • Does reassurance help for a while?
  • Can they have friends without paying for it?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Share the feeling, not the accusation: “I felt a pang when you laughed with her. I think it’s my insecurity. Can I have a hug?”

If someone says it about you: Reassurance, offered freely and kindly, tends to help more than defending yourself. If it becomes monitoring, talk about that separately.

Saying it without the label: “I trust you. I’m having a hard night with my own thoughts, and I could use a bit of reassurance.”

Evidence: A plain-language checklist. Attachment research, such as Hazan and Shaver, 1987 (strong evidence as a framework), describes how some people feel more anxious about closeness than others.

Monitoring, or worry?

Monitoring is keeping track of where someone is or what they do. Worry is the feeling behind a wish to know that they are safe.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For monitoring to be a problem: it is one-sided and the other person has not agreed.
  • It is constant, and upset follows when they do not answer at once.
  • It is enforced, with a cost for not complying.
  • It extends to private things such as messages, passwords or location without consent.

What is not enough on its own

  • Asking someone to text when they get home, when it is mutual.
  • Sharing locations that both of you agreed to.
  • A parent keeping age-fit track of a child.
  • Checking in after a scary night or a long trip.
  • A caregiver tracking medicine or appointments for someone who has asked for help.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Did we both agree?
  • Does it go both ways?
  • Can I turn it off without a fight?
  • Does it ease the worry, or feed it?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say what you need and offer a limit: “It helps me to know you’ve landed. A text is enough.”

If someone says it about you: If you are the one who is checked on, say what feels like too much and offer something that would feel fair.

Saying it without the label: “I get that you worry. Could we agree on one text when I arrive, and leave it at that?”

Evidence: A plain-language checklist. No single research test separates the two. Consent and fairness are the practical markers.

Clingy, or anxious about connection?

“Clingy” is a judgment. The attachment research word for needing a lot of reassurance is anxious attachment, a style that many people have to some degree.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For pressure to be a problem: asking again and again after a clear “not now.”
  • Penalties for needing space, such as sulking, accusations or anger.
  • Being unable to manage any time apart, week after week, so the other person loses their own time.

What is not enough on its own

  • Wanting a hug when you are upset.
  • Wanting more messages on a hard day.
  • Asking once, “Can I stay with you?”
  • Feeling anxious when someone pulls away. Feeling it is human, and what you do with it is what matters.
  • Needing reassurance after a past hurt.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Can the person hear “not now” and wait, even if it is hard?
  • Does the other person feel free to take space without paying for it?
  • Is the need steady, or is it louder after a hurt?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say what you need as a request, with room for “no”: “I’d love a call tonight. If tonight doesn’t work, can we plan one for tomorrow?”

If someone says it about you: Naming a time you will be back often helps more than pulling away: “I need an hour. I’ll call you at 8.”

Saying it without the label: “I want to be near you, and I also need twenty minutes first. Can I come back with a cup of tea at 7?”

Evidence: Hazan and Shaver, 1987 (strong evidence) and Downey and Feldman, 1996 (strong evidence) link early experiences and fear of rejection to how people seek closeness.

A boundary, or an ultimatum?

A boundary is a limit about what you will do. An ultimatum is a demand about what the other person must do, with a threat attached.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For a boundary: it is about your own action (“If the shouting starts, I’ll leave the room”).
  • It stays the same whether or not the other person changes.
  • It is said calmly and followed through.
  • For an ultimatum: it is a demand on their behavior with a penalty (“Stop seeing your friends or I’m leaving”).

What is not enough on its own

  • A firm “no.”
  • A clear line after other tries (“I won’t stay if this keeps happening”), said calmly about your own action.
  • Needing time or space.
  • Being angry while setting a limit.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is this about what I will do, or what they must do?
  • Would I follow through, or is it a threat to win?
  • Am I saying it to protect something, or to punish?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say the limit and the action in one breath: “When we shout, I’ll step out and come back in 30 minutes.”

If someone says it about you: A limit is not a punishment. Ask what it protects and whether there is a way to meet it.

Saying it without the label: “I can’t talk while we’re both shouting. I’ll take a break and come back at 8.”

Evidence: A plain-language distinction. It is widely used in counseling and has not been tested as a single measure.

Everyday friction

The small words people use in the middle of an argument.

Criticism, or feedback?

In relationship research, criticism means attacking the person instead of the problem. Feedback or a complaint describes one thing that happened and what you need.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • It is about who someone is (“You’re so lazy”), not what happened.
  • It uses words like “always,” “never” and “you’re the kind of person who.”
  • It comes with blame and no request.

What is not enough on its own

  • A complaint about a specific event (“The dishes were left again”).
  • A calm, clear request.
  • Hard feedback that stings. Feeling hurt does not make something an attack.
  • Frustration said once, in plain words.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Does it describe what happened, or judge who someone is?
  • Is there a request in it?
  • Would I say it the same way to a friend?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Turn it into a complaint with a request: “The dishes were left again, and I’m worn out. Can we split them tonight?”

If someone says it about you: Try hearing the request inside it. Ask, “What would help?” before defending.

Saying it without the label: “I felt overlooked when the plans changed without me. Next time, can you text me first?”

Evidence: Gottman and Levenson, 1992 (moderate evidence) found that conflict with more negative than positive moments went with relationships getting worse.

Nagging, or reminding?

Nagging usually means repeated requests that feel critical and wear both people down. A reminder helps someone remember something they agreed to.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • The same request is repeated with blame (“I’ve told you a hundred times”).
  • There is no shared system, so one person acts as the manager.
  • The reminder carries criticism in its tone.

What is not enough on its own

  • A short, neutral reminder.
  • Asking again because the task has no clear owner.
  • A reminder that the other person asked for.
  • Repeating something because it is urgent.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Did we agree who owns this job, and how they want to be reminded?
  • Is the reminder the same each time, or does it grow in tone?
  • Is one person carrying the memory for both?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Agree who owns the job and how reminders should work (a list, a shared calendar, one text), so you stop being the manager.

If someone says it about you: If you are being “nagged,” ask whether the task has a real owner. The repeated asking often points to a gap in the system, not a flaw in either person.

Saying it without the label: “Let’s agree you own the recycling and I’ll never mention it. If it slips, we can talk about the system.”

Evidence: Schrodt and colleagues, 2014 (strong evidence) found that the demand and withdraw pattern, where one pushes and the other pulls back, goes with poorer relationship outcomes.

Blame, or accountability?

Blame looks for who to punish. Accountability looks at what happened, who owns the fix and what will change.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For blame: the aim is to make someone feel bad or pay.
  • It is about the person (“you’re careless”), not the event.
  • Nothing changes afterward, except the mood.

What is not enough on its own

  • Naming what happened, plainly.
  • Asking someone to own their part.
  • Saying “I was wrong” about your own piece.
  • Feeling guilt, which is about an action, and which can push repair.

Questions that tell them apart

  • After this talk, will anything be different?
  • Is it about this event, or about the whole person?
  • Are we asking what happened, or who is guilty?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Use fact, feeling and ask: “The bill was late (fact). I felt stressed (feeling). Can we set a reminder (ask)?”

If someone says it about you: Owning your part without piling on shame usually leads to more repair. Shame (“I am bad”) tends to lead to hiding, and guilt (“I did a bad thing”) tends to lead to making amends.

Saying it without the label: “I’m not looking to blame anyone. I want to understand what happened so it doesn’t happen again.”

Evidence: Tangney and Dearing, 2002 (moderate evidence) compares shame and guilt. Dekker, 2007 (moderate evidence) describes how workplaces separate accountability from blame.

Passive-aggressive, or indirect?

Passive-aggressive describes expressing anger or resistance indirectly: sarcasm, “forgetting” on purpose, doing a task badly to make a point, or saying “fine” while meaning the opposite.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • The indirectness is a way of hitting back while keeping deniability.
  • It repeats.
  • When asked directly, the person denies any feeling or resentment.

What is not enough on its own

  • Indirect communication in general. Many people and cultures soften requests, avoid conflict or hint.
  • A short reply that is just short.
  • A forgotten task that was a real memory slip.
  • Saying “fine” because the topic is too big to start tonight.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is the indirectness a habit across topics, or only when the topic feels risky?
  • Does a gentle direct question get a straight answer?
  • Would the person say it plainly if it felt safe?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Ask kindly and directly: “When you said ‘fine,’ I wasn’t sure you meant it. Is something bothering you?”

If someone says it about you: If you tend to hint, try one direct sentence: “I’m upset about Saturday and I’d like to talk about it tomorrow.”

Saying it without the label: “I might be reading this wrong. Could you tell me what you would like me to do?”

Evidence: Pinker and colleagues, 2008 (theory) explains why people use indirect speech. Gunraj and colleagues, 2016 (early evidence) found a period at the end of a short text was rated as less sincere, which shows how easily a plain reply is read as cold.

Rude, or direct?

Rude means disrespect that is meant, or that ignores someone on purpose. Direct means saying things plainly, without padding.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For “rude” to fit: the person knows the effect and carries on, or is meaning to insult.
  • It happens with different people, not only in one style clash.
  • There is contempt, mocking or put-downs.

What is not enough on its own

  • Blunt honesty without padding.
  • Taking words literally and answering exactly what was asked.
  • Not saying “please” or small talk in a culture where they are not used that way.
  • A short answer when someone is busy or tired.
  • A different norm for eye contact or interrupting.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Did they mean to hurt?
  • Is this their style with everyone?
  • Would a plain question clear it up?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say what landed and ask: “That sounded sharp to me. Did you mean it that way?”

If someone says it about you: If your style is direct, adding a short opener helps people listen: “I’m going to be blunt, and I’m on your side.”

Saying it without the label: “I know you didn’t mean anything by it. Can you say that again with a bit of warmth? That helps me take it in.”

Evidence: Matsumoto and colleagues, 2008 (moderate evidence) found that how much people hide their feelings differs across 23 countries, so what reads as blunt or cold in one place is ordinary in another.

Lying, or misremembering?

Lying is saying something you know is not true, to mislead. Misremembering is honestly holding a version that is wrong or incomplete.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For lying: the person knows the truth and says something else.
  • It is meant to mislead or avoid a consequence.
  • It repeats, or the story changes when checked.

What is not enough on its own

  • Remembering an event differently. Memory rebuilds events each time they are recalled.
  • Forgetting a detail.
  • Mixing up two similar days.
  • Leaving something out because it was not on your mind.
  • Telling a kind white lie. That is still a lie, and it deserves its own conversation, but it is a different thing from deceiving someone to gain something.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Is there a written record that settles it?
  • Do they accept it when shown?
  • Did something change in their favor if I believe them?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Say what you remember without accusing: “I remember it as Tuesday. Can we check the calendar?”

If someone says it about you: If you are not sure, say so: “I thought it was Tuesday, but I could be wrong.” Honest doubt builds trust.

Saying it without the label: “I remember this differently. I’m not saying you’re lying. Can we look at the text together?”

Evidence: Loftus and Palmer, 1974 (opens a new tab) (moderate evidence) showed that the wording of a question changed what people remembered. One later replication using a different wording did not hold, so treat it as a classic demonstration and not a rule.

Wiring and energy

Differences in how a mind works are often read as a character problem.

Lazy, or trouble with executive function?

“Lazy” says someone could and will not. Executive function is the set of mind skills for starting, planning, switching and keeping going, and it works differently in different people.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For “unwilling”: the person can do the task easily, has energy and a clear plan, and simply chooses not to, again and again.
  • There is no stress, illness, low mood or overload behind it.
  • They take part in other tasks without trouble.

What is not enough on its own

  • Wanting to do something and not being able to start.
  • Getting stuck when a task has many steps.
  • Forgetting tasks that are not in sight.
  • Doing well under a deadline and badly without one.
  • Exhaustion, low mood, pain or too much on the plate.
  • ADHD, autism, depression or sleep loss affecting how a person starts and finishes tasks.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Does the person want to do it and get stuck anyway?
  • Would a smaller first step, a timer, a list or a partner sitting nearby help?
  • Are they doing a lot that I don’t see?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Ask for the first step and not the whole job: “Can you put the laundry in? I’ll fold it with you.” Make the task visible and shared.

If someone says it about you: If you want to do it and cannot start, say so: “I want to, and I can’t start. Can you sit with me for ten minutes?”

Saying it without the label: “I don’t think you’re lazy. I think the way we’ve set this up isn’t working for you or for me. Can we try a different setup?”

Evidence: Barkley, 1997 (moderate evidence) links ADHD to differences in executive function. Demetriou and colleagues, 2018 (moderate evidence) reviewed executive function in autism.

Obsessive, or a special interest?

A special interest is a deep, enjoyable focus on a subject, common in autistic people and many others. Obsessive can mean distressing thoughts or behaviors a person cannot stop, and that is a different thing.

What has to be there

These need to be true together, and over time:

  • For “obsessive” in the clinical sense: unwanted thoughts or urges that cause distress and take a lot of time, often with rituals meant to relieve them.
  • The person wants to stop and cannot.
  • It gets in the way of life and relationships.

What is not enough on its own

  • Loving a subject and talking about it a lot.
  • Collecting, researching or practicing for hours.
  • Needing routine to feel steady.
  • Strong enthusiasm that looks odd to others.
  • Focus that helps a person recharge and feel well.

Questions that tell them apart

  • Does it bring joy or calm, or distress?
  • Can the person put it down when something important comes up?
  • Does it leave room for the people they love?

Both views

If it seems to describe what you live with: Ask for a share of attention, and be curious: “Tell me the best part. And then can we plan our evening?”

If someone says it about you: If your interest crowds out others, agree on a time for it: “I’ll talk about trains for ten minutes, and then I want to hear about your day.”

Saying it without the label: “I love how much you care about this. Can we agree on a time to hear about it, and a time to be together?”

Evidence: Grove and colleagues, 2018 (moderate evidence) found that special interests were linked to better wellbeing in autistic adults.

The same method for any word

If a word you are about to use is not on this page, the same three steps work.

  1. Say what happened. Only what a camera would have recorded: “The plans changed twice on Friday.”
  2. Say what it did to you. “I felt like my time didn’t count.”
  3. Make a request. “Could you text me before you change the plan?”

That leaves the other person room to answer, and it leaves you with what you actually want, which is for something to change.

If you are thinking of using one of the stronger words, ask yourself whether you could prove it to a fair stranger who knew both of you. If you could not, describe instead.
