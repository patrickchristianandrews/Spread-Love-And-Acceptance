The three rungs

How your body feels changes how you sound and how you hear. The book calls these three everyday states the Nervous System Ladder:

  • Calm and connected. Breathing is easy. You can hear something hard without getting defensive. Calm doesn’t mean happy: you can be calm and sad, or calm and firm.
  • Revved up. You feel tense and fast. Your heart is quicker and thoughts move fast. Small things feel urgent or personal. Pace speeds up, and tone gets sharper.
  • Running on empty. You feel tired and flat, and words are harder to find. Replies get slower and shorter, which the other person may read as not caring.

The labels are loosely borrowed from polyvagal theory, whose main claims scientists still debate. Here they are only everyday words, not a medical model. The point is simple: when you are revved up or running on empty, you are more likely to send and hear signals off-key. If you check first, you can choose to wait, to warn (“I’m low, so I’ll be brief”), or to go ahead.

Want the fuller daily version, with sleep, pressure and who you’ll be around? Switch to the full forecast (Today’s Weather), or tap “Add the rest” under your result and your rung comes with you. There, calm and connected is a clear sky, revved up is gusty, and running on empty is fogged in.

Words you could use

Say your state out loud. It changes how your words land, even if the other person never uses the ladder.

“I’m revved up right now. Give me ten minutes and I’ll be easier to talk to.”

“I’m running low right now. It’s not about you. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

“I’m in a decent place to talk. Is now good for you?”

One common mix-up: “You’re revved up” as a put-down. The ladder is for checking yourself. Don’t use it to label someone else. And if you feel like you’re running on empty most days for weeks, that’s a sign to rest and get support, not to stop talking forever.
