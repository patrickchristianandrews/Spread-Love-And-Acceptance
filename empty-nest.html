The house is quiet now

For a long time, most of your talk was about the children. Who picks them up. What they need. How they are doing. When they leave, that talk stops. Some couples find they don’t know what to talk about. Some feel like two housemates, not a couple.

This is very common. It does not mean your marriage is in trouble. It means life changed, and the two of you need some new habits.

Often nothing is wrong. You are kind to each other. You are not fighting. That is a good thing, and it is worth protecting. But it can slowly become weaker if nobody looks after it. The page on complacency explains this in more detail.

Small daily habits

You don’t need a big talk or a big change. Small things, done often, work better. Pick one or two.

  • A 90-second check-in. Each evening, each person says how full they feel today, one thing they liked, and one thing that would help tomorrow. No arguing and no fixing. The 90-second daily check-in gives you a simple form to fill in. You can share it between two phones.
  • One meal together, without phones. It does not need to be special. Just the two of you at the table.
  • One thank-you a day. Thank your partner for one small thing. Say exactly what it was.
  • One question a day. Ask something you don’t already know the answer to. Then listen to the whole answer.
  • Say yes more often. When your partner says “Look at this” or “Come for a walk”, try to say yes. These small moments matter. Turning toward explains why.

Words you could use

“The house feels very quiet. I would like us to spend more time together. Can we eat dinner together, without phones?”

“Can we try a short check-in every evening this week? Only 90 seconds.”

“Thank you for making my lunch today. It made my day easier.”

Care you show by doing counts

People show love in different ways. Some people show it with words. Many people show it with food, practical help, time or small gifts. These ways are often shaped by family and culture.

If your partner shows care by doing things, that care is real. Cooking a meal, fixing the car or driving them somewhere can say “I love you” as clearly as words.

Problems start when one person is waiting for words, and the other is showing care with actions. Each one may feel they are giving a lot, and not getting much back. Neither of you is wrong.

  • Notice the care your partner gives by doing. Thank them for it, in words.
  • Tell your partner, once, what your actions mean. “When I cook your favourite food, it means I care about you.”
  • Ask your partner what makes them feel cared for. Then do one of those things this week.

Words you could use

“I don’t always say it, but when I fix things in the house, I am doing it for you.”

“I noticed you cleaned the kitchen. Thank you. I see it.”

“What is one small thing that makes you feel cared for?”

If one of you feels “everything is fine”

Often one partner feels distance and the other does not. One says “We are not close.” The other thinks “But everything is fine. We don’t fight.”

Both can be true. “We are not close” usually does not mean “You did something wrong.” It often means “I miss you, and I want more time with you.”

If you are the one who feels everything is fine, here is one practical thing to do. Ask your partner this question, and then do what they say:

“What is one small thing we could do together this week?”

Keep it small: a walk, a meal, a short drive, a film. Put it in the calendar. Then do it. You don’t need to talk about feelings for a long time. Doing it together already helps.

If you are the one who feels far away, try to ask for one clear, small thing. Hints can be easy to miss. “Can we walk together on Sunday morning?” is easier to say yes to than “We never spend time together.”

You may talk in different ways

Some people say things directly. Others give hints and hope the other person understands. Neither way is wrong, but the two of you can misunderstand each other. The free Wave Code quiz takes about two minutes. Each of you can do it on your own phone, then share your result to see the two side by side, with one simple tip for each difference.

Words you could use

“Please tell me in plain words what you would like. I don’t always see hints.”

“When I say ‘I’m fine’, sometimes I am not. Please ask me one more time.”

Bonding tips: new things for the two of you

For many years, the children gave you things to do together. Now you can choose your own. New things, done together, help couples feel close again.

  • Try one new thing together this month. A class, a new walk, a recipe from a country you have never visited.
  • Go back to something from before the children. A place you went when you first met. A song you both liked. Music you used to dance to.
  • Start a small project together. A garden bed, painting a room, planning a trip. Working side by side makes talking easier.
  • Talk about more than the children. It is fine to talk about them. Also talk about you: your plans, your worries, your hopes.
  • Plan one thing to look forward to. A day out, a visit, a holiday. Put it in the calendar.

Words you could use

“Do you remember the walk we did before the children? Shall we go back there on Saturday?”

Questions to ask each other

People keep changing, even after many years together. These questions help you get to know each other again. Ask one at a time. Listen to the answer. You don’t need to fix anything.

  1. What is on your mind this week that I don’t know about?
  2. What do you miss most about when the children were at home? What do you not miss?
  3. What is something you would like to try, now that we have more time?
  4. Where would you like to go together this year?
  5. What is one thing I do that makes you feel cared for?
