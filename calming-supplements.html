Start with these

A supplement can’t fix what is causing the stress: too much on your plate, a hard relationship, money, grief, or poor sleep. At best, some may take the edge off for a while. The things with the strongest evidence cost nothing. Try these first, in any order:

The short version

  • The things with the best evidence for stress and worry are free: sleep, movement, slow breathing, less caffeine and alcohol, and talking to someone.
  • A few supplements have some research behind them, but the effects are usually small and the studies small.
  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe. Kava, ashwagandha, skullcap and black cohosh have liver warnings, and St John’s wort, 5-HTP and kanna clash with antidepressants.
  • Use Find what fits, or the search and filters in the full list, to see options for a goal (like sleep or panic) or for a group (like ADHD or autistic).

And when you need calm right now: breathe out slowly, longer than you breathe in. Take a one-minute Breathe break.

If you still want to try a supplement: check the safety notes, change one thing at a time, and jot down how you feel over a few weeks so you can tell whether it helps.

What the evidence labels mean
  • Good evidence many good studies agree.
  • Some evidence several decent studies, with limits.
  • Mixed evidence studies disagree, or it depends how it’s used.
  • Early evidence a few small studies: a maybe.
  • Weak evidence little or poor-quality research.
  • No good evidence tested, and no benefit found.

        Everyday habit

        Good evidence

        Movement

        The strongest evidence of anything here for low mood, anxiety and stress.

        Studied in
        Hundreds of trials on depression, anxiety and distress
        Forms
        Walking, dancing, cycling, swimming, gardening, strength training
          Details

          Watch out Start gently if you have a heart condition.

          What the research shows

          Hundreds of trials show physical activity eases depression, anxiety and distress. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training did especially well.

          Which kinds of exercise helped most: Noetel and colleagues, 2024 (opens a new tab). For PMS, the NHS lists regular exercise and sleep among things that can help, though the trials are of poor quality (NHS: PMS (opens a new tab)).

          What it is

          Any physical activity: walking, dancing, cycling, swimming, gardening or strength training.

          Used for

          Low mood, anxiety and stress.

          Interactions and cautions

          Side effects
          Aches. Start gently if you have been inactive.
          Take care if
          you have a heart or other health condition, or a condition where activity makes you worse later: go gently and pace yourself.

          Good to know

          How different neurotypes may respond

          ADHD: A review of eight trials in children with ADHD found exercise, especially aerobic exercise, helped with attention and hyperactivity (Cerrillo-Urbina and colleagues, 2015 (opens a new tab)).

          Autistic: Predictable, familiar movement often works best: the same walk, swim or stretch at the same time. Sensory-friendly places (quiet times at the pool, headphones) count.

          Anxiety or OCD: A racing heart during exercise can feel like panic at first. Starting gently helps the body learn the feeling is safe.

          Chronic illness or low energy: With ME/CFS or similar conditions, “push through” plans can make you worse. UK guidance says not to use fixed, step-by-step increases. Stay within your energy limits (NICE ME/CFS guideline (NG206) (opens a new tab)).

          Source: Singh and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab)

            B vitamins (B-complex, B12, folate)

            A small benefit for stress in pooled trials, but not for depression or anxiety.

            Studied in
            16 trials of B-complex supplements taken for at least four weeks
            Forms
            Meat, eggs, dairy, beans, leafy greens; B-complex supplements
              Details

              Watch out Stick to normal amounts.

              What the research shows

              Across 16 trials of B-complex supplements taken for at least four weeks, people reported a little less stress. There was no clear effect on depression or anxiety. The benefit may be bigger in people who eat poorly or already have low mood.

              What it is

              A group of vitamins (including B6, B12 and folate) found in meat, eggs, dairy, beans and leafy greens.

              Used for

              Stress, tiredness and mood.

              Side effects
              Usually well tolerated in the trials.
              Pregnancy and breastfeeding
              Folic acid is often advised around early pregnancy.
              Medicines
              Interactions are not well studied, and they matter most with antidepressants (such as SSRIs), blood thinners, thyroid medicine and anything that makes you sleepy.

              No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. Liver or kidney disease can still change how the body handles any supplement.
              Children and teens
              Use a children’s product only.

              Good to know

              How different neurotypes may respond

              Chronic illness or low energy: Long-lasting tiredness has many causes. Low B12 or folate can be found with a blood test, which is better than guessing.

                Device or environment

                Some evidence

                Heart rate variability biofeedback

                Slow breathing guided by a heart-rate sensor. Trials found less stress and anxiety.

                Studied in
                24 studies with 484 people
                Forms
                A sensor on a finger, ear or chest strap, shown on a screen
                  Details

                  Watch out Apps vary a lot in quality. You don’t need a device to breathe slowly.

                  What the research shows

                  Across 24 studies (484 people), training with heart-rate biofeedback reduced stress and anxiety compared with control groups. The authors called it promising and asked for more well-controlled trials.

                  What it is

                  A sensor (on a finger, ear or chest strap) shows your heart rhythm on a screen, so you can learn the breathing pace that makes it smoothest, usually about six breaths a minute.

                  Used for

                  Stress and anxiety.

                  Interactions and cautions

                  Side effects
                  Light-headedness.
                  Take care if
                  you have a heart rhythm condition: the readings can be unreliable, and this is not a treatment for it.

                  Good to know

                  How different neurotypes may respond

                  ADHD: The live feedback can make slow breathing more engaging.

                  Autistic: A screen showing your heart rhythm can make body signals easier to “see” when they are hard to feel.

                  Food or nutrient

                  Some evidence

                  Iron (only if you are low)

                  Helps restless legs a little, even with normal iron. Otherwise only useful if a blood test shows you are low.

                  Studied in
                  10 trials in restless legs syndrome (Cochrane review)
                  Forms
                  Red meat, beans, lentils, fortified cereals; supplements
                  • Medicine interactions
                  • Condition caution
                  Details

                  Watch out Iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6. Keep supplements locked away.

                  What the research shows

                  A Cochrane review of 10 trials found iron eased restless legs syndrome a little more than a dummy pill, sometimes even when blood iron was normal. For tiredness or focus, iron helps people who are actually low.

                  What it is

                  A mineral in red meat, beans, lentils and fortified cereals.

                  Used for

                  Restless legs at night, tiredness, and focus.

                  Interactions and cautions

                  Side effects
                  Constipation, stomach upset and nausea. The adult upper limit is 45 mg a day.
                  Pregnancy and breastfeeding
                  Needs rise in pregnancy.
                  Medicines
                  Iron reduces how well some medicines are absorbed, such as levothyroxine (thyroid medicine) and some antibiotics. The timing of doses matters.
                  Liver and kidneys
                  Don’t take iron if you have a condition that causes iron overload (such as hemochromatosis).
                  Children and teens
                  Not for general use in children. Keep adult iron tablets out of reach.

                  Good to know

                  How different neurotypes may respond

                  ADHD: On average, children with ADHD have lower ferritin (stored iron) than other children, though their blood iron is similar (Tseng and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)). That is a link, not proof that iron helps. A blood test shows whether iron is actually low.

                  Chronic illness or low energy: Iron deficiency is a common, fixable cause of tiredness. A blood test is a better guide than taking iron “just in case.”

                  Listed under Sleep · Burnout and fatigue · Focus and attention

                  Sources: Trotti and Becker, 2019 (Cochrane) (opens a new tab) · NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, iron (opens a new tab)

                  Herb

                  Some evidence

                  Lavender capsules (Silexan) and smelling it

                  Lavender oil capsules have the better research for anxiety. Smelling lavender is pleasant but unproven.

                  Studied in
                  1,213 adults with anxiety (capsules, 10 weeks); all five trials paid for by the maker
                  Forms
                  Oil capsules made to be swallowed (Silexan), or smelling the oil
                    Details

                    Watch out Only swallow products made to be swallowed. Burping and stomach upset can happen.

                    What the research shows

                    Five trials of the capsules (1,213 adults, 10 weeks) found less anxiety than a dummy pill. All five were paid for by the maker, and results can’t be assumed for other lavender products. For smelling lavender, it is unclear whether it helps.

                    An earlier review of five trials found the higher dose looked best, but the differences were not statistically clear (Yap and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)).

                    What it is

                    A purple-flowered herb. There are two very different ways to use it: lavender oil capsules made to be swallowed (most research used one product, Silexan), and smelling the oil (aromatherapy).

                    Used for

                    Anxiety, stress and sleep.

                    Interactions and cautions

                    Side effects
                    Capsules: stomach upset, headache, nausea, burping. Smelling it: sometimes headache or coughing. On skin: allergic rashes in some people.
                    Pregnancy and breastfeeding
                    Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so its safety there is unknown.
                    Medicines
                    Not tested alongside antidepressants or sleeping pills.
                    Liver and kidneys
                    No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. Liver or kidney disease can still change how the body handles any supplement.
                    Children and teens
                    Not for children. Never let children swallow essential oils.

                    Good to know

                    How different neurotypes may respond

                    Anxiety or OCD: The capsule trials included people with generalized anxiety and with “subthreshold” anxiety (worry that doesn’t meet a diagnosis). They did not test it alongside antidepressants, so mixing them is untested.

                    Sensory sensitivity: Smell is personal. If strong scents bother you, the capsules (or nothing) may suit you better than oils in the room.

                    Listed under Anxiety and worry · Stress · Sleep · Restlessness and racing thoughts

                    Sources: Dold and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab) · NCCIH lavender fact sheet (opens a new tab)

                      Everyday habit

                      Some evidence

                      Less caffeine

                      In people with panic disorder, large amounts of caffeine set off panic attacks in about half.

                      Studied in
                      People with panic disorder, in lab studies
                      Forms
                      Cutting down coffee, tea, energy drinks, cola and pre-workout products
                      • Medicine interactions
                      Details

                      Watch out Cut down gradually: stopping suddenly can cause headaches for a few days.

                      What the research shows

                      In lab studies, about half of people with panic disorder had a panic attack after a large dose of caffeine (400 mg or more, about four cups of coffee), compared with very few people without it. Everyday amounts haven’t been studied as well.

                      The NHS self-help advice for anxiety includes cutting down on caffeine (NHS anxiety self-help (opens a new tab)).

                      What it is

                      Cutting down on coffee, tea, energy drinks, cola and pre-workout products, especially after midday.

                      Used for

                      Anxiety, panic and sleep.

                      Interactions and cautions

                      Side effects
                      Withdrawal headaches and tiredness for a few days.
                      Take care if
                      you rely on caffeine to stay safe at work or driving: cut down slowly.

                      Good to know

                      How different neurotypes may respond

                      ADHD: Caffeine and ADHD stimulant medicines both raise heart rate and blood pressure, so together they can add up: jitters, a racing heart, poor sleep (Drugs.com interaction checker (opens a new tab)).

                      Anxiety or OCD: People with panic disorder are much more sensitive to caffeine.

                      Some evidence

                      Melatonin

                      Helps with body-clock problems like jet lag, not an anxiety treatment.

                      Studied in
                      Jet lag and shift-work sleep problems
                      Forms
                      Supplement in the US; prescription-only in the UK
                      • Medicine interactions
                      Details

                      Watch out Can make you drowsy. Not well studied in children. Prescription-only in the UK.

                      What the research shows

                      May help jet lag and shift-work sleep problems. Guidelines advise against it for long-term insomnia.

                      Don’t drive soon after taking it. The rules differ by country: in the UK it is a prescription medicine, mainly for adults 55 and over. In the US it is sold as a supplement. UK guidance: NHS: who can take melatonin (opens a new tab).

                      What it is

                      A hormone the body makes at night. Sold as a supplement in the US. Prescription-only in the UK.

                      Used for

                      Sleep timing: jet lag and shift work.

                      Interactions and cautions

                      Side effects
                      Uncommon: drowsiness, headache, dizziness, nausea. Long-term safety is less studied. In the US, the amount in a product can differ a lot from the label.
                      Pregnancy and breastfeeding
                      Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so its safety there is unknown.
                      Medicines
                      Take extra care if you take a blood thinner or epilepsy medicine, or anything else that makes you sleepy.
                      Liver and kidneys
                      Take extra care if you have liver or kidney problems or an autoimmune condition.
                      Children and teens
                      Good sleep habits come first. In the UK it is a prescription medicine.

                      Good to know

                      How different neurotypes may respond

                      ADHD: Limited evidence suggests it may help sleep problems in children with ADHD. Long-term safety is uncertain (NCCIH: ADHD and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)). Stimulant medicines can also affect sleep, so the timing of doses matters.

Don’t swap in shop-bought products.

                        Practice or body-based

                        Some evidence

                        Mindfulness and meditation

                        Small improvements in anxiety, low mood and pain. MBCT helps prevent depression coming back.

                        Studied in
                        47 trials; plus a course tested to prevent depression coming back
                        Forms
                        Short apps to eight-week courses
                          Details

                          Watch out Some people feel worse at first. A gentle start helps.

                          What the research shows

                          Across 47 trials, mindfulness programs gave small improvements in anxiety, low mood and pain. They were not better than other active choices, such as exercise. For people who have had depression several times, an eight-week course called MBCT cut the risk of it coming back by about a third.

                          What it is

                          Practices that train attention on the present moment, from short apps to eight-week courses.

                                                                                          Before you buy anything

                                                                                          Staying safe

                                                                                          None of this is meant to worry you. These are the plain facts worth knowing before you buy anything.

                                                                                          • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe.

                                                                                            Plants contain active chemicals. That is why some can help, and why some can harm.

                                                                                          • Supplements aren’t checked like medicines before they are sold.

                                                                                            In the US, the FDA does not approve supplements before they reach shops. Makers are responsible for safety. In the UK, herbal medicines with a THR mark are registered for quality and safety, based on traditional use rather than trials.

                                                                                          • Quality varies.

                                                                                            Products can contain more, less or something other than the label says. Look for an independent testing mark such as USP Verified or NSF. These check what is in the bottle, not whether it works.

                                                                                          • Medicines

                                                                                            The biggest risks are with antidepressants (serotonin syndrome with St John’s wort, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan or kanna), blood thinners (bleeding with ginkgo and high-dose fish oil, and changes with St John’s wort and CBD), thyroid medicine (ashwagandha, and calcium and iron block absorption), birth control pills (St John’s wort) and anything sedating.

                                                                                          • Never mix sedating herbs

                                                                                            (such as valerian, kava, passionflower or hops) with alcohol, sleeping pills, opioid painkillers or benzodiazepines. Together they can slow breathing and make you dangerously drowsy.

                                                                                          • Liver or kidney problems

                                                                                            Extra caution applies. Kava, ashwagandha, skullcap, black cohosh, CBD and high-absorption curcumin have liver warnings, and the kidneys clear minerals such as magnesium.

                                                                                          • Pregnancy and breastfeeding

                                                                                            Most herbs have not been well tested in pregnancy, so their safety there is unknown.

                                                                                          • Children and teens

                                                                                            Calming and sleep supplements, including melatonin, are not well studied in children. Keep iron tablets locked away: iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children.

                                                                                          • Before surgery

                                                                                            Some herbs, such as passionflower and ginkgo, matter for anesthesia and bleeding.

                                                                                          • Don’t stop a prescribed medicine

                                                                                            to switch to a herb. Some medicines need to be reduced slowly.

                                                                                          • Driving

                                                                                            If something makes you sleepy, don’t drive or use machines until you know how it affects you.

                                                                                          Notes by group

                                                                                          How different neurotypes may respond

                                                                                          Brains and bodies differ. Something that suits one person can be useless or unpleasant for another, and a few things have research for one group but not others. These notes are careful and general: they are not instructions. Open a group to read its note, then use the link under it to see only the options with notes for that group.

                                                                                          ADHD

                                                                                          Stimulant medicines and caffeine both raise heart rate and blood pressure, so coffee, energy drinks and “focus” blends can add up (Drugs.com interaction checker (opens a new tab)). Some ADHD medicines lower appetite and affect sleep, so meal and bedtime timing matter (NHS: side effects of methylphenidate (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          Exercise has good support for attention and hyperactivity in children (Cerrillo-Urbina and colleagues, 2015 (opens a new tab)). Omega-3 has little evidence for ADHD symptoms (Gillies and colleagues, 2023 (Cochrane) (opens a new tab)), and ginkgo and St John’s wort have not been shown to help (NCCIH: ADHD and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)). Children with ADHD have lower stored iron on average, which is a reason to test first, not to give iron blindly (Tseng and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          Autistic

                                                                                          Taste, smell, texture and capsule size can make supplements hard to take. Deep pressure and weighted blankets are liked by many, but in a careful trial they didn’t lengthen autistic children’s sleep (Gringras and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab)). A prescription prolonged-release melatonin has helped autistic children fall asleep sooner (Gringras and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          About half of autistic people find it hard to name their feelings (alexithymia), compared with about 1 in 20 others (Kinnaird and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)). So “do you feel calmer?” can be a hard question. Track simple things instead: time to fall asleep, how often meltdowns or shutdowns happen, how long it takes to settle, or a 1 to 5 rating at the same time each day. Be wary of anything sold as an autism “cure”: some, such as chelation, can be dangerous (NCCIH: autism and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          Show the options with notes for autistic

                                                                                          Anxiety or OCD

                                                                                          If you take an SSRI or other antidepressant, avoid supplements that act on serotonin (St John’s wort, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan, sceletium, SAMe), as the NCCIH St John’s wort fact sheet (opens a new tab) explains. Stimulating products such as caffeine, energy drinks and ginseng can feel like anxiety. In people with panic disorder, large amounts of caffeine set off panic attacks in about half (Klevebrant and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          With OCD, a supplement routine can quietly turn into a ritual: needing to take things perfectly, at the exact time, or checking labels again and again. The recommended therapy for OCD is CBT with exposure and response prevention (Oxford Health NHS: OCD (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          Show the options with notes for anxiety or OCD

                                                                                          Sensory sensitivity

                                                                                          People differ a lot in how strongly they notice sounds, light, smells and touch (Dunn, 1997 (opens a new tab)). Turning down the input often helps more than adding something: noise-reducing headphones lowered a stress measure in a small study of autistic children (Pfeiffer and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)), and quiet green spaces are lower in noise and crowds. Strong-smelling oils and some textures can make things worse rather than better.

                                                                                          Show the options with notes for sensory sensitivity

                                                                                          Highly sensitive

                                                                                          “Highly sensitive” describes a trait, not a disorder: some people notice more and feel more, in good ways and hard ones (Aron and Aron, 1997 (opens a new tab)). We found no research on supplements for highly sensitive people specifically. What tends to suit is the low-stimulus end of this page: quiet time, nature, soft sound, dimmer light, and planned recovery after busy days.

                                                                                          Dyslexic or dyspraxic

                                                                                          Only one thing here has research specific to these: in the Oxford-Durham trial of children with dyspraxia (developmental coordination disorder), fatty-acid capsules didn’t help movement skills, but reading, spelling and behavior improved over three months (Richardson and Montgomery, 2005 (opens a new tab)). It is one trial. Otherwise, the general advice applies. Voice notes and typing count for any “write it down” suggestion.

                                                                                          Chronic illness or low energy

                                                                                          Pacing (planning activity within your energy limits) is the approach UK guidance recommends for ME/CFS, and it says not to use fixed, step-by-step exercise increases (NICE ME/CFS guideline (NG206) (opens a new tab)). If you take several medicines, interactions matter more: St John’s wort, CBD, ginkgo and kava are the ones to be most careful with (NCCIH: herb-drug interactions (opens a new tab)). Blood tests (iron, B12, vitamin D) are a better guide than guessing. Tai chi may lift mood in some long-term illnesses (NCCIH tai chi fact sheet (opens a new tab)).

                                                                                          If you live with someone

                                                                                          Partners, family and roommates often have strong and different views about “natural” remedies. A few kind ground rules help:

                                                                                          • Don’t pressure anyone off their prescribed medicine in favor of herbs. That decision belongs to them.
                                                                                          • Share what you take. If you were ever unwell or confused, the person you live with could tell paramedics. A note on the fridge or in your phone works.
                                                                                          • Respect different views. One of you may love herbal teas and the other may trust only medicine. You don’t have to agree. You only need to be safe.
                                                                                          • Watch the shared shelf. Keep supplements, especially sweet gummies and iron tablets, away from children and pets.

                                                                                          Words you could use

                                                                                          To someone you live with: “I’m trying a magnesium supplement for sleep. I’m telling you so you know what I take.”

                                                                                          If someone pushes a remedy on you: “Thanks for caring. I’ll read the safety notes before I try anything new.”
