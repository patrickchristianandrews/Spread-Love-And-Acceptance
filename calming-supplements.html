Calming supplements, herbs and natural approaches: what the research says

Lots of people reach for chamomile tea, magnesium or a herbal capsule when life feels like too much. Some of these may help a little. A few can be risky, especially alongside prescribed medicines. Here is what the research actually shows for 79 options, from supplements and herbs to breathing, sleep and therapy, in plain words, with a link to every source.

Not medical advice. This page is general information from published research. It can’t know your health, your medicines or your situation. Before you start, stop or mix anything, ask a pharmacist or doctor. Never stop a prescribed medicine without talking to whoever prescribed it.

Last checked: October 2026

In short:

  • The things with the best evidence for stress and worry are free: sleep, movement, slow breathing, less caffeine and alcohol, and talking to someone.
  • A few supplements have some research behind them, but the effects are usually small and the studies small.
  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe. Kava, ashwagandha, skullcap and black cohosh have liver warnings, and St John’s wort, 5-HTP and kanna clash with antidepressants.
  • Use the drop-downs to see every option for a condition (like sleep or panic) or a neurotype (like ADHD or autistic).
  • Tell your pharmacist or doctor everything you take. If anxiety or low mood is stopping your daily life, get help: see when to get help.

Start here: before any supplement

A supplement can’t fix what is causing the stress: too much on your plate, a hard relationship, money, grief, or poor sleep. At best, some may take the edge off for a while. The things with the strongest evidence cost nothing. Try these first, in any order:

  1. Sleep. Steady wake-up times and a wind-down hour do more than most pills. For long-term insomnia, CBT for insomnia is the recommended first treatment. A regular schedule
  2. Movement. Hundreds of trials show regular activity eases anxiety and low mood. A brisk walk counts. Movement
  3. Daylight and green space. Get outside in daylight, especially in the morning and in winter. Daylight · Time in nature
  4. Food and drink. Eat regularly, cut down on caffeine, and go easy on alcohol, which makes anxiety worse afterward. Steady meals · Less caffeine · Less alcohol
  5. People. A friend, family or a counselor. Being heard is calming in itself. People

And when you need calm right now: breathe out slowly, longer than you breathe in. Take a one-minute Breathe break.

If you still want to try a supplement: check the safety notes, ask a pharmacist, change one thing at a time, and jot down how you feel over a few weeks so you can tell whether it helps.

Staying safe

None of this is meant to worry you. These are the plain facts worth knowing before you buy anything.

  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe. Plants contain active chemicals. That is why some can help, and why some can harm.
  • Supplements aren’t checked like medicines before they are sold. In the US, the FDA does not approve supplements before they reach shops. Makers are responsible for safety. In the UK, herbal medicines with a THR mark are registered for quality and safety, based on traditional use rather than trials.
  • Quality varies. Products can contain more, less or something other than the label says. Look for an independent testing mark such as USP Verified or NSF. These check what is in the bottle, not whether it works.
  • Medicines: the biggest risks are with antidepressants (serotonin syndrome with St John’s wort, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan or kanna), blood thinners (bleeding with ginkgo and high-dose fish oil, and changes with St John’s wort and CBD), thyroid medicine (ashwagandha, and calcium and iron block absorption), birth control pills (St John’s wort) and anything sedating.
  • Never mix sedating herbs (such as valerian, kava, passionflower or hops) with alcohol, sleeping pills, opioid painkillers or benzodiazepines. Together they can slow breathing and make you dangerously drowsy.
  • Liver or kidney problems: ask first. Kava, ashwagandha, skullcap, black cohosh, CBD and high-absorption curcumin have liver warnings, and the kidneys clear minerals such as magnesium.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: ask first. Most herbs have not been well tested in pregnancy.
  • Children and teens: talk to their doctor before giving any calming or sleep supplement, including melatonin. Keep iron tablets locked away: iron overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in young children.
  • Before surgery: tell the team everything you take. Some herbs, such as passionflower and ginkgo, matter for anesthesia and bleeding.
  • Don’t stop a prescribed medicine to switch to a herb without talking to the person who prescribed it. Some medicines need to be reduced slowly.
  • Driving: if something makes you sleepy, don’t drive or use machines until you know how it affects you.

What to ask a pharmacist

Pharmacists are free to talk to, and checking interactions is part of their job. Bring the bottle or a photo of the label.

“I take [your medicines]. I’m thinking of trying [the supplement]. Is it safe with what I take?”

“Is this product tested by anyone independent, and is the amount on the label sensible?”

“Does it make you sleepy, or affect the liver, kidneys or thyroid?”

“I’m pregnant / breastfeeding / buying this for my child. Is it okay?”

Chamomile

Herb Early evidence

A gentle tea with a small amount of research for worry. Nothing clear for sleep.

What it is
A daisy-like flower, usually drunk as a tea.
Used for
Worry, feeling on edge, and winding down at night.
What research shows
A few small studies hint it may ease long-running worry (generalized anxiety). For sleep, no clear benefit has been found.
Watch out
Allergy if you react to ragweed. May interact with warfarin.

Listed under: Anxiety and worry · Stress · Sleep · Restlessness and racing thoughts

Source: NCCIH chamomile fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Likely safe in tea amounts. Allergic reactions can happen, especially in people allergic to ragweed or related plants.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist first.
Medicines
May interact with warfarin (a blood thinner) and other medicines processed by the liver. Early studies suggest it might make birth control pills work less well.
Liver and kidneys
No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. If you have liver or kidney disease, ask first.
Children and teens
Not well studied in children. Ask their doctor before giving it.

In the largest trial, people with generalized anxiety took chamomile for 12 weeks. Those who improved were then given chamomile or a dummy pill for six months. Symptoms stayed lower on chamomile, but the difference in relapses was not statistically clear (Mao and colleagues, 2016 (opens a new tab)).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add chamomile, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Anxiety or OCD: A warm, caffeine-free tea is a gentle routine many people find settling. The routine may matter as much as the herb.

Lavender capsules (Silexan) and smelling it

Herb Some evidence

Lavender oil capsules have the better research for anxiety. Smelling lavender is pleasant but unproven.

What it is
A purple-flowered herb. There are two very different ways to use it: lavender oil capsules made to be swallowed (most research used one product, Silexan), and smelling the oil (aromatherapy).
Used for
Anxiety, stress and sleep.
What research shows
Five trials of the capsules (1,213 adults, 10 weeks) found less anxiety than a dummy pill. All five were paid for by the maker, and results can’t be assumed for other lavender products. For smelling lavender, it is unclear whether it helps.
Watch out
Only swallow products made to be swallowed. Burping and stomach upset can happen.

Listed under: Anxiety and worry · Stress · Sleep · Restlessness and racing thoughts

Sources: Dold and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab) · NCCIH lavender fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Capsules: stomach upset, headache, nausea, burping. Smelling it: sometimes headache or coughing. On skin: allergic rashes in some people.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding: ask first.
Medicines
Not tested alongside antidepressants or sleeping pills. Ask a pharmacist if you take other medicines.
Liver and kidneys
No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. If you have liver or kidney disease, ask first.
Children and teens
Not for children unless their doctor agrees. Never let children swallow essential oils.

An earlier review of five trials found the higher dose looked best, but the differences were not statistically clear (Yap and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add lavender, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Anxiety or OCD: The capsule trials included people with generalized anxiety and with “subthreshold” anxiety (worry that doesn’t meet a diagnosis). They did not test it alongside antidepressants, so ask your prescriber before mixing.

Sensory sensitivity: Smell is personal. If strong scents bother you, the capsules (or nothing) may suit you better than oils in the room.

Magnesium

Food or nutrient Weak evidence

Popular for stress and sleep, but the research is weak and low quality.

What it is
A mineral found in nuts, seeds, beans, whole grains and leafy greens. Supplements come in different forms.
Used for
Stress, anxiety, sleep and muscle cramps.
What research shows
Of eight studies on anxiety, four found a benefit. The reviewers called the evidence poor quality, though it may help people who are prone to anxiety. For sleep in older adults, three small trials found people fell asleep about 17 minutes sooner, but the evidence was low to very low quality.
Watch out
Over 350 mg a day from supplements can cause diarrhea. Ask first if you have kidney problems.

Listed under: Stress · Anxiety and worry · Sleep · Tension and pain-related stress · PMS and menstrual-cycle mood

Sources: Boyle and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab) · Mah and Pitre, 2021 (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Too much from supplements causes diarrhea, nausea and cramps. The adult upper limit from supplements and medicines is 350 mg a day (food doesn’t count). Very large amounts can cause heart problems.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Food sources are fine. Ask before taking a supplement in pregnancy.
Medicines
Antacids and laxatives often contain magnesium already. Magnesium can also affect how some antibiotics and bone medicines are absorbed: ask a pharmacist about timing.
Liver and kidneys
Ask first if you have kidney problems: healthy kidneys are what clear the extra.
Children and teens
Not well studied in children. Ask their doctor before giving it.

Forms: magnesium aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride dissolve better and are absorbed a little better than magnesium oxide and sulfate, in small studies. That doesn’t mean one form is “calming.” There is no good research comparing forms for stress or sleep (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, magnesium (for health professionals) (opens a new tab)).

Upper limits and side effects: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, magnesium (opens a new tab).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add magnesium, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Chronic illness or low energy: Magnesium is in many antacids and laxatives. If you take several medicines, ask a pharmacist to add it all up.

Ashwagandha

Herb Early evidence Liver and thyroid warnings

Small trials show lower stress scores, but there are rare liver injuries and thyroid effects.

What it is
A herb from Ayurvedic medicine.
Used for
Stress, anxiety and sleep.
What research shows
Small trials, pooled together, show lower stress and anxiety scores than a dummy pill. The anxiety result did not hold up when one trial was left out.
Watch out
Rare liver injury; may affect the thyroid; concerns in pregnancy.

Listed under: Stress · Anxiety and worry · Sleep · Burnout and fatigue

Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, ashwagandha (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Liver injury has been reported, usually 2 to 12 weeks after starting. Most people recovered, but rare cases of liver failure have been described. It may also change thyroid hormone levels.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
Medicines
Ask first if you take thyroid medicine, or medicines for diabetes, blood pressure, sleep or anxiety (sedatives), or to calm the immune system.
Liver and kidneys
Avoid it if you have liver problems. Stop and get checked if you notice yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or itching.
Children and teens
Not well studied in children. Ask their doctor before giving it.

It seems safe for up to about three months. Longer use hasn’t been studied well. The liver reports: LiverTox (NIH) (opens a new tab). The pooled trials: Akhgarjand and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab).

Denmark banned ashwagandha in food supplements in 2023, after a risk assessment said no safe dose could be set, and other European countries have looked at the same concerns. Supplement makers and some researchers dispute the Danish assessment (NutraIngredients news report, 2023 (opens a new tab)).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add ashwagandha, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Chronic illness or low energy: Ask first if you have a thyroid condition or an autoimmune condition, or take medicine that calms the immune system.

Valerian

Herb Mixed evidence

Studies on sleep disagree, and a sleep guideline advised against it for long-term insomnia.

What it is
A plant root, taken as a capsule or tea.
Used for
Sleep, and sometimes anxiety.
What research shows
Studies on sleep disagree, and a 2017 sleep-medicine guideline advised against it for long-term insomnia. Nothing conclusive for anxiety.
Watch out
Don’t mix with alcohol or sleeping pills.

Listed under: Sleep · Anxiety and worry · Restlessness and racing thoughts

Source: NCCIH valerian fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Headache, upset stomach, vivid dreams. Short-term use seems safe for most adults. Long-term safety is unknown.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist first.
Medicines
It may add to the effect of anything else that makes you sleepy: alcohol, sleeping pills, benzodiazepines, opioid painkillers and some antihistamines.
Liver and kidneys
No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. If you have liver or kidney disease, ask first.
Children and teens
Not well studied in children. Ask their doctor before giving it.

Don’t drive or use machines until you know how it affects you.

Kava

Herb Mixed evidence Liver warning

A small effect on anxiety in older trials, with rare but serious liver damage.

What it is
A plant from the Pacific islands, taken as a drink or supplement.
Used for
Anxiety.
What research shows
An older review of 12 trials found it eased anxiety a little more than a dummy pill, but the effect was small and not solid.
Watch out
Rare but serious liver damage. Never with alcohol or sedatives.

Listed under: Anxiety and worry · Social anxiety

Source: NCCIH kava fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Rare but serious liver injury, including deaths, from supplements and from drinks. Heavy long-term use can cause dry, scaly, yellowish skin.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
Medicines
Never with alcohol, sleeping pills or anxiety medicines such as benzodiazepines. It may add to the effect of anything else that makes you sleepy: alcohol, sleeping pills, benzodiazepines, opioid painkillers and some antihistamines.
Liver and kidneys
Avoid it if you have liver problems.
Children and teens
Not for children.

Most people are better off choosing something safer. The older trial review: Pittler and colleagues, 2003 (opens a new tab).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add kava, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Chronic illness or low energy: Best avoided if you have any liver condition or take several medicines.

St John’s wort

Herb Mixed evidence Many interactions

Not consistently helpful for low mood, and it interferes with many medicines.

What it is
A yellow-flowered plant.
Used for
Low mood more than anxiety.
What research shows
Not consistently effective for depression, and not approved as a medicine for it in the US.
Watch out
Never take it with an antidepressant (such as an SSRI) unless your prescriber says so: the mix can cause serotonin syndrome, which can be life-threatening. It also stops birth control pills working well and clashes with many other medicines.

Listed under: Low mood

Source: NCCIH St John’s wort fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Stomach upset, dizziness, confusion, tiredness, and burning more easily in the sun. Rarely, it can trigger psychosis in people with bipolar disorder.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist first.
Medicines
It changes how the body handles many medicines: antidepressants such as SSRIs (the mix can cause serotonin syndrome, with shaking, confusion and stiffness, and it can be life-threatening), birth control pills (they can stop working), blood thinners such as warfarin, HIV medicines, transplant medicines such as cyclosporine, digoxin, and benzodiazepines.
Liver and kidneys
No specific liver or kidney warning in the sources we checked. If you have liver or kidney disease, ask first.
Children and teens
Not well studied in children. Ask their doctor before giving it.

The list of interactions: NCCIH: herb-drug interactions (opens a new tab).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add St John’s wort, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

ADHD: For ADHD, it did no better than a dummy pill in a trial, and it interacts with many medicines (NCCIH: ADHD and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)).

Anxiety or OCD: If you take an SSRI or another antidepressant, this is the herb to be most careful with: the mix can cause serotonin syndrome.

Chronic illness or low energy: If you take several prescribed medicines, avoid it unless a pharmacist has checked every one.

Melatonin

Supplement Some evidence

Helps with body-clock problems like jet lag. Not an anxiety treatment.

What it is
A hormone the body makes at night. Sold as a supplement in the US. Prescription-only in the UK.
Used for
Sleep timing: jet lag and shift work.
What research shows
May help jet lag and shift-work sleep problems. Guidelines advise against it for long-term insomnia.
Watch out
Can make you drowsy. Children only with a doctor’s advice. Prescription-only in the UK.

Listed under: Sleep

Source: NCCIH melatonin fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
Uncommon: drowsiness, headache, dizziness, nausea. Long-term safety is less studied. In the US, the amount in a product can differ a lot from the label.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Not well studied in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Ask a doctor, midwife or pharmacist first.
Medicines
Ask first if you take a blood thinner or epilepsy medicine, or anything else that makes you sleepy.
Liver and kidneys
Ask first if you have liver or kidney problems or an autoimmune condition.
Children and teens
Talk to a doctor first. Good sleep habits come first. In the UK it is a prescription medicine.

Don’t drive soon after taking it. The rules differ by country: in the UK it is a prescription medicine, mainly for adults 55 and over. In the US it is sold as a supplement. UK guidance: NHS: who can take melatonin (opens a new tab).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add melatonin, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

ADHD: Limited evidence suggests it may help sleep problems in children with ADHD. Long-term safety is uncertain (NCCIH: ADHD and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)). Stimulant medicines can also affect sleep, so ask the prescriber about timing before adding melatonin.

Autistic: In one trial of 125 autistic children (and children with a rare genetic condition), a prescription prolonged-release melatonin helped them fall asleep sooner and sleep longer than a dummy pill (Gringras and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)). Ask the child’s doctor. Don’t swap in shop-bought products.

CBD (cannabidiol)

Supplement Weak evidence

Little good research for everyday stress or anxiety, and it can harm the liver.

What it is
A compound from the cannabis plant that doesn’t make you high.
Used for
Stress, anxiety, sleep and pain.
What research shows
Only one CBD medicine is approved in the US: a purified prescription form for seizures linked to three rare conditions (Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet syndrome and tuberous sclerosis complex). For everyday stress or anxiety, there is little good research so far.
Watch out
Can harm the liver and change how other medicines work, including antidepressants and blood thinners: ask your pharmacist first if you take any medicine.

Listed under: Stress · Anxiety and worry · Sleep · Tension and pain-related stress

Source: FDA: what you need to know about CBD (opens a new tab)

Safety, medicines and who should avoid it
Side effects
The FDA warns CBD can cause liver injury and changes in alertness, such as sleepiness.
Pregnancy and breastfeeding
Avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding (the FDA advises against it).
Medicines
CBD can change how other medicines work, including antidepressants and blood thinners, which can cause serious side effects.
Liver and kidneys
Can harm the liver: avoid it if you have liver problems.
Children and teens
Not for children unless prescribed.

The law on CBD products differs between countries and US states. The approved CBD medicine: FDA, 2018 (opens a new tab) and FDA: regulation of cannabis and CBD (opens a new tab).

Ask a pharmacist: “I take [my medicines and anything else]. Is it safe to add CBD, and is this product a good one?”

How different neurotypes may respond

Chronic illness or low energy: If you take several medicines, CBD is one to check carefully: it changes how many of them work.

Go deeper: what we left out, and why

We also looked at taurine, echium (Persian borage) and ear-clip vagus nerve stimulators sold for stress. We left them out because the research we could check on calm or mood in people was too thin, mostly in animals, or about other conditions. Leaving something out doesn’t mean it is harmful. It means we couldn’t confirm enough to say anything useful.

How different neurotypes may respond

ADHD

Stimulant medicines and caffeine both raise heart rate and blood pressure, so coffee, energy drinks and “focus” blends can add up (Drugs.com interaction checker (opens a new tab)). Some ADHD medicines lower appetite and affect sleep, so meal and bedtime timing matter. Ask the prescriber (NHS: side effects of methylphenidate (opens a new tab)).

Children with ADHD have lower stored iron on average, which is a reason to ask about a blood test, not to give iron blindly (Tseng and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)).

Deep pressure and weighted blankets are liked by many, but in a careful trial they didn’t lengthen autistic children’s sleep (Gringras and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab)). A prescription prolonged-release melatonin has helped autistic children fall asleep sooner (Gringras and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)).

About half of autistic people find it hard to name their feelings (alexithymia), compared with about 1 in 20 others (Kinnaird and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)). So “do you feel calmer?” can be a hard question. Track simple things instead: time to fall asleep, how often meltdowns or shutdowns happen, how long it takes to settle, or a 1 to 5 rating at the same time each day. Be wary of anything sold as an autism “cure”: some, such as chelation, can be dangerous (NCCIH: autism and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)).

Anxiety or OCD

If you take an SSRI or other antidepressant, avoid supplements that act on serotonin (St John’s wort, 5-HTP, L-tryptophan, sceletium, SAMe) unless your prescriber agrees (NCCIH St John’s wort fact sheet (opens a new tab)). Stimulating products such as caffeine, energy drinks and ginseng can feel like anxiety. In people with panic disorder, large amounts of caffeine set off panic attacks in about half (Klevebrant and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab)).

With OCD, a supplement routine can quietly turn into a ritual: needing to take things perfectly, at the exact time, or checking labels again and again. If a routine is becoming a rule, mention it to your therapist. The recommended therapy for OCD is CBT with exposure and response prevention (Oxford Health NHS: OCD (opens a new tab)).

Sensory sensitivity

People differ a lot in how strongly they notice sounds, light, smells and touch (Dunn, 1997 (opens a new tab)). Turning down the input often helps more than adding something: noise-reducing headphones lowered a stress measure in a small study of autistic children (Pfeiffer and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)), and quiet green spaces are lower in noise and crowds. Strong-smelling oils and some textures can make things worse rather than better.

Highly sensitive

“Highly sensitive” describes a trait, not a disorder: some people notice more and feel more, in good ways and hard ones (Aron and Aron, 1997 (opens a new tab)). We found no research on supplements for highly sensitive people specifically. What tends to suit is the low-stimulus end of this page: quiet time, nature, soft sound, dimmer light, and planned recovery after busy days.

Dyslexic or dyspraxic

Only one thing here has research specific to these: in the Oxford-Durham trial of children with dyspraxia (developmental coordination disorder), fatty-acid capsules didn’t help movement skills, but reading, spelling and behavior improved over three months (Richardson and Montgomery, 2005 (opens a new tab)). It is one trial. Otherwise, the general advice applies. Voice notes and typing count for any “write it down” suggestion.

Chronic illness or low energy

Pacing (planning activity within your energy limits) is the approach UK guidance recommends for ME/CFS, and it says not to use fixed, step-by-step exercise increases (NICE ME/CFS guideline (NG206) (opens a new tab)). If you take several medicines, interactions matter more: St John’s wort, CBD, ginkgo and kava are the ones to be most careful with (NCCIH: herb-drug interactions (opens a new tab)). Ask a pharmacist for a full medicines review, and ask for blood tests (iron, B12, vitamin D) rather than guessing. Tai chi may lift mood in some long-term illnesses (NCCIH tai chi fact sheet (opens a new tab)).

When to get help instead

Please talk to a doctor or a counselor if:

  • panic attacks don’t settle, or keep coming back;
  • anxiety or worry is stopping you working, sleeping, eating or seeing people;
  • you have felt low or numb most days for two weeks or more;
  • you often feel unreal, detached or outside your body.

If you live with someone

Partners, family and roommates often have strong and different views about “natural” remedies. A few kind ground rules help:

  • Don’t pressure anyone off their prescribed medicine in favor of herbs. That decision belongs to them and their prescriber.
  • Share what you take. If you were ever unwell or confused, the person you live with could tell paramedics. A note on the fridge or in your phone works.
  • Respect different views. One of you may love herbal teas and the other may trust only medicine. You don’t have to agree. You only need to be safe.
  • Watch the shared shelf. Keep supplements, especially sweet gummies and iron tablets, away from children and pets.

Words you could use

To a pharmacist: “I take sertraline and I’m thinking of trying St John’s wort. Is that safe together?”

“I take [your medicine] every day. Is [the supplement] safe with it, and is there anything I should watch for?”

To your doctor: “I’ve been taking ashwagandha for stress. Should I keep going, and do I need a blood test?”

To your doctor: “I’m tired all the time. Could we check my iron, B12 and thyroid before I try supplements?”

To someone you live with: “I’m trying a magnesium supplement for sleep. I’m telling you so you know what I take.”

If someone pushes a remedy on you: “Thanks for caring. I’ll ask my pharmacist before I try anything new.”
