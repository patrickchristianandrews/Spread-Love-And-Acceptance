Calming supplements and herbs: what the research says

Lots of people reach for chamomile tea, magnesium or an herbal capsule when life feels like too much. Some of these may help a little. A few can be risky, especially alongside prescribed medicines. Here is what the research actually shows, in plain words, with a link to every source.

Not medical advice. This page is general information from published research. It can’t know your health, your medicines or your situation. Before you start, stop or mix anything, ask a pharmacist or doctor. Never stop a prescribed medicine without talking to whoever prescribed it.

Last checked: October 2026

In short:

  • The things with the best evidence for stress and worry are free: sleep, movement, slow breathing, less caffeine and alcohol, and talking to someone.
  • A few supplements have some research behind them, but the effects are usually small and the studies small.
  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe. Kava and ashwagandha have liver warnings, and St John’s wort clashes with many medicines, including antidepressants and birth control pills.
  • Tell your pharmacist or doctor everything you take. If anxiety or low mood is stopping your daily life, get help: see when to get help.

Start here: what a supplement can and can’t do

A supplement can’t fix what is causing the stress: too much on your plate, a hard relationship, money, grief, or poor sleep. At best, some may take the edge off for a while. The things with the strongest evidence cost nothing:

  • Sleep. A regular bedtime and a wind-down hour do more than most pills. The NHS lists good sleep among its first self-help steps for anxiety.
  • Movement. Hundreds of trials show regular activity eases anxiety and low mood. A brisk walk counts.
  • Slow breathing. Breathing out slowly, longer than you breathe in, settles the body. Take a one-minute Breathe break.
  • Less caffeine and alcohol. Caffeine can speed up your heart and disturb sleep; alcohol can make anxiety worse afterwards.
  • Talking to someone. A friend, a helpline or a counsellor. Being heard is calming in itself.

Sources: NHS anxiety self-help (opens a new tab) · Singh and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab) · Zaccaro and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)

The common ones, honestly

Each card says what people use it for, what the research shows, side effects, and who should be careful. The labels mean:

  • Good evidence many good studies agree.
  • Some evidence several decent studies, with limits.
  • Early evidence a few small studies: a maybe.
  • Mixed evidence studies disagree, or it depends how it’s used.
  • Weak evidence little or poor-quality research.
  • No good evidence tested, and no benefit found.

Chamomile

Early evidence

Used for
Worry, feeling on edge, and winding down at night, usually as a tea.
What research shows
A few small studies hint it may ease long-running worry (generalized anxiety). For sleep, no clear benefit has been found.
Watch out
Allergy if you react to ragweed; may interact with warfarin.

Source: NCCIH chamomile fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Likely safe in tea amounts. Allergic reactions can happen, especially in people allergic to ragweed or related plants.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take warfarin or other medicines processed by the liver. Early studies suggest it might make birth control pills work less well.

In the largest trial, people with generalized anxiety took chamomile for 12 weeks. Those who improved were then given chamomile or a dummy pill for six months. Symptoms stayed lower on chamomile, but the difference in relapses was not statistically clear (Mao and colleagues, 2016 (opens a new tab)).

Lavender

Mixed evidence

Used for
Anxiety, stress and sleep. There are two very different ways to use it: lavender oil capsules made to be swallowed, and smelling the oil (aromatherapy).
What research shows
Capsules have the better research: a few trials in anxiety disorders, with promising but not fully clear results. For smelling lavender, it is unclear whether it helps anxiety, stress or sleep.
Watch out
Only swallow products made to be swallowed.

Source: NCCIH lavender fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Capsules: stomach upset, headache, nausea, burping. Smelling it: sometimes headache or coughing. On skin: allergic rashes in some people.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or take other medicines. Only swallow products made to be swallowed.

Most capsule trials used one standard product (Silexan). A 2019 review of five trials found the higher dose looked best, but the differences were not statistically clear (Yap and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)).

L-theanine

Early evidence

Used for
Feeling wired or stressed. It is an amino acid found in tea.
What research shows
Nine small trials suggest 200 to 400 mg a day may take the edge off stress and anxiety in people under pressure. The reviewers said bigger, longer studies are needed.
Watch out
Little safety research yet.

Source: Williams and colleagues, 2020 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Little research so far.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant, breastfeeding or on other medicines.

Magnesium

Weak evidence

Used for
Stress, anxiety, sleep and muscle cramps.
What research shows
Of eight studies on anxiety, four found a benefit. The reviewers called the evidence poor quality, though it may help people who are prone to anxiety.
Watch out
Over 350 mg a day from supplements can cause diarrhea. Ask first if you have kidney problems.

Source: Boyle and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Too much from supplements causes diarrhea, nausea and cramps. The adult upper limit from supplements and medicines is 350 mg a day (food doesn’t count). Very large amounts can cause heart problems.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have kidney problems (healthy kidneys are what clear the extra), or you already take antacids or laxatives that contain magnesium.

Upper limits and side effects: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, magnesium (opens a new tab).

Ashwagandha

Early evidence Safety warnings

Used for
Stress, anxiety and sleep. It is a herb from Ayurvedic medicine.
What research shows
Small trials, pooled together, show lower stress and anxiety scores than a dummy pill. The anxiety result did not hold up when one trial was left out.
Watch out
Rare liver injury; may affect the thyroid; concerns in pregnancy.

Source: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, ashwagandha (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Liver injury has been reported, usually 2 to 12 weeks after starting. Most people recovered, but rare cases of liver failure have been described. It may also change thyroid hormone levels.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have a thyroid condition or take thyroid medicine, have liver problems, or take medicines for diabetes, blood pressure, sleep or anxiety (sedatives), or to calm the immune system. It seems safe for up to about three months; longer use hasn’t been studied well.

The liver reports: LiverTox (NIH) (opens a new tab). The pooled trials: Akhgarjand and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab).

Denmark banned ashwagandha in food supplements in 2023, after a risk assessment said no safe dose could be set, and other European countries have looked at the same concerns. Supplement makers and some researchers dispute the Danish assessment (NutraIngredients news report, 2023 (opens a new tab)).

Valerian

Mixed evidence

Used for
Sleep, and sometimes anxiety.
What research shows
Studies on sleep disagree, and a 2017 sleep-medicine guideline advised against it for long-term insomnia. Nothing conclusive for anxiety.
Watch out
Don’t mix with alcohol or sleeping pills.

Source: NCCIH valerian fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Headache, upset stomach, vivid dreams. Short-term use seems safe for most adults; long-term safety is unknown.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take anything else that makes you sleepy, drink alcohol, or need to drive.

Passionflower

Early evidence

Used for
Anxiety, including nerves before surgery or the dentist.
What research shows
A small amount of research suggests it might ease anxiety, but nothing is definite.
Watch out
Stop before surgery unless your care team agrees.

Source: NCCIH passionflower fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
May be safe as a tea for up to 7 nights, or as a daily extract for up to 8 weeks.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have surgery coming up: it might interact with some anesthesia drugs, so stop it beforehand unless your care team says otherwise.

Lemon balm

Early evidence

Used for
Anxiety, low mood and sleep.
What research shows
Pooled trials found lower anxiety and low-mood scores than a dummy pill, especially in the short term. The trials differed a lot, so the reviewers urged caution.
Watch out
No serious side effects in trials. Ask first if pregnant or on medicines.

Source: Ghazizadeh and colleagues, 2021 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
No serious side effects were reported in the trials.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant, breastfeeding or on other medicines.

Kava

Mixed evidence Liver warning

Used for
Anxiety. It is a plant from the Pacific islands, taken as a drink or supplement.
What research shows
An older review of 12 trials found it eased anxiety a little more than a dummy pill, but the effect was small and not solid.
Watch out
Rare but serious liver damage. Never with alcohol or sedatives.

Source: NCCIH kava fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Rare but serious liver injury, including deaths, from supplements and from drinks. Heavy long-term use can cause dry, scaly, yellowish skin.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you drink alcohol, take sleeping pills or anxiety medicines such as benzodiazepines, have liver problems, or are pregnant or breastfeeding. Most people are better off choosing something safer.

The older trial review: Pittler and colleagues, 2003 (opens a new tab).

St John’s wort

Mixed evidence Many interactions

Used for
Low mood more than anxiety.
What research shows
Not consistently effective for depression, and not approved as a medicine for it in the US.
Watch out
Clashes with many medicines, including antidepressants and birth control pills.

Source: NCCIH St John’s wort fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Stomach upset, dizziness, confusion, tiredness, and burning more easily in the sun. Rarely, it can trigger psychosis in people with bipolar disorder.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take any prescribed medicine. It changes how the body handles many of them: antidepressants such as SSRIs (the mix can cause serotonin syndrome, with shaking, confusion and stiffness, and it can be life-threatening), birth control pills (they can stop working), blood thinners such as warfarin, HIV medicines, transplant medicines such as cyclosporine, digoxin, and benzodiazepines.

The list of interactions: NCCIH (opens a new tab).

Melatonin

Some evidence

Used for
Sleep timing: jet lag and shift work. It is a hormone the body makes at night, not an anxiety treatment.
What research shows
May help jet lag and shift-work sleep problems. Guidelines advise against it for long-term insomnia.
Watch out
Can make you drowsy. Children only with a doctor’s advice. Prescription-only in the UK.

Source: NCCIH melatonin fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Uncommon: drowsiness, headache, dizziness, nausea. Long-term safety is less studied. In the US, the amount in a product can differ a lot from the label.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have epilepsy or take a blood thinner, have liver or kidney problems or an autoimmune condition, or need to drive soon after. Children: talk to a doctor first; good sleep habits come first. The rules differ by country: in the UK it is a prescription medicine, mainly for adults 55 and over; in the US it is sold as a supplement.

UK guidance on who can take it: NHS (opens a new tab).

CBD (cannabidiol)

Weak evidence

Used for
Stress, anxiety, sleep and pain.
What research shows
Only one CBD medicine is approved in the US: a purified prescription form for seizures in two rare, severe forms of epilepsy. For everyday stress or anxiety, there is little good research so far.
Watch out
Can harm the liver and change how other medicines work.

Source: FDA: what you need to know about CBD (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
The FDA warns CBD can cause liver injury.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take other medicines: CBD can change how they work, which can cause serious side effects. The law on CBD products differs between countries and US states.

The one approved CBD medicine: FDA, 2018 (opens a new tab).

Omega-3 (fish oil)

Early evidence

Used for
Low mood and anxiety.
What research shows
Across 19 trials, omega-3 was linked with less anxiety, more clearly in people with a medical or mental health condition. The authors said it is too early to call it a first choice.
Watch out
High doses with a blood thinner may cause bleeding.

Source: Su and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Usually mild, such as an upset stomach.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take warfarin or another blood thinner: high doses may cause bleeding problems.

Side effects and bleeding: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, omega-3 (opens a new tab).

Saffron

Early evidence

Used for
Low mood and anxiety, as a supplement rather than the cooking spice.
What research shows
Across 23 trials, saffron eased low mood and anxiety more than a dummy pill. But there were signs that unhelpful results may be missing, and most trials came from one region, so the real effect may be smaller.
Watch out
Little long-term safety research.

Source: Marx and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Long-term safety: little research so far.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant, breastfeeding or on other medicines.

Rhodiola

Weak evidence

Used for
Tiredness, stress and mood.
What research shows
Not enough reliable evidence to show it helps anything; most studies are low to moderate quality.
Watch out
Possibly safe for up to 12 weeks; an interaction with losartan has been reported.

Source: NCCIH rhodiola fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Dizziness, headache, trouble sleeping, dry mouth. May be safe for up to 12 weeks.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take losartan (a blood pressure medicine), or are pregnant or breastfeeding.

Lesser-known options with promising research

You may see these called “powerful” online. The honest picture is that each has only a few studies, sometimes just one small one. They are interesting maybes, not proven treatments, and none of them replaces treatment a doctor has prescribed.

Curcumin (from turmeric)

Early evidence

Used for
Low mood.
What research shows
Six short trials (4 to 8 weeks) found a small improvement in low mood compared with a dummy pill. Nobody knows yet whether it lasts.
Watch out
“High-absorption” supplements may harm the liver.

Source: Ng and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Supplements made to be “highly absorbable”, often with black pepper extract (piperine), may harm the liver. Products vary in how much curcumin they contain.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have liver problems or take other medicines.

Safety and product quality: NCCIH turmeric fact sheet (opens a new tab).

Inositol

Early evidence

Used for
Panic attacks.
What research shows
One small 1995 study of 21 people found 12 grams a day (a large amount) cut panic attacks more than a dummy powder. A later review of small trials found no clear benefit for anxiety overall.
Watch out
The study used a large dose; stomach upset.

Source: Benjamin and colleagues, 1995 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Stomach upset was a little more common than on the dummy pill.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant or on other medicines.

The later review: Mukai and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab).

Glycine

Early evidence

Used for
Sleep. It is an amino acid.
What research shows
In a small study of poor sleepers, 3 grams before bed helped people feel they slept better and fell asleep sooner, and they were less sleepy the next day.
Watch out
Little safety research yet.

Source: Yamadera and colleagues, 2007 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Little research so far.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are pregnant or on other medicines.

Tart cherry juice

Early evidence

Used for
Sleep.
What research shows
A pilot study of 15 older adults with insomnia, linked to a cherry industry group, found a modest drop in time awake at night.
Watch out
Mind the sugar if you have diabetes.

Source: Pigeon and colleagues, 2010 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
It is juice: think about the sugar if you have diabetes.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have diabetes.

Holy basil (tulsi)

Early evidence

Used for
Stress. Often taken as a tea.
What research shows
A review of 24 mostly small studies reported good results for stress, blood sugar and blood pressure, with no significant side effects. The reviewers asked for more research on dose and form.
Watch out
May lower blood sugar: ask first if you take diabetes medicine.

Source: Jamshidi and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
No significant side effects reported in the studies.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you take diabetes medicine (it may lower blood sugar too), or are pregnant.

Zinc

Early evidence

Used for
Low mood, sometimes alongside prescribed treatment.
What research shows
A few small trials found zinc lowered depression scores, but a 2022 review found this only when zinc was taken on its own, not added to antidepressants. Earlier reviews disagreed. Never use it in place of prescribed treatment.
Watch out
No more than 40 mg a day for adults. Never instead of prescribed treatment.

Source: Yosaee and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
The adult upper limit is 40 mg a day. Too much causes nausea, vomiting and headaches, and over time can lower copper levels.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you already take a multivitamin or cold remedy with zinc.

Upper limits and side effects: NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, zinc (opens a new tab).

Probiotics (“psychobiotics”)

Mixed evidence

Used for
Low mood and anxiety, through the gut.
What research shows
Across 34 trials, prebiotics (fibre supplements) did no better than a dummy pill. Probiotics showed small effects, mostly in people with a diagnosed condition. The best strains and doses are unknown.
Watch out
Ask first if you are unwell.

Source: Liu and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Not covered by the review.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are unwell or on other medicines.

N-acetylcysteine (NAC)

Early evidence

Used for
Low mood.
What research shows
Five trials (574 people, mostly with other conditions too) found it eased low mood a little more than a dummy pill.
Watch out
Minor side effects in trials. Ask first if you take other medicines.

Source: Fernandes and colleagues, 2016 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Only minor side effects in the trials.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you are on other medicines.

Vitamin D

No good evidence

Used for
Low mood, especially in winter.
What research shows
In a very large trial of 18,353 adults aged 50 and over, five years of vitamin D did not prevent depression or lift mood. Most of them were not low in vitamin D to begin with.
Watch out
If a blood test shows you are low, that is a question for your doctor.

Source: Okereke and colleagues, 2020 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Not the issue here.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
a blood test shows you are low: that is a separate question for your doctor.

Natural medicine without a pill

Some of the best-supported “natural” options don’t come in a bottle.

Movement

Good evidence

Used for
Low mood, anxiety and stress.
What research shows
Hundreds of trials show physical activity eases depression, anxiety and distress. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training did especially well.
Watch out
Start gently. Ask what is safe if you have a heart condition.

Source: Singh and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Aches; start gently if you have been inactive.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have a heart or other health condition: ask what is safe for you.

Which kinds of exercise helped most: Noetel and colleagues, 2024 (opens a new tab).

Time in nature (“forest bathing”)

Early evidence

Used for
Stress, worry and low mood.
What research shows
Twenty studies found time among trees eased anxiety, low mood and anger in the short term. Longer, stronger studies are still needed.
Watch out
Low risk.

Source: Kotera and colleagues, 2022 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
None to speak of. Mind ticks and the weather.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
never: but go with someone if being alone outdoors feels unsafe.

Daylight

Early evidence

Used for
Winter low mood and sleep timing.
What research shows
The NHS suggests getting as much natural daylight as you can. Light boxes help some people, but the NHS says there isn’t enough evidence to be sure.
Watch out
Ask before using a light box if you have an eye condition.

Source: NHS: seasonal affective disorder treatment (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Light boxes can be a problem for some eyes.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
you have an eye condition or take medicine that makes you sensitive to light: check before using a light box.

People

Good evidence

Used for
Everything. Feeling less alone.
What research shows
Across 148 studies, people with stronger social ties were more likely to survive over the years studied. That shows a strong link, though it can’t prove cause.
Watch out
Low risk.

Source: Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, 2010 (opens a new tab)

Side effects and who should avoid it
Side effects
Some people drain you. Choose the ones who leave you calmer.
Avoid it, or ask first, if
never.
Go deeper: what we left out, and why

We also looked at bacopa, echium (Persian borage) and magnolia bark. We left them out because the research we could check on calm or mood was too thin, or mostly about other things such as memory. Leaving something out doesn’t mean it is harmful; it means we couldn’t confirm enough to say anything useful.

Other approaches, graded honestly

Slow breathing

Some evidence

Breathing out slowly and steadily is linked with feeling calmer and less wound up. Free, quick and safe. Try the Breathe break.

Source: Zaccaro and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)

Mindfulness and meditation

Some evidence

Across 47 trials, mindfulness programs gave small improvements in anxiety, low mood and pain. They were not better than other active choices, such as exercise.

Source: Goyal and colleagues, 2014 (opens a new tab)

Yoga

Some evidence

May ease anxiety and low mood symptoms; unclear for anxiety disorders. Generally safe with a qualified teacher.

Source: NCCIH yoga fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Tai chi

Early evidence

Safe and gentle. Research on mood is limited, but it may lift mood and quality of life in people living with some long-term illnesses.

Source: NCCIH tai chi fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Massage

Mixed evidence

May ease anxiety in some settings, but results are inconsistent, and it hasn’t been shown to treat generalized anxiety. Low risk.

Source: NCCIH massage fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Acupuncture

Early evidence

Some studies are positive, but many are poor quality. Use a licensed practitioner with sterile, single-use needles.

Source: NCCIH: anxiety and complementary approaches (opens a new tab)

Aromatherapy

Weak evidence

It is unclear whether smelling lavender or other oils helps anxiety, stress or sleep. Pleasant and low risk if you don’t swallow the oils.

Source: NCCIH lavender fact sheet (opens a new tab)

Music

Early evidence

Early research suggests music can ease anxiety, for example while waiting for surgery. Try the calm music and sounds here.

Source: NCCIH music and health (opens a new tab)

Homeopathy

No good evidence

There is no good-quality evidence that homeopathy works for any health condition. If you use it, don’t let it replace treatment that works.

Source: NHS: homeopathy (opens a new tab)

Staying safe

  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safe. Plants contain active chemicals. That is why some can help, and why some can harm.
  • Supplements aren’t checked like medicines before they are sold. In the US, the FDA does not approve supplements before they reach shops; makers are responsible for safety. In the UK, herbal medicines with a THR mark are registered for quality and safety, based on traditional use rather than trials.
  • Quality varies. Products can contain more, less or something other than the label says. Look for an independent testing mark such as USP Verified or NSF. These check what is in the bottle, not whether it works.
  • Tell your doctor and pharmacist what you take, including teas and gummies, every time a new medicine is started.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: ask first. Many herbs have not been well tested in pregnancy.
  • Children and teens: talk to their doctor before giving any calming or sleep supplement, including melatonin.
  • Liver or kidney problems: ask first. Several herbs on this page have liver warnings, and the kidneys clear minerals such as magnesium.
  • Before surgery: tell the team everything you take. Some herbs, such as passionflower, may interact with anesthesia.
  • Never mix sedating herbs (such as valerian, kava or passionflower) with alcohol, sleeping pills, opioid painkillers or benzodiazepines. Together they can slow breathing and make you dangerously drowsy.
  • Don’t stop a prescribed medicine to switch to a herb without talking to the person who prescribed it. Some medicines need to be reduced slowly.
  • Driving: if something makes you sleepy, don’t drive or use machines until you know how it affects you.

Sources: FDA: supplements Q&A (opens a new tab) · NHS: herbal medicines (opens a new tab) · MHRA: the THR mark (opens a new tab) · USP Verified (opens a new tab) · NSF certification (opens a new tab) · NCCIH: herb-drug interactions (opens a new tab)

When to get help instead

Please reach out to a doctor, a counsellor or a helpline if:

  • panic attacks don’t settle, or keep coming back;
  • anxiety or worry is stopping you working, sleeping, eating or seeing people;
  • you have felt low or numb most days for two weeks or more;
  • you are having thoughts of harming yourself.

Free, any time: in the US, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline). In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans on 116 123. In an emergency, call 911 or 999.

Help beyond this site lists counsellors, helplines and support groups. Afraid of someone at home? Not safe at home? has private help.

If you live with someone

Partners, family and housemates often have strong and different views about “natural” remedies. A few kind ground rules help:

  • Don’t pressure anyone off their prescribed medicine in favour of herbs. That decision belongs to them and their prescriber.
  • Share what you take. If you were ever unwell or confused, the person you live with could tell paramedics. A note on the fridge or in your phone works.
  • Respect different views. One of you may love herbal teas and the other may trust only medicine. You don’t have to agree; you only need to be safe.
  • Watch the shared shelf. Keep supplements, especially sweet gummies, away from children and pets.

Words you could use

To a pharmacist: “I take sertraline and I’m thinking of trying St John’s wort. Is that safe together?”

“I take [your medicine] every day. Is [the supplement] safe with it, and is there anything I should watch for?”

To your doctor: “I’ve been taking ashwagandha for stress. Should I keep going, and do I need a blood test?”

To someone you live with: “I’m trying a magnesium supplement for sleep. I’m telling you so you know what I take.”

If someone pushes a remedy on you: “Thanks for caring. I’ll ask my pharmacist before I try anything new.”
