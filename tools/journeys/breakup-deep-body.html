The examples on this page are made up. They are built from common situations, not real people.

The first days

The first days after a breakup can feel physical: a tight chest, no appetite, broken sleep, a mind that keeps replaying the same scenes. That is a normal stress response to a big change. It isn’t a sign that something is wrong with you.

The aim for now is not to understand everything. It’s to keep your body steady enough that the rest can come later.

  • Food and water at roughly normal times, even if it’s small.
  • Daylight in the morning, and a short walk if you can.
  • One or two people who know, with a clear ask: “Text me in the evenings this week.”
  • A pause on big decisions: moving, quitting, telling everyone, or getting back together, unless you have to.
  • A plan for the evenings, when it is often hardest: a show, a call, a bath, a walk, a calm game.

Words you could use

To a friend: “I don’t need you to fix it. I just don’t want Saturday to be empty.”

To a work manager, if you choose to: “I’m going through a breakup. I’m okay to work, and I might need a little flexibility this week.”

Feelings come in waves

Grief researchers describe loss as coming in waves rather than in neat stages. A breakup is a loss too: of a person, a routine and a future you had pictured. So it’s common to swing between sadness, anger, relief and calm, sometimes within an hour.

What research suggests

Eastwick, Finkel, Krishnamurti and Loewenstein (2008) followed dating students over nine months. Before any breakup, they asked how upset people expected to feel if it ended. Those who did break up felt less distress afterwards than they had predicted, and the people most in love overestimated it the most. The study was small, but it fits a wider pattern: we tend to overestimate how bad and how long future pain will be.

Bonanno (2004) reviewed research on loss and found that resilience, staying fairly steady or bouncing back, is more common than many people assume, and that it comes about in different ways for different people.

Try this: For two weeks, take a one-minute reading each day with How am I right now?. Look back on day 14. Most people see the hard days spacing out, even if some are still hard.

A kinder inner voice

Many people meet a breakup with harsh self-talk. It can feel like taking responsibility. Often it turns into going over the same faults again and again, which keeps the hurt going.

What research suggests

Neff (2003) described self-compassion as three things: being kind to yourself instead of harshly judging, remembering that suffering is part of being human, and noticing painful feelings without being swept away by them.

Sbarra, Smith and Mehl (2012) asked 109 divorcing adults to talk for four minutes about their separation. Listeners rated how self-compassionate each person sounded. Those who sounded more self-compassionate had less divorce-related distress in daily life, and the difference was still there nine months later. It shows a link over time, not proof that self-compassion caused the recovery, but it points the same way as other self-compassion research.

Words you could use

“This is a hard moment. Hard moments pass.”

“I did some things I’d do differently. So did they. That doesn’t make either of us a bad person.”

“What would I say to a friend in my place? I’ll say that to me.”

Try this: When the harsh voice starts, write down what it says. Then write what a kind friend would say back. Read the second one out loud.

Who am I now?

In a close relationship, two people’s lives and identities overlap: shared friends, shared jokes, “we” instead of “I.” When it ends, part of that overlap goes too.

What research suggests

Slotter, Gardner and Finkel (2010) found that people’s sense of who they are changed after a breakup, and that feeling less clear about who you are went with more emotional distress.

Larson and Sbarra (2015) gave 210 recently separated young adults either many short research visits over nine weeks, where they thought about their breakup in a structured way, or just two visits. The group that reflected more often grew clearer about who they were, and that clearer sense of self went with less intrusive breakup feelings, less loneliness and less “we” talk about the separation. It wasn’t a therapy, just calm, regular reflection, which is something you can do on your own.

For example (made up): Marcus, 38, realised most of his weekends had been built around his ex’s family. In the first month he felt like a guest in his own life. He made a short list: climbing, cooking, his cousin. He went climbing twice in March and called his cousin every Sunday. “I didn’t become a new person,” he said. “I found a few bits of the old one.”

Their posts and photos

Looking at an ex’s profile is common, and easy to slip into at night.

What research suggests

Marshall (2012) surveyed 464 people after a breakup. Those who checked their ex’s Facebook page more often reported more distress, more longing for the ex and less personal growth, even after allowing for offline contact and personality. It is a survey, so it shows a link, not cause and effect.

Langeslag and Sanchez (2018) asked people after a breakup to try different ways of thinking while looking at photos of their ex. Thinking about the ex’s less likeable sides lowered love feelings but also lowered mood for a while. Distraction lifted mood but didn’t change the feelings. The study was small and short, but it suggests mixing the two: a clear reminder of why it ended, then something absorbing.

  • Mute or archive rather than deciding on unfriending right now.
  • Move photos to a folder you don’t open by accident.
  • Phone out of the bedroom after a set time, for a while.
  • Write a short “why it ended” note to read when the pull is strong, then do something you enjoy.

Write it out

Frattaroli (2006) pooled 146 studies of “experimental disclosure”, such as writing about something hard for a short time on a few days. On average it helped a little, and the effect varied from person to person. So it is worth a try, and fine to stop if it doesn’t help you.

Try this: On three days this week, write for fifteen minutes about what happened and how you feel. On the last day, add one thing you learned about what you want next.

Sleep and moving

Ben Simon and Walker (2018) found that after a night without sleep, people kept others at more of a distance and felt lonelier. A breakup already makes the world feel lonely, so protecting sleep matters more now, not less.

Singh and colleagues (2023) brought together nearly a hundred reviews of trials and found that physical activity reduced symptoms of depression, anxiety and distress. A daily walk counts.

Small steps

Small actions repeated each day add up to steadiness. Noticing them helps too, because on a low day it’s easy to forget what you did manage.

When it feels heavy

If the heaviness doesn’t lift over a few weeks, if sleep or eating stay badly off, or if you feel hopeless, please tell someone today: a friend or your doctor.
