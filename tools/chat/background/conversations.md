# Shaping hard conversations

## A simple agenda for a hard talk
aka: conversation agenda; agenda for a difficult conversation; planning a talk
see: The parking lot; Timeboxing a conversation; Ending a hard conversation well
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Borrowed from meeting facilitation. It’s practical advice rather than a tested method, but structure tends to lower anxiety for many people, especially those who dislike surprises.
try: “Tonight: ten minutes on the weekend plan. Each of us says what we need, then we pick one thing to try. That’s it.”

A hard conversation goes better with a small, agreed shape: the one topic, roughly how long, and what “done” looks like (usually one agreed next step). Saying it at the start lowers the sense of threat. Nobody is walking into an ambush.

For many people, especially those who process slowly or dislike surprises, knowing the topic in advance makes a big difference. A text like “Can we talk about the bills tonight? Nothing bad, just want a plan” gives them time to think.

Program: Check-ins are built this way: a named time, one topic, hearing each other, one ask, one owner, one time.

## The parking lot
aka: parking lot list; park it; topics for later
see: A simple agenda for a hard talk; One topic at a time
go: /check-ins.html#ground | Check-in ground rules
evidence: A standard facilitation technique for keeping meetings on track. There’s little formal research, but it’s widely used because it works simply.
try: “That’s a real one, and it deserves its own time. Let’s put it on the list for Sunday.”

When a second topic pops up in a hard talk, the parking lot is a note where it goes to wait safely. It isn’t dismissed. It’s scheduled. That lets you finish the first topic without the conversation sprawling into every old grievance.

Keep the list somewhere both people can see (a note on the fridge or a shared phone note), and actually come back to it. A parking lot that never gets visited stops being trusted.

Program: The Check-ins ground rules say: if a second topic comes up, jot it down for another day.

## One topic at a time
aka: single topic; stay on topic; no kitchen sinking
see: The parking lot; A simple agenda for a hard talk
go: /library/conflict.html#one-topic | One topic (Library)
evidence: Relationship researchers describe “kitchen-sinking” (throwing in every complaint) as a sign of escalating conflict. Keeping to one topic is common advice from couples researchers and mediators.
try: “I want to stay on the holiday plan for now. Can we save the money stuff for Thursday?”

A conversation about the bins that turns into a conversation about everything is almost impossible to finish well. One topic keeps it small enough to actually solve, and small wins build trust for the bigger ones.

If one topic keeps dragging others in, that’s useful information too: maybe the topics share a root, like a missing owner or a timing clash, worth its own conversation.

Program: The Library’s one-topic entry goes deeper. WP-04, the monthly look-back, is where you look for the shared root behind several topics.

## Timeboxing a conversation
aka: time limit on a talk; timeboxing; 20 minute talk
see: A simple agenda for a hard talk; Coming back after a break
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A practical technique from work and facilitation. Many couples and families find shorter, more frequent talks easier than long ones, though there’s no magic number.
try: “Let’s give this twenty minutes. If we’re not done, we’ll pick another time.”

Hard talks that have no end can feel endless. Agreeing a time limit (often twenty or thirty minutes) makes it easier to start, and stops a tired conversation from sliding into a fight.

When the time is up, stop, even mid-topic, and agree when to continue. Stopping on purpose is a success, not a failure. It protects both people’s batteries.

Program: This fits Pillar III, Read your state first. The daily check-in (WP-13) is the extreme version: 90 seconds, no debate.

## Coming back after a break
aka: returning after a timeout; resuming a conversation; after a pause
see: Timeboxing a conversation; Conflict escalation ladder
go: /wp-11.html | WP-11: The Calm-Down Kit
evidence: Couples research suggests breaks during flooded arguments help only if people actually calm down (rather than rehearsing grievances) and come back. The returning part is what builds trust.
try: “I’m back. Thanks for the space. Can we start with just the plan for tomorrow?”

A pause only works if it ends. Coming back at the time you named shows the break was about calming down, not walking out. That promise is what makes the next pause easier to take.

When you return, start small and gentle: thank them for the space, restate the one topic, and maybe begin with something easy you can agree on. Diving straight back into the hardest point often reignites things.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) uses your battery score to decide when to go back, and suggests starting with one small, simple task first.

## Ending a hard conversation well
aka: ending on a good note; closing a conversation; wrapping up a talk
see: Summarizing a conversation; Written recaps after a talk
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Research on memory (the peak-end rule) suggests people remember experiences partly by how they end. Applying that to conversations is a reasonable inference rather than a direct finding.
try: “Thanks for talking this through with me. I feel better. So: you’ll book the plumber, and we’ll check in Friday.”

How a hard talk ends colors how both people remember it. Ending with a clear next step, a thank-you and something warm (even a small joke or a hug, if welcome) helps the whole thing feel worth doing.

If you didn’t reach agreement, you can still end well: name what you understood about each other, and when you’ll pick it up again.

Program: The Library entry on the peak-end rule explains why endings matter. Check-ins end with one ask, one owner and one time.

## Written recaps after a talk
aka: recap message; follow-up message; write it down afterward
see: Summarizing a conversation; Handoff notes at work
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: A common workplace practice. Research on memory shows people remember conversations differently afterward, which is the problem a written note solves.
try: “Thanks for today. Just so we’re on the same page: I’ll handle school forms, you’ll do Friday pickups, and we’ll review next month.”

Memories of a conversation drift, especially emotional ones. A short written recap, sent the same day, gives both people the same record. It’s not a contract, just a shared note.

Keep it neutral and brief, stick to what was agreed, and invite corrections. A recap that adds new complaints stops being a recap.

Program: For household jobs, write agreements into WP-03 and both initial it. It’s the same idea: change it on purpose, not by drift.

## Talking side by side
aka: side by side talks; talking in the car; walk and talk
see: Open and closed questions; Emotion coaching
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: A widely shared observation from parents, therapists and youth workers, especially about teenagers and many men. It’s practical wisdom rather than a tested finding.
try: “Want to walk the dog with me?” rather than “We need to talk.”

Some people talk more easily side by side than face to face: in the car, on a walk, while cooking or fixing something. Less eye contact and a shared task can make a conversation feel lower-stakes.

This is especially useful with teenagers, with people who find direct eye contact uncomfortable, and with anyone who tenses up at “can we talk?”. The talk can be shorter and lighter, and it still counts.

Program: Turning Toward is about small everyday moments rather than big sit-down talks. A side-by-side chat is a good example.

## Stop, start, continue
aka: stop start continue; three questions review; start stop keep
see: Rose, bud, thorn; Retrospective for households
go: /workpapers/wp-04-what-keeps-coming-back.html | WP-04: What keeps coming back?
evidence: A common review format from workplace teams and teaching. It’s simple and widely used; there’s little formal research on it specifically.
try: “Stop: leaving laundry in the machine overnight. Start: a Sunday menu plan. Continue: the daily check-in.”

A quick way to review how something is going: what should we stop doing, start doing, and continue doing? It keeps a review practical and balanced, because “continue” makes room for what’s working.

It works for households, friendships with shared plans, teams and co-parents. Keep each list short, one or two items, and give each change one owner.

## Rose, bud, thorn
aka: rose bud thorn; highs and lows; best and hardest part
see: Stop, start, continue; Open and closed questions
go: /workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: A popular reflection game from camps, schools and design teams. It’s a conversation tool, not a researched method.
try: “Rose: lunch with you. Bud: the trip next month. Thorn: that email from work.”

Each person shares a rose (something good), a thorn (something hard) and a bud (something they’re looking forward to). It’s quick, gentle and works well at dinner, even with kids.

It gives everyone a turn and naturally mixes good news with hard news, so nobody has to announce “I need to talk about something”.

Program: The 90-second daily check-in (WP-13) has a similar rhythm: how full you were today, one thing you appreciated, one small thing that didn’t feel great, and one thing that would help tomorrow.

## Retrospective for households
aka: household retro; family retrospective; home review meeting
see: Stop, start, continue; Family meetings
go: /workpapers/wp-04-what-keeps-coming-back.html | WP-04: What keeps coming back?
evidence: Adapted from team retrospectives in software and project work. Evidence for home use is anecdotal, but the structure (look at the process, not blame) matches the program’s approach.
try: “What went well this month? What kept slipping? What’s one change, and who owns it?”

A retrospective is a regular, blame-free look back at how things went: what worked, what didn’t, and one or two changes to try. Teams use them after projects. Households can use them monthly.

The key rule is the same as in good team retros: look at the setup and the process, not at who failed. A slipped job is a clue about the system, like a missing owner or a bad time slot.

Program: WP-04 is the program’s household retro, and the Library’s entry on retrospectives covers the team version.

## Choosing the channel
aka: text or talk; which channel; call or text; in person or message
see: Don’t fight by text; When to switch to voice
go: /carrier-wave-decoder.html | The Carrier Wave Decoder
evidence: Communication research suggests text carries fewer cues (tone, face, timing) than voice or in-person talk, so ambiguity rises. The best channel still depends on the people and the topic.
try: “This one feels like a talk, not a text. Can I call you on my lunch break?”

Logistics suit texts: times, lists, confirmations. Feelings, disagreements and anything complicated usually go better by voice or in person, where tone and a face can soften the words.

Some people, especially many autistic people or those who need time to process, prefer writing for hard topics because it lets them think. That’s a good reason to agree the channel together rather than assuming.

Program: The Carrier Wave Decoder looks at pace, tone and timing, which a channel can distort. The Wiring Card can say which channel works best for you.

## One ask at a time
aka: a single request; one clear ask; make one request
see: One topic at a time; Clear yes or no questions
go: /workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A practical guideline from communication and negotiation training. People can only act on so much at once, especially under stress.
try: “Could you take the recycling out on Tuesdays from now on?” rather than a list of five things.

A conversation that ends with five requests usually ends with none of them happening. One clear ask, with a who and a when, is much more likely to stick.

If you have several, pick the one that matters most or would make the biggest difference. The others can wait for the next check-in, or go on the parking lot list.

Program: Every check-in ends with one ask, one owner, one time. WP-09’s last step is the ask.

## Starting from a shared goal
aka: common ground; shared goal first; we want the same thing
see: A simple agenda for a hard talk; Trading across issues
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Negotiation and mediation research emphasizes shared interests as a foundation for agreement. Naming the shared goal first is a practical application of that.
try: “We both want Sundays to feel restful. Let’s figure out how to get there.”

Before discussing a disagreement, naming what you both want (a calm home, happy kids, a project that ships) reminds you that you’re on the same side, facing the problem together.

It changes the question from “who wins?” to “how do we get there?”. Even when you disagree on the how, agreeing on the why makes the disagreement feel smaller.

Program: The Check-ins opener says it out loud: “I am not against you. I do not think you meant harm. I want to sort out this one thing.”
