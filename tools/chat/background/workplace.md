# Workplace team dynamics

These add to the Library’s teams section.

## Team working agreements
aka: working agreement; team charter; team norms
see: Meeting norms; Decision rights at work
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: Widely used in agile and team-development practice. Research on team norms suggests explicit agreements help, though evidence on specific formats is limited.
try: “Replies within a day, meetings start on time, and every task in the tracker has one owner.”

A working agreement is a short list of how a team works together: response times, meeting habits, how decisions are made, how to raise problems. It makes unwritten rules visible.

Teams write it together and revisit it when things change. It helps new members settle in, too.

Program: WP-03 is a working agreement for jobs. The same one-owner-per-job idea works for a team of seven.

## Decision rights at work
aka: who decides; DACI; decision roles; decision making at work
see: Team working agreements; Consensus and consent
go: /library/teams.html#raci | RACI (Library)
evidence: Frameworks like RACI and DACI are common in organizations. They’re practical tools, not research-backed models, and their value depends on actually using them.
try: “For the launch date, who drives it, who approves it, and who needs to be consulted?”

Many workplace frustrations come from unclear decision rights: nobody knows who gets the final say, so decisions stall or get made without the right people. Frameworks like DACI (driver, approver, contributors, informed) spell this out.

Even without a framework, asking “who decides this?” at the start of a project saves a lot of friction.

Program: The Library’s RACI entry explains the classic version. WP-03 applies it to household jobs.

## Meeting norms
aka: meeting rules; better meetings; meeting etiquette
see: Team working agreements; Round-robin turns
go: /library/communication.html#workplace-communication | Workplace communication (Library)
evidence: Research on meetings suggests clear purpose, agendas and inclusive participation improve them. Specific rules are practice-based.
try: Agenda shared a day before, one person talks at a time, and every action gets an owner and a date.

Good meetings tend to share a few habits: a clear purpose, an agenda, starting and ending on time, everyone gets heard, and each action has an owner.

Agreeing these as a team, rather than hoping for them, makes meetings calmer and more useful.

Program: The coworker road in the Workpaper Suite leans on one owner per job and the Signal Translator.

## Round-robin turns
aka: round robin; everyone gets a turn; taking turns in meetings
see: Meeting norms; Being interrupted at work
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: A simple facilitation technique. Research on group discussion suggests structured turn-taking can widen participation, especially for quieter members.
try: “Let’s go around once. Thirty seconds each, no interruptions, then open discussion.”

In a round-robin, each person speaks in turn before open discussion begins. It makes sure quieter voices are heard and reduces interruptions.

It’s especially useful in teams where a few people tend to dominate.

Program: For interruptions at work, the situation advice suggests naming the pattern kindly and proposing a structure like this.

## Being interrupted at work
aka: talked over in meetings; interruptions in meetings; holding the floor
see: Round-robin turns; Meeting norms
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Research shows interruptions are common in meetings and that some people get interrupted more than others. Practical techniques come from communication training.
try: “I’d like to finish this point, and then I really want to hear your view.”

Being interrupted repeatedly can leave you feeling unheard and your ideas lost. Often the interrupter is enthusiastic rather than dismissive, but the effect is the same.

Useful moves: a calm line to hold your turn, a private word with the person, and suggesting team norms like round-robins.

Program: The Signal Translator helps you test how your “let me finish” line might land.

## One-on-ones
aka: one on one meetings; 1:1s; check ins with manager
see: Asking for feedback; Managing up
go: /workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: Regular one-on-ones are widely recommended in management practice. Research links good manager relationships with engagement, though one-on-ones themselves are less studied.
try: Bring three things: what’s going well, what’s stuck, and one thing you need.

A one-on-one is a regular, private meeting between two people at work, often a manager and a team member. It’s a place to raise small things before they grow, and to build trust.

The best ones are regular, protected from cancellation and led partly by the team member’s agenda.

Program: It’s the workplace cousin of the daily check-in: small and often beats big and rare.

## Asking for feedback
aka: requesting feedback; how am I doing; feedback request
see: One-on-ones; Receiving criticism at work
go: /library/communication.html#feedback | Feedback (Library)
evidence: Research suggests specific feedback requests get more useful answers than general ones. Feedback-seeking is linked with learning, though the relationship is complex.
try: “What’s one thing I could do differently in these client calls?”

A general “any feedback?” usually gets “all good!”. A specific question (“what’s one thing I could improve in my reports?”) gets something useful.

Asking regularly makes feedback feel normal, not alarming, for both sides.

Program: The Library’s feedback entry covers giving and receiving it well.

## Receiving criticism at work
aka: handling criticism at work; taking feedback; defensive at work
see: Asking for feedback; Defensiveness as a feeling
go: /library/communication.html#feedback | Feedback (Library)
evidence: Research on feedback shows it often triggers defensiveness, and that separating the message from the delivery helps people use it.
try: “Thanks. Let me make sure I understand: the main issue is the summary, not the data?”

Criticism at work stings, especially when it’s vague or public. A useful approach: thank them, ask a clarifying question, and take time before responding in full.

You can also ask for criticism to come privately and with specific examples.

Program: WP-09 helps you weigh what you heard before reacting.

## Managing up
aka: managing up; working with your boss; managing your manager
see: One-on-ones; Decision rights at work
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: A common management idea: proactively understanding your manager’s priorities and communication style. Evidence is mostly practical.
try: “Would you prefer updates by email or in our one-on-one? And how much detail?”

Managing up means helping your working relationship with your manager go well: learning how they like to communicate, keeping them informed and raising problems early.

It isn’t flattery. It’s the same as tuning to anyone’s wiring: finding the signal they receive best.

Program: Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, works at work too. A Wiring Card can be shared with a manager.

## Handoff notes at work
aka: handoff notes; handover notes; shift handover; project handoff
see: Written recaps after a talk; Team working agreements
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Structured handoffs are widely used in healthcare, aviation and other fields to reduce errors. Their principles apply to any work handover.
try: “Done: X. In progress: Y (next step Z). Watch out for: the client prefers phone calls.”

When work passes from one person to another (a shift change, a vacation, a new owner), a short written handoff note prevents things falling through the cracks.

Good notes cover what’s done, what’s in progress, the next step and anything tricky.

Program: This is the same logic as clear handoffs in WP-03 and in co-parenting: clear owners and a clean handover.

## Disagree and commit
aka: disagree and commit; commit after disagreeing
see: Decision rights at work; Consensus and consent
go: /library/conflict.html#work-disagreements | Work disagreements (Library)
evidence: A management principle popularized by technology companies such as Intel and Amazon. It’s a practice, not a research finding, and works best when disagreement is genuinely heard first.
try: “I still think we should wait, but I’ve said my piece. I’m in, and I’ll give it my full effort.”

Disagree and commit means voicing your disagreement openly, and then, once a decision is made, supporting it fully rather than undermining it.

It works when people really are heard first, and when decisions can be revisited if new information appears.

Program: The Library’s work disagreements entry covers handling disagreements with colleagues.

## Status updates that help
aka: status update; progress update; async update
see: Handoff notes at work; Response time expectations
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: Practical guidance from project management. Clear, short updates reduce meetings and misunderstandings.
try: “Done this week: A, B. Next: C. Blocked on: D (need input from Sam by Thursday).”

A good status update is short and scannable: what’s done, what’s next, and what’s blocked. It lets others help and prevents surprises.

Regular updates are especially helpful in remote teams and for colleagues who prefer written communication.

Program: This is the workplace version of the daily check-in: small, regular, and no debate.

## Credit and recognition at work
aka: giving credit; taking credit; recognition at work
see: Status updates that help; Thanking for invisible work
go: /library/fairness.html#appreciation-and-recognition | Appreciation and recognition (Library)
evidence: Research links recognition at work with engagement and wellbeing. Credit disputes are common in collaborative work.
try: In a team update: “Priya did the analysis that made this possible.”

Giving specific, public credit for others’ work builds trust and makes invisible contributions visible. When credit is unclear, resentment grows quietly.

Written recaps and clear ownership help everyone see who did what.

Program: The Library’s appreciation entry and Pillar V (fairness and thanks keep things steady) apply at work too.

## Remote team connection
aka: remote work connection; hybrid teams; working from home team
see: Status updates that help; Texting norms that prevent fights
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: Research on remote work suggests people can feel more isolated and misread tone more often in text-heavy teams. Deliberate connection helps.
try: Start meetings with a quick round of “one good thing this week”.

Remote and hybrid teams lose the small, casual moments that build trust in an office. Messages carry less tone, so misunderstandings rise.

Small, deliberate rituals (quick check-ins, video for tricky topics, clear written norms) help replace what’s lost.

Program: The Signal Translator helps with messages that might be misread. Turning Toward’s small moments work with colleagues too.

## Workload conversations at work
aka: too much work; workload talk; at capacity at work
see: Boundaries at work; Decision rights at work
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Research on burnout links chronic workload imbalance with exhaustion. Raising workload early, with facts, is widely recommended.
try: “Here’s what’s on my plate this sprint. If this new project is a priority, which of these should move?”

When work piles up, a conversation with facts (a list of current tasks and their time) goes better than a general “I’m swamped”. It lets your manager help you prioritize.

Asking “what should move?” rather than “can I say no?” frames it as a shared problem.

Program: The Lemonade Stand works for teams of two to eight, and shows the split as a plain fact.
