# Words for feelings

## The emotion wheel
aka: emotion wheel; feelings wheel; feeling wheel; Plutchik wheel
see: Primary and secondary emotions; Feelings intensity scale; Mixed feelings
go: /library/emotions.html#emotional-granularity | Emotional granularity (Library)
evidence: Robert Plutchik proposed a wheel of emotions in 1980, and Gloria Willcox published “The Feeling Wheel” in 1982. They’re teaching tools, not settled science: researchers still debate how emotions should be categorized.
try: If “bad” is all you can find, look at the wheel: is it closer to sad, scared or angry? Then go one ring out: lonely, anxious, frustrated?

An emotion wheel arranges feeling words in rings: a few broad feelings in the middle (like happy, sad, angry, scared), with more specific words further out (like content, lonely, irritated, anxious). You start with the broad word and move outward to find a better fit.

It’s useful when you only have “fine”, “bad” or “stressed” to hand. More precise words help you understand what you need, and help others understand you.

Program: The Library’s entry on emotional granularity explains why precise feeling words help. WP-09 asks for “the feeling underneath”, and the wheel is a handy way to find it.

## Primary and secondary emotions
aka: primary emotions; secondary emotions; the feeling under the feeling
see: The emotion wheel; Anger as a signal; Hurt
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A common idea in emotion-focused approaches and everyday counseling. The exact split is debated by researchers, but the practical point (look underneath the first feeling) is widely used.
try: “I snapped because I was angry, but underneath I think I was scared we’d be late and it’d be my fault.”

Sometimes the first feeling we show isn’t the deepest one. Anger often sits on top of hurt, fear or embarrassment. Irritation can sit on top of exhaustion. The surface feeling is real, but the one underneath is often what needs attention.

Sharing the underneath feeling (“I felt left out”) usually invites care, while the surface one (“I’m furious”) tends to invite defense.

Program: WP-09 asks for the feeling underneath, like tired or unseen, before the fact and the ask.

## Feelings intensity scale
aka: intensity scale; one to ten feelings; how strong is it
see: The emotion wheel; Body clues of emotion
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: Rating intensity is used in many coaching and counseling settings. It’s a self-report tool, not a measurement, but it helps people notice change.
try: “I’m about a 7 out of 10 frustrated right now, so I’d like to wait before we talk.”

Rating a feeling from 1 to 10 turns a vague storm into something you can track and share. A 3 is “noticeable”; an 8 is “hard to think past”. Numbers can be easier to say than words, especially in a tense moment.

It also shows you when a feeling is fading, which it usually does, and helps you judge whether now is the time for a talk.

Program: Drift asks how strong a feeling is before it begins. Today’s Weather and WP-02 do a similar check on your overall load.

## Mixed feelings
aka: ambivalence; two feelings at once; conflicted
see: The emotion wheel; Relief
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: Research on mixed emotions shows people can feel positive and negative emotions at the same time, especially during transitions. It’s a normal experience.
try: “I’m relieved we decided, and also a bit sad. Both are true.”

It’s common to feel two things at once: proud and scared about a new job, relieved and guilty about saying no, happy for a friend and a little envious. Mixed feelings don’t mean you’re confused. They mean the situation matters in more than one way.

Naming both, without rushing to pick one, often makes them easier to hold.

Program: Know your own wiring helps you sort where a feeling comes from: your wiring, a learned pattern, or today’s conditions.

## Body clues of emotion
aka: body signals; where I feel it; physical signs of feelings
see: Feelings intensity scale; Stress versus pressure
go: /wp-11.html | WP-11: The Calm-Down Kit
evidence: Research on interoception (sensing your body’s internal state) suggests people vary a lot in how well they notice body signals. Noticing can be practiced.
try: “My jaw is tight and my shoulders are up. I’m more wound up than I thought.”

Emotions often show up in the body before we have words: a tight chest, a hot face, a clenched jaw, a sinking stomach, restless legs. Learning your own early signs gives you a head start to pause before a reaction takes over.

Everyone’s map is different. Some people notice very little in their body, especially when stressed; that’s okay, and it can be practiced gently.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit asks where you feel it first. The Library’s entry on interoception goes deeper.

## Frustration
aka: frustrated; fed up; exasperated
see: Anger as a signal; Impatience; Irritation
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: Psychologists generally describe frustration as a response to a blocked goal. It’s among the most common everyday emotions.
try: “I’m frustrated because I’ve asked about this three times, and I want us to find a way that works.”

Frustration is the feeling of something getting in the way of what you want or expect: a task that won’t work, a plan that keeps changing, a request that keeps getting missed. It often builds with repetition.

Frustration is useful information. It points to a need or goal that matters to you. Naming the goal (“I want the evenings to feel calmer”) often turns frustration into a request.

Program: Repeated frustration about the same thing is a sign to look at the setup: the monthly look-back (WP-04) is designed for exactly that.

## Disappointment
aka: disappointed; let down; letdown
see: Frustration; Hurt
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Disappointment is widely studied as a reaction to unmet expectations. It’s a normal part of close relationships.
try: “I was really looking forward to Saturday, so I’m disappointed. Can we find another day?”

Disappointment comes when something we hoped for doesn’t happen. It’s often quieter than anger, and easier to hide, which can let it pile up unspoken.

Saying it simply and without blame (“I’m disappointed, I was looking forward to it”) is honest and usually lands softly. It also helps others understand what matters to you.

Program: If disappointment keeps coming from the same source (like plans falling through), look at the setup: a shared calendar, clearer owners, or a check-in on expectations.

## Irritation
aka: irritated; annoyed; annoyance; grumpy
see: Frustration; Hungry, tired and snappy
go: /workpapers/wp-02-battery-stress-meter.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Everyday irritability is strongly linked with tiredness, hunger and stress in research on mood. Small annoyances land bigger when you’re depleted.
try: “I’m irritable today, and it’s not about you. Let me get some food and I’ll be better company.”

Irritation is a low-level, prickly annoyance. On its own it’s small. When your battery is low, though, small annoyances stack up quickly and can come out sharper than you mean.

Noticing “I’m just irritable today” can stop you from turning a general mood into a complaint about someone.

Program: WP-02 separates how much you’re already carrying from how upset you are about one thing. Irritation is often mostly the first.

## Resentment
aka: resentful; resent; bitterness; silent resentment
see: Frustration; Kinkeeping; Thanking for invisible work
go: /book/preface.html | Preface: The work nobody sees
evidence: Relationship research links resentment with perceived unfairness, especially in unpaid household work. It tends to grow when grievances go unspoken.
try: “I’ve been quietly resentful about always doing the planning. I don’t want to be, so I want to talk about it.”

Resentment is a slow-building feeling that something unfair keeps happening, often without being acknowledged. It tends to grow in silence: each unmentioned unfairness adds a little more.

It’s best treated as information, not as a character flaw. It usually points to invisible work, an unbalanced setup or a need that hasn’t been said out loud. Saying it early, and kindly, stops it hardening.

Program: The Preface calls the pile of unseen work “unbilled debt”. The Lemonade Stand and WP-01 help make it visible before it turns into resentment.

## Hurt
aka: hurt feelings; feeling hurt; wounded
see: Primary and secondary emotions; Disappointment
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Research on hurt feelings links them to feeling devalued by someone who matters to us. Hurt often sits underneath anger.
try: “When you laughed at my idea in front of everyone, I felt hurt. I don’t think you meant it that way.”

Hurt is the pain of feeling let down, dismissed or not valued by someone you care about. Because it feels vulnerable, many people show anger or silence instead.

Saying “I felt hurt” is often more effective than saying “you were rude”, because it invites the other person to care rather than to defend.

Program: The Check-ins guide suggests saying one fact and how it landed, then letting them say it back.

## Embarrassment
aka: embarrassed; mortified; awkward
see: Spotlight feelings after a mistake; Hurt
go: /library/thinking.html#spotlight-effect | The spotlight effect (Library)
evidence: Research on the spotlight effect (Gilovich and colleagues, 2000) found people overestimate how much others notice their mistakes and appearance.
try: “That was embarrassing. Most people probably forgot it by lunch.”

Embarrassment is the hot, awkward feeling of having been seen doing something clumsy or wrong. It’s strong but usually short-lived.

It helps to remember that others notice our slips much less than we think. A light acknowledgment (“well, that was awkward!”) often ends it faster than hiding.

Program: The Library’s spotlight effect entry explains why embarrassment feels bigger inside than it looks outside.

## Spotlight feelings after a mistake
aka: after a mistake; I messed up in public; replaying a mistake
see: Embarrassment; Self-forgiveness
go: /library/thinking.html#spotlight-effect | The spotlight effect (Library)
evidence: Based on the spotlight-effect research and on work on rumination. Replaying a mistake rarely changes it.
try: Write one line: what happened, what you’d do differently, and one small repair. Then close the notebook.

After a public mistake, it’s natural to replay it and imagine everyone else is too. Usually they aren’t. The replay is mostly happening in your own head.

A useful move is to turn the replay into one practical step (an apology, a fix, a note for next time) and then give yourself permission to stop.

Program: If replaying becomes a habit, the Library’s rumination entry and the Calm-Down Kit can help settle it.

## Envy
aka: envious; envy versus jealousy; comparing
see: Social media and comparison at home; Mixed feelings
go: /library/life.html#social-comparison | Social comparison (Library)
evidence: Researchers distinguish envy (wanting what someone else has) from jealousy (fearing losing someone to a rival). Both are common and normal.
try: “I felt envious when I saw their holiday photos. Maybe I just need a break too.”

Envy is wanting something another person has: their time off, their job, their calm household. It’s a normal feeling, and it can point to something you want more of.

It becomes a problem when it turns into resentment of the other person. Asking “what does this tell me I want?” turns envy into direction.

Program: The Library’s social comparison entry explains why comparison often makes us feel worse. The Library also has a separate entry on jealousy.

## Anxiety and fear
aka: fear versus anxiety; anxious; scared; afraid
see: Worry versus problem-solving; Dread
go: /wp-11.html | WP-11: The Calm-Down Kit
evidence: Psychologists often describe fear as a response to a present threat and anxiety as a response to a possible future one. This is general information, not a diagnosis.
try: “I’m anxious about the talk tonight. Can we agree to keep it to one topic and take a break if it gets heavy?”

Fear tends to be about something here and now. Anxiety tends to be about what might happen. Both show up in the body: a racing heart, tight chest, restlessness.

Everyday anxiety before a hard conversation is very normal, and a bit of preparation helps. If anxiety is intense, frequent or getting in the way of life, it’s worth talking with a qualified professional.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit and the Night Garden’s box breathing are gentle ways to settle before a hard talk.

## Worry versus problem-solving
aka: worry versus planning; productive worry; worrying
see: Anxiety and fear; Dread; Worry time
go: /library/emotions.html#rumination | Rumination (Library)
evidence: Research on worry distinguishes repetitive worrying from constructive problem-solving. Turning worries into specific next steps is a common technique in cognitive approaches.
try: Ask: “Is there something I can do about this today?” If yes, write the step. If no, give the worry a time slot later.

Worry and problem-solving can feel similar, but they’re different. Problem-solving ends in a next step. Worry circles, asking “what if?” without landing.

A simple test helps: can I act on this now? If so, pick one step. If not, set it aside on purpose, perhaps with a note, so your mind doesn’t have to keep holding it.

Program: The Library’s rumination entry goes deeper. Writing it down is also the idea behind the parking lot in check-ins.

## Dread
aka: dreading; sense of dread; dreading a conversation
see: Anxiety and fear; A simple agenda for a hard talk
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Research on anticipation suggests people often dread upcoming unpleasant events more than the events themselves turn out to warrant. Waiting can be the hardest part.
try: “I’ve been dreading this talk, so I’d like to just get it started. Can we do ten minutes now?”

Dread is the heavy feeling of anticipating something unpleasant: a difficult talk, a family gathering, a bill. It often grows the longer something is put off.

Shrinking the event (a short, scheduled talk with one topic) and preparing one opening sentence usually makes it more manageable than the dread suggested.

Program: The Signal Translator lets you test your opening line, which takes a lot of the dread out of starting.

## Overwhelm as a feeling
aka: feeling overwhelmed; overwhelm; swamped
see: Irritation; Energy audit
go: /workpapers/wp-02-battery-stress-meter.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Overwhelm isn’t a technical term, but it describes something research on stress calls high demands relative to resources.
try: “I’m overwhelmed. Can we list what’s on my plate and see what can move?”

Overwhelm is the feeling that there’s more than you can handle: too many tasks, too much noise, too many decisions. It often makes it harder to start anything, which adds to the pile.

It’s usually a signal about load, not about your ability. Getting everything out of your head onto paper, and choosing one thing, is often the first way through.

Program: The Lemonade Stand and WP-01 help you see the whole load; WP-02 helps you notice how full your battery is.

## Helplessness
aka: helpless; powerless; nothing I do matters
see: Small wins at home; Overwhelm as a feeling
go: /library/motivation.html#small-wins | Small wins (Library)
evidence: Research on perceived control suggests that feeling in control of even small things supports wellbeing. Persistent helplessness is worth talking to a professional about.
try: “I can’t fix the whole thing, but I can decide what we have for dinner tonight.”

Helplessness is the feeling that nothing you do will make a difference. It often grows when problems repeat and efforts seem to go nowhere.

Finding one small thing within your control, and doing it, can gently push back against that feeling. Small wins add up. If helplessness is heavy or lasting, please reach out to someone you trust or a qualified professional.

Program: The Library’s small wins entry explains why tiny progress matters. WP-04 helps you see that a repeating problem is the setup, not you.

## Insecurity
aka: insecure; feeling insecure; not good enough feeling
see: Self-talk; Reassurance that helps
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: A broad everyday word covering self-doubt and worry about acceptance. Research on self-esteem and attachment both touch on it.
try: “I’m feeling a bit insecure today. A quick ‘we’re good’ would really help.”

Insecurity is doubt about whether you’re good enough, liked enough or safe in a relationship. Everyone feels it sometimes, especially when tired, stressed or in new situations.

Naming it simply, and asking for what would help, is often more effective than testing people or looking for hidden signs.

Program: Know your own wiring helps you separate a lasting pattern from how today is going. The Library covers attachment and self-esteem in depth.

## Reassurance that helps
aka: reassurance; asking for reassurance; how to reassure someone
see: Insecurity; Anxiety and fear
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: Research suggests reassurance is comforting, but repeated reassurance-seeking can sometimes keep anxiety going. Balance and specific, warm responses help.
try: Instead of “Stop worrying,” try “I’m here, and we’re okay. What’s on your mind?”

Good reassurance is warm, specific and honest: “I’m not angry, I’m just tired,” or “I love you, and I want to sort this out together.” It speaks to the actual worry.

If someone needs reassurance very often about the same thing, it may help to gently talk about the worry itself, or what would help them feel steadier, rather than repeating the same words.

Program: Turning Toward’s small moments of connection are a steady, everyday form of reassurance.

## Contentment
aka: content; contented; satisfied; at ease
see: Relief; Joy
go: /library/connection.html#savoring | Savoring (Library)
evidence: Research on positive emotions treats contentment as a low-energy, pleasant state. It tends to get overlooked because it’s quiet.
try: “This is nice. Just sitting here with a cup of tea.”

Contentment is a calm sense that things are okay right now. It’s quieter than joy and easy to miss, especially in busy lives focused on the next problem.

Noticing and naming contentment, even for a moment, can make ordinary good times feel fuller.

Program: The Library’s savoring entry explains how lingering on good moments helps. The Night Garden is a place to practice a few minutes of calm.

## Relief
aka: relieved; weight off my shoulders
see: Mixed feelings; Contentment
go: /workpapers/wp-13-pll-protocol.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: Relief is widely described in emotion research as the feeling after a threat or burden passes.
try: “I’m so relieved we sorted out the schedule. Thank you.”

Relief is the release when a worry passes or a burden is lifted. It’s worth saying out loud, because it tells the other person their effort mattered.

Relief sometimes comes mixed with other feelings, like tiredness or even sadness. That’s normal.

Program: Sharing relief and thanks fits Pillar V: appreciation keeps things steady. The daily check-in has a line for one thing you appreciated.

## Pride
aka: proud; feeling proud; healthy pride
see: Small wins at home; Joy
go: /library/motivation.html#small-wins | Small wins (Library)
evidence: Researchers distinguish pride in effort or achievement from arrogant pride. The first is linked with motivation and wellbeing.
try: “I’m proud of us for sticking with the daily check-in for a whole month.”

Pride in something you worked for is a healthy feeling. It helps you notice progress and keep going.

Shared pride (“we did that”) is especially good for relationships, households and teams. It builds a sense of being on the same side.

Program: Noticing progress over weeks is what REPORT-01 is for: your record, so progress builds instead of starting over.

## Joy
aka: joyful; happiness in the moment; delight
see: Contentment; Amusement
go: /library/connection.html#savoring | Savoring (Library)
evidence: Research on positive emotions suggests they broaden attention and help build relationships over time, though the size of these effects is debated.
try: “That made my day. Look at this!”

Joy is a bright, lively feeling of delight. It often comes from small things: a good joke, a message from a friend, a sunny morning.

Sharing joy is a form of connection. When someone shares good news or a delight with you, responding with warmth and interest strengthens the bond.

Program: The Library’s entry on capitalization covers responding to good news. Turning Toward is full of small ways to share joy.

## Hope
aka: hopeful; optimism; looking forward
see: Joy; Small wins at home
go: /five-pillars.html | The Five Pillars
evidence: Research on hope (for example, C. R. Snyder’s work) describes it as having goals plus ways to reach them. It’s linked with coping in many studies.
try: “It’s hard now, but I think if we try the new setup for a month, it could get easier.”

Hope is believing things can get better and seeing at least one way there. It isn’t the same as ignoring problems.

In relationships, hope often comes from a concrete plan: one change to try, a time to look back. Hope grows when small plans work.

Program: The program’s whole approach is hopeful: problems usually live in the setup, and setups can be changed.

## Amusement
aka: amused; laughter; finding it funny
see: Joy; Humor that repairs
go: /library/communication.html#humour | Humor (Library)
evidence: Research suggests shared laughter can ease tension and build closeness, though humor at someone’s expense has the opposite effect.
try: When a small tension breaks into a shared laugh, let it. It’s a good sign.

Amusement is the light feeling of finding something funny. Shared amusement is one of the easiest ways to feel close.

Gentle humor can defuse a tense moment, but only if it’s shared, not aimed at the other person.

Program: The Library’s humor entry explains the difference between humor that connects and humor that stings.

## Humor that repairs
aka: repair humor; joking to make up; breaking tension with a joke
see: Amusement; Anatomy of an apology
go: /library/relationships.html#repair-attempts | Repair attempts (Library)
evidence: Relationship research counts humor among common repair attempts. Whether it works depends on timing and whether both people are in on the joke.
try: After a silly argument about the thermostat: “Okay, so we’re arguing about two degrees. Truce?”

A light joke, a silly face or a shared reference can signal “we’re okay” in the middle of a tense moment. It works best when the tension is mild and the humor is about the situation, not the person.

If the other person is really hurt, humor can feel dismissive. Then a sincere word works better.

Program: The Library’s repair-attempts entry covers the many small ways people signal “let’s not fight”.

## Impatience
aka: impatient; can’t wait; hurry
see: Frustration; Different paces
go: /tools/frequency-calibration.html | Frequency Calibration
evidence: Impatience is closely tied to differences in pace and expectations. The program’s Frequency Framework treats pace mismatch as a common source of friction.
try: “I’m getting impatient, but I know you like to think things through. When could you give me an answer?”

Impatience is the tense feeling of wanting something to happen faster. Between people, it often comes from different paces: one person decides fast, the other slowly.

Naming the pace difference, and agreeing a time, usually helps more than pushing.

Program: Frequency Calibration maps your natural pace in areas like decisions and money, and suggests agreements to close the gap.

## Defensiveness as a feeling
aka: feeling defensive; getting defensive; defensive reaction
see: Hurt; Owning your part
go: /library/relationships.html#four-horsemen | The four horsemen (Library)
evidence: Relationship research lists defensiveness as a common pattern in escalating conflict. Taking some responsibility is the usual antidote.
try: Notice the urge to say “but I…”, pause, and try: “You’re right that I forgot. Let me fix it.”

Feeling defensive is a natural response to feeling criticized or blamed. The body tenses, and the mind rushes to explain, justify or counter-attack.

Noticing the defensive urge, and taking a breath before responding, gives you a chance to hear the need underneath the criticism. Owning even a small part lowers the heat for both people.

Program: WP-09 asks: are you answering their words, or an old argument? That question is a good pause for defensiveness.

## Feeling flat
aka: flat; numb; empty; checked out
see: Body clues of emotion; Helplessness
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: Feeling flat or far away is a common response to stress and exhaustion. Persistent numbness or low mood is worth talking to a qualified professional about.
try: “I’m feeling a bit flat today. Not upset with anyone, just low on everything.”

Sometimes, instead of feeling a lot, you feel very little: flat, far away, hard to get going. It’s often a sign of being depleted rather than of not caring.

Telling others (“I’m flat today, it’s not about you”) prevents them from reading your quietness as distance or anger. If the flatness lasts a long time, please talk with someone you trust or a professional.

Program: Today’s Weather calls this “fogged in”: running on empty. It suggests what today is good for, and what to leave until the fog lifts.

## Anger as a signal
aka: anger signal; what anger tells you; using anger well
see: Frustration; Primary and secondary emotions
go: /library/emotions.html#anger | Anger (Library)
evidence: Emotion researchers often describe anger as a response to perceived unfairness or blocked goals. How it’s expressed matters more for relationships than whether it’s felt.
try: “I’m angry because I think this is unfair. Let me cool down, and then I want to talk about the split.”

Anger often points to something that feels unfair, disrespectful or blocked. The feeling itself is information. What you do with it is what matters.

Pausing until the intensity drops, then naming the unfairness clearly and calmly, usually gets further than acting in the heat.
