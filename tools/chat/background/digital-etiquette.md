# Digital communication etiquette

## Don’t fight by text
aka: fighting over text; arguing by text; texting arguments
see: When to switch to voice; Cooling off before replying
go: /conversation-reader.html | The Conversation Reader
evidence: Research on text communication suggests tone is often misread and negative intent is overestimated. Many relationship experts advise against resolving conflict by text.
try: “I don’t want to argue over text. Can we talk tonight?”

Text strips out tone, timing and facial expression, so disagreements escalate easily. Each person fills the gaps with their worst guess, and replies get sharper.

Moving hard conversations to voice or in person, and keeping texts for logistics and warmth, prevents a lot of misunderstandings.

## When to switch to voice
aka: call instead of text; move to a call; switch channels
see: Don’t fight by text; Choosing the channel
go: /carrier-wave-decoder.html | The Carrier Wave Decoder
evidence: Communication research suggests voice carries emotional cues that text lacks. Some people prefer text for hard topics, so ask.
try: “This thread is getting tangled. Quick call?”

Signs it’s time to switch from text to a call: the thread is getting longer and sharper, you’re rereading messages for hidden meaning, or feelings are involved.

A two-minute call often clears up what twenty messages couldn’t. If the other person prefers writing, a calm, longer message can work too.

## Texting norms that prevent fights
aka: texting rules; texting etiquette; text norms
see: Don’t fight by text; Tone indicators
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Practical guidance. People have very different texting habits, and mismatched expectations are a common source of friction.
try: Agree a few basics: “A thumbs-up means yes, not annoyed. No serious topics by text.”

People text very differently: some are brief, some use lots of emoji, some reply instantly, some once a day. Without shared norms, brief replies can seem cold and slow replies can seem like ignoring.

Talking about texting habits openly, and agreeing a few basics, removes a lot of guesswork.

## Response time expectations
aka: reply time; slow replies; left on read; not replying
see: Texting norms that prevent fights; Notifications and boundaries
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: Research on messaging suggests people often interpret slow replies negatively, even though delays usually have ordinary causes.
try: “I often can’t reply during work. No reply means busy, not upset.”

Different people have very different expectations about how quickly messages should be answered. A slow reply is usually about a busy day, not about you.

Saying your usual response pattern out loud (“I check messages at lunch and in the evening”) prevents worry and hurt.

## Read receipts
aka: read receipts; seen but no reply; left on read
see: Response time expectations; Texting norms that prevent fights
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: A common source of anxiety in messaging. Research on read receipts is limited, but people report reading meaning into them.
try: Decide together whether you use read receipts, and agree that “seen” doesn’t mean “ignoring you”.

Read receipts show when a message has been seen, which can create pressure to reply instantly and worry when there’s no reply.

Some people find them reassuring; others find them stressful. Agreeing on them (or turning them off) is a small kindness.

## Group chat norms
aka: group chat rules; group chat etiquette; muting group chats
see: Family group chats; Don’t fight by text
go: /conversation-reader.html | The Conversation Reader
evidence: Practical guidance. Group chats combine many readers and little tone, which makes misunderstandings more likely.
try: “This group is for plans and photos. Anything personal, let’s take it to a private message.”

Group chats work best with a clear purpose (planning, sharing news) and a shared understanding that muting is fine and not everyone needs to reply.

Disagreements in group chats tend to escalate because everyone watches. Taking them private helps.

## Emails that get read
aka: clear emails; email subject lines; writing clear emails
see: Status updates that help; One ask at a time
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: Practical guidance from workplace communication. Short, clear emails with one request tend to get faster, more accurate replies.
try: Subject: “Decision needed by Friday: venue for team lunch.” Body: two options and your recommendation.

Emails get read and answered faster when the subject line says what’s needed, the request comes first, and there’s one clear ask.

Long emails with the request buried at the end often get missed.

## Notifications and boundaries
aka: notifications; turning off notifications; always on
see: Response time expectations; Boundaries at work
go: /library/calm.html#phones-and-presence | Phones and presence (Library)
evidence: Research on interruptions suggests frequent notifications can fragment attention. Effects vary by person and context.
try: Turn off non-urgent notifications during dinner and after 9 p.m.

Constant notifications pull attention away from the people and tasks in front of you. Setting quiet times, and telling people when you’re offline, protects focus and presence.

It’s a boundary, and it’s okay to have it.

## Location sharing and consent
aka: location sharing; tracking location; sharing location
see: Kinds of boundaries; Posting about others online
go: /legal/privacy-policy.html | Privacy policy (this site)
evidence: A common modern discussion topic. The healthiest arrangements are based on mutual, freely given agreement.
try: “Would you be comfortable sharing locations when we travel? Totally fine if not.”

Sharing locations can feel reassuring for some couples, families and friends, and intrusive for others. It works best when it’s mutual, freely agreed, and easy to change.

It shouldn’t be required as proof of trust.

## Posting about others online
aka: posting photos of others; sharing online; asking before posting
see: Location sharing and consent; Kinds of boundaries
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: A common modern etiquette topic. People’s comfort with being posted about varies widely.
try: “Mind if I post this photo? Happy not to.”

Not everyone likes being posted about online. Asking before posting photos or stories about others (including kids, as they get older) respects their privacy.

It’s a small courtesy that can prevent a lot of hurt.

## Voice notes etiquette
aka: voice notes; voice messages; audio messages
see: Texting norms that prevent fights; When to switch to voice
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Practical guidance. Preferences vary: some people love voice notes, others find them hard to listen to.
try: “Do you prefer voice notes or texts? I’ll send whichever’s easier for you.”

Voice notes carry tone better than text, which can help with warmth. But long voice notes can be hard for busy listeners, and some people process text more easily.

Asking which someone prefers is simple and kind.

## The pause before sending
aka: pause before sending; send delay; draft first
see: Cooling off before replying; Don’t fight by text
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: Practical advice. Emotion typically fades with time, and rereading a draft often reveals a harsher tone than intended.
try: Write it, wait five minutes, reread it as if you were receiving it, then decide.

A short pause before sending an emotional message gives you a chance to read it as the other person will. Many messages get softened, shortened or deleted in those few minutes.

Some email apps let you delay sending. A draft folder works too.

## Social media and comparison at home
aka: comparing to other couples; instagram families; comparison online
see: Envy; Notifications and boundaries
go: /library/life.html#social-comparison | Social comparison (Library)
evidence: Research on social comparison and social media suggests seeing curated highlights of others’ lives can lower satisfaction for some people. Findings vary, and effects are often modest.
try: “Their photos are the best five minutes of their week. Ours looks different, and that’s okay.”

Online, other households often look tidier, calmer and happier than yours. What you’re seeing is a highlight reel, not the whole week, including the invisible work behind it.

Noticing when scrolling leaves you feeling worse about your home or relationship is useful. A break, or following fewer accounts that spark comparison, can help.
