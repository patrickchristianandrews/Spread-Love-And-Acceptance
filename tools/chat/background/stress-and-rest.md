# Stress, rest and sleep basics

General, everyday information only. Not health or medical advice.

## Stress versus pressure
aka: stress and pressure; good stress; helpful stress
see: Everyday stress spillover; Recovery after a hard day
go: /workpapers/wp-02-battery-stress-meter.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Research on stress (including the older Yerkes-Dodson idea) suggests moderate arousal can help performance while too much hurts it. The details are debated, but the general shape is widely accepted.
try: “A deadline this week is fine pressure. A deadline plus a sick kid plus no sleep is too much.”

A bit of pressure can help you focus: a deadline, a goal, a challenge you care about. Stress becomes a problem when demands keep outrunning what you have to meet them, especially for a long time without recovery.

The same event can feel like healthy pressure one week and overwhelming stress the next, depending on what else you’re carrying. That’s why checking your overall load matters.

Program: WP-02 separates what you’re already carrying from what just happened. The Library’s stress entries go deeper.

## Everyday stress spillover
aka: spillover; bringing work stress home; stress spill
see: Stress versus pressure; Transition rituals after work
go: /library/life.html#work-life | Work and home (Library)
evidence: Research on work-family spillover shows stress from one area often carries into another, including into how partners treat each other at home.
try: “Rough day at work. Give me fifteen minutes to reset, and then I’m all yours.”

Stress from one part of life often leaks into another. A hard day at work can come out as snapping at home; a tense morning at home can color the whole workday.

Noticing spillover (“this isn’t about you, it’s the day I had”) helps protect the people around you, and helps them not take it personally.

## Transition rituals after work
aka: transition ritual; decompression; coming home ritual; switching off
see: Everyday stress spillover; Recovery after a hard day
go: /soundscapes.html | The soundscapes
evidence: Research on psychological detachment from work suggests switching off in the evening is linked with better recovery. Specific rituals are personal choices rather than tested methods.
try: A ten-minute walk, one song in the car, changing clothes, or a cup of tea before joining the household.

A transition ritual is a small, regular routine that marks the switch from one role to another, like work to home. It gives your body a chance to settle before you walk into new demands.

It doesn’t need to be long. The point is a clear signal to yourself, and ideally an agreement with others (“give me ten minutes when I get in”).

## Recovery after a hard day
aka: recovery; rest and recovery; recharging
see: Micro-breaks; Transition rituals after work
go: /night-garden.html | The Night Garden
evidence: Research on recovery from work stress points to four helpful experiences: detaching mentally, relaxing, doing something absorbing, and feeling in control of your free time.
try: Pick one thing tonight that is truly restful for you: a walk, a game, a bath, a call with a friend.

Rest isn’t just stopping. Real recovery tends to come from switching off mentally, relaxing your body, getting absorbed in something different, and having some say over your own time.

What recovers you is personal. Some people recharge alone, others with company; some with quiet, others with movement.

Program: Frequency Calibration includes rest and recovery as one of the five rhythms. The Night Garden and Pause & Play are made for small recovery moments.

## Micro-breaks
aka: short breaks; micro breaks; mini breaks
see: Recovery after a hard day; Energy audit
go: /library/calm.html#breaks | Breaks (Library)
evidence: Research on short breaks during work finds modest benefits for energy and wellbeing, with effects varying by person and task.
try: Every hour or so, stand up, look out a window, and take three slow breaths.

Micro-breaks are tiny pauses of a minute or a few minutes during a busy stretch: stretching, a glass of water, looking at something far away, a few slow breaths.

They won’t fix an overloaded week, but they can keep the battery from draining quite as fast.

Program: The Library’s breaks entry goes deeper. A few rounds of box breathing in the Night Garden make a good micro-break.

## Worry time
aka: scheduled worry; worry window; worry period
see: Worry versus problem-solving; A wind-down routine
go: /library/emotions.html#rumination | Rumination (Library)
evidence: “Worry postponement” is a technique used in cognitive approaches to worry, with some research support. It helps some people more than others.
try: Jot worries on a note during the day, then spend ten minutes at 5 p.m. looking at them and choosing next steps.

Worry time means setting aside a short, fixed slot each day for worrying, and gently postponing worries to it when they pop up earlier. Many people find worries feel smaller by the time the slot arrives.

It pairs well with writing things down: once a worry is on paper with a time, your mind doesn’t have to keep holding it.

Program: The parking lot idea from Check-ins is similar: a topic waits safely until its time.

## A wind-down routine
aka: wind down; bedtime routine for adults; evening routine
see: Worry time; Sleep and irritability at home
go: /soundscapes.html | The soundscapes
evidence: Sleep researchers commonly recommend a regular, relaxing pre-sleep routine as part of general good sleep habits. This is general information, not medical advice.
try: Same rough time each night: dim the lights, put the phone away, a few pages of a book or a calm soundscape.

A wind-down routine is a predictable set of calming steps before bed. Doing the same few things in the same order signals to your body that the day is ending.

It’s also a good time not to start hard conversations. Late-night talks often go badly simply because everyone is tired. If sleep problems last or affect daily life, it’s worth talking to a qualified professional.

Program: The soundscapes and the Night Garden are gentle options for winding down. Pillar III suggests saving big topics for a better time.

## Sleep and irritability at home
aka: tired and snappy; short sleep and mood; sleep debt
see: A wind-down routine; Hungry, tired and snappy
go: /library/stress.html#sleep-and-mood | Sleep and mood (Library)
evidence: Research consistently links short sleep with worse mood and more conflict in relationships. This is general information, not medical advice.
try: “I slept badly, so I might be short today. Let’s keep the big stuff for tomorrow.”

After a short night, most people are more irritable, less patient and quicker to read things as negative. Couples and families often argue more after bad sleep.

Knowing this helps in two ways: go easier on yourself and others after a rough night, and avoid scheduling big decisions or hard talks then.

## Hungry, tired and snappy
aka: hangry; HALT; hungry angry lonely tired
see: Sleep and irritability at home; Irritation
go: /library/stress.html#halt | HALT (Library)
evidence: HALT (hungry, angry, lonely, tired) is a popular self-check from recovery communities. Research on hunger and mood suggests low blood sugar can affect irritability, though effects vary.
try: Before a hard talk, check: have I eaten? Slept? Had a moment alone? If not, sort that first.

A lot of snapping comes from basic needs, not the topic at hand. Being hungry, tired, lonely or already wound up lowers patience for everyone.

A quick check of basic needs before a hard conversation can prevent a lot of avoidable friction. Sometimes the best first move is a sandwich.

Program: The Library covers HALT and hangry in depth. Today’s Weather asks about body needs like hunger and thirst.

## Movement breaks
aka: moving to reset; walk it off; physical activity for mood
see: Micro-breaks; Walk and talk for hard topics
go: /wp-11.html | WP-11: The Calm-Down Kit
evidence: Research generally finds physical activity is linked with better mood. This is general information, not medical or exercise advice.
try: When you’re too wound up to talk, a brisk ten-minute walk around the block before coming back.

Moving your body, even briefly, can help shift a tense or flat state: a short walk, stretching, dancing to one song, carrying laundry up the stairs.

A slow walk is one of the calming steps suggested in the Calm-Down Kit for heated moments.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) asks you to pick two calming steps in advance; movement is a common choice.

## Walk and talk for hard topics
aka: walking meeting; talk while walking; walk and talk
see: Talking side by side; Movement breaks
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Walking side by side is often recommended by therapists and coaches. There’s limited formal research, but it combines movement with lower eye-contact pressure.
try: “Want to talk about the holiday plans on a walk after dinner?”

Walking together while you talk combines two helpful things: gentle movement, which can settle the body, and side-by-side positioning, which can make hard topics feel less confrontational.

It works well for planning conversations and for people who find sitting face to face intense. Keep it private enough to speak freely.

Program: Check-ins say to choose a good time and place. A quiet walk can be one.

## Noise, light and stress
aka: sensory stress; noisy home; too much noise
see: Sensory-friendly spaces for talks; Micro-breaks
go: /library/stress.html#sensory-load | Sensory load (Library)
evidence: Research suggests noise and crowding can raise stress for many people, and much more for some sensory-sensitive people. Individual differences are large.
try: Before a hard talk, turn off the TV, dim harsh lights and step away from the busy kitchen.

A loud, bright, busy environment drains the battery, sometimes without you noticing until you’re snapping. People differ a lot: some barely notice, others are worn out quickly.

Small changes (a quieter room, softer light, headphones) can make a big difference to how a conversation goes.

## Energy audit
aka: energy audit; what drains me; what energizes me
see: Recovery after a hard day; Overwhelm as a feeling
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: A practical coaching exercise, not a research tool. It helps people notice patterns they might miss.
try: For one week, mark each activity with a plus (gave energy) or a minus (drained it). Look for patterns.

An energy audit is a simple look at what fills your battery and what drains it. Some tasks are draining for everyone; others drain some people and energize others.

Seeing your own pattern helps you plan: put draining tasks when you’re fresher, protect recovery time, and swap jobs with people who mind them less.

Program: Today’s Weather keeps a private almanac so you can spot your own patterns. WP-03 is where you might swap draining jobs.

## Sunday evening dread
aka: Sunday scaries; Sunday night anxiety; start of the week dread
see: Dread; Weekly planning together
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: A popular term rather than a scientific one. Anticipating a busy week often feels worse than the week itself.
try: Spend fifteen minutes on Sunday afternoon looking at the week together, then close the calendar and do something nice.

Many people feel a dip on Sunday evenings as the new week looms. It’s often driven by uncertainty about what’s coming and unfinished tasks from the week before.

A short planning moment earlier in the day, plus something enjoyable after, can take the edge off.
