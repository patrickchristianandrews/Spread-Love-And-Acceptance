# Co-parenting logistics

Practical, general guidance. A court order or parenting plan always comes first.

## Parallel parenting
aka: parallel parenting; low contact co-parenting; separate parenting
see: BIFF responses; Co-parenting calendar
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#co-parents | Where co-parents start
evidence: Parallel parenting is a recognized approach for high-conflict separations, described by family courts and practitioners. It aims to reduce contact and conflict between parents.
try: Each home keeps its own routines; communication is written, brief and only about the kids’ logistics.

In parallel parenting, each parent runs their own home with little direct coordination, and communication is kept brief, written and focused on logistics. It’s often used when direct cooperation keeps leading to conflict.

It isn’t a failure. It can protect children from ongoing tension, and some families move toward more cooperation over time.

Program: The co-parent road keeps messages short and factual, with one owner per task.

## BIFF responses
aka: BIFF; brief informative friendly firm; replying to a hostile message
see: Parallel parenting; Don’t fight by text
go: /workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: BIFF (Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm) was developed by Bill Eddy of the High Conflict Institute. It’s a practical method widely used in family law settings; formal research is limited.
try: “Thanks for letting me know. I’ll have Sam ready at 5 on Friday. Have a good week.”

A BIFF response is a reply to a hostile or emotional message that’s Brief, Informative, Friendly and Firm. It gives the needed information, ignores the bait, and doesn’t invite more argument.

It’s especially useful for co-parents, but works with anyone whose messages tend to escalate.

Program: WP-09 turns a raw reaction into fact, feeling and ask. For co-parents, the fact and the ask are often enough.

## Co-parenting calendar
aka: shared calendar; custody calendar; parenting schedule
see: Transition bags; Schedule change protocol
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Shared calendars are widely recommended by family mediators to reduce conflict over schedules. Evidence is practical.
try: One shared calendar with the kids’ schedule, activities, appointments and who has them each day.

A shared calendar gives both homes the same picture of the children’s schedule: custody days, school events, appointments and activities. It reduces “you didn’t tell me” arguments.

Keep it factual, update it promptly, and agree who adds what.

## Transition bags
aka: transition bag; kids’ bag between homes; what goes back and forth
see: Co-parenting calendar; Handoffs between homes
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#co-parents | Where co-parents start
evidence: Practical advice from family educators. Having duplicates of basics in each home is commonly recommended to reduce stress.
try: Keep toothbrushes, pajamas and basics in both homes; the bag carries school things, medicines and one comfort item.

Moving between homes is easier when kids don’t have to pack everything each time. Duplicates of basics in both homes, and a short checklist for what travels, cut down on forgotten items and tense texts.

Let children have a say in what comes with them, especially comfort items.

Program: A checklist with an owner (WP-03) helps: who packs, who checks.

## Handoffs between homes
aka: exchanges; custody exchange; drop off and pick up
see: Transition bags; BIFF responses
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#co-parents | Where co-parents start
evidence: Family practitioners widely recommend short, predictable, conflict-free exchanges for children’s sake. Some families use school or neutral places.
try: Keep it to hello, the bag, one kid-related note, and goodbye.

Handoffs are often the tensest moments of the week, for parents and children. Keeping them brief, friendly and predictable helps children feel secure.

Anything more than a quick note belongs in a planned message or conversation, never at the door and never in front of the kids.

Program: This is the program’s co-parent advice: keep handoffs short and calm, and keep bigger topics for a planned time.

## Schedule change protocol
aka: changing the schedule; swapping weekends; last minute changes
see: Co-parenting calendar; BIFF responses
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Practical guidance from family mediation. Agreed notice periods reduce conflict over changes. Always check any court order first.
try: “Changes need 48 hours’ notice by text, except emergencies. Confirm in writing.”

Last-minute changes are a common source of co-parenting conflict. An agreed protocol (how much notice, how it’s asked, how it’s confirmed) takes the heat out.

Being flexible when you can, and asking for the same, tends to build goodwill over time.

Program: Write the protocol down, like a WP-03 agreement, so it isn’t renegotiated every time.

## Kids as messengers
aka: using kids as messengers; tell your dad; children in the middle
see: Triangulation; Handoffs between homes
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#co-parents | Where co-parents start
evidence: Research on children of separated parents consistently finds being caught in the middle of parental conflict is harmful for children.
try: Instead of “Tell your mom she’s late with the money,” message her directly.

Asking children to carry messages between parents (even simple ones) puts them in the middle of adult tension. It can make them feel responsible for keeping the peace.

Keeping all parent-to-parent communication direct protects children and keeps messages accurate.

Program: The relationships page is clear: never use the kids as messengers.

## Talking kindly about the other parent
aka: speaking well of the other parent; not badmouthing; in front of the kids
see: Kids as messengers; Parallel parenting
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#co-parents | Where co-parents start
evidence: Research suggests children do better when parents avoid criticizing each other in front of them.
try: “Your dad loves you a lot. We just do things differently in our two homes.”

Children often feel that criticism of a parent is partly criticism of them. Speaking neutrally or kindly about the other parent, even when it’s hard, protects them.

Venting belongs with adult friends or a professional, not with the children.

## Co-parenting check-in agenda
aka: co-parent meeting; co-parenting meeting; monthly co-parent review
see: Co-parenting calendar; A simple agenda for a hard talk
go: /workpapers/wp-04-what-keeps-coming-back.html | WP-04: What keeps coming back?
evidence: Structured, business-like co-parenting meetings are recommended by many family practitioners. Evidence is practical.
try: Monthly: school updates, health and appointments, activities and costs, upcoming schedule changes, one thing that’s going well.

A regular, business-like co-parenting check-in (in person, by phone or by structured email) keeps logistics from piling up into conflict.

A fixed agenda keeps it focused on the kids and away from old issues.

## Shared child expenses
aka: splitting kids’ costs; child expenses; school costs
see: Money and fairness splits; Co-parenting calendar
go: /lemonade-stand.html | The Lemonade Stand
evidence: Practical guidance. Any legal arrangements come first; clear records reduce disputes.
try: A shared list of kid costs, receipts attached, reviewed at the monthly check-in.

Extra costs (school trips, activities, clothes, devices) often cause friction between co-parents. Agreeing in advance which costs are shared, how, and how they’re recorded reduces arguments.

Keep records simple and visible to both.

## New partners and co-parenting
aka: introducing a new partner; new partner and kids; dating after separation
see: Talking kindly about the other parent; Stepfamily basics
go: /library/life.html#stepfamilies | Stepfamilies (Library)
evidence: Family research suggests gradual introductions and giving children time help adjustment. Timing depends on the family.
try: A heads-up to the other parent before a new partner meets the kids is often appreciated.

New relationships can stir strong feelings in co-parenting. Going slowly with introductions, and letting the other parent know in advance, usually reduces tension.

Children may need time and reassurance that the new person isn’t replacing anyone.

## Holiday schedules for two homes
aka: holiday schedule; alternating holidays; splitting holidays
see: Co-parenting calendar; Holiday load
go: /library/life.html#holidays | Holidays (Library)
evidence: Common practical arrangements include alternating years or splitting days. Court orders and parenting plans come first.
try: Settle holiday plans by the end of summer for the whole year, and put them in the shared calendar.

Holidays can be emotionally loaded for separated families. Planning early, following any agreed schedule, and keeping the children’s experience in focus helps.

Some families create new traditions in each home so children look forward to both.
