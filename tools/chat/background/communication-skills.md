# Communication skills

Background notes for Professor Puddles. Not published as pages. Format: see tools/chat/README.md.

## Paraphrasing
aka: paraphrase; saying it back; reflecting back; restating
see: Summarizing a conversation; Looping for understanding; Clarifying questions
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins: hear it back before you answer
evidence: Paraphrasing is a core skill in counseling and mediation training. Studies of listening suggest people feel more understood when they hear their words reflected, though the effect depends on sounding sincere rather than mechanical.
try: “So the part that got to you wasn’t the dishes, it was feeling like you’re the only one who notices. Did I get that right?”

Paraphrasing means putting what someone said into your own words and checking it back with them. It isn’t agreeing. It’s showing you heard, and giving them a chance to correct you before you respond.

Good paraphrases are short and focus on the heart of what was said, often including the feeling as well as the facts. A paraphrase that’s too long, or that sneaks in your own argument, stops working.

It feels awkward at first. That’s normal. Most people find that one honest “did I get that?” slows a heated talk down more than any clever reply.

Program: The Check-ins guide builds this in: one person says one fact and how it landed, the other says it back and asks, “Did I get that?”, then you swap.

## Summarizing a conversation
aka: summarising; wrap-up summary; recap at the end
see: Paraphrasing; Written recaps after a talk; Ending a hard conversation well
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A standard step in meeting facilitation and mediation. It’s practical wisdom more than a tested finding, but it reliably reduces “I thought we agreed…” moments.
try: “Okay, so we agreed you’ll own the grocery list, I’ll own the bins, and we’ll look again at the end of the month. Anything I missed?”

A summary pulls a whole conversation together into a few sentences: what each of you said matters, what you agreed, and what happens next. It’s different from paraphrasing one comment. It’s the wrap-up.

Summaries are especially useful after long or emotional talks, when memories quickly drift apart. Saying the agreement out loud, and inviting corrections, turns a vague feeling of “we sorted it” into something both people can point to later.

Program: End every check-in with one ask, one owner and one time, then say it back. If the agreement is about jobs, write it into WP-03 so it isn’t just remembered.

## Clarifying questions
aka: clarifying question; checking what they meant; asking before assuming
see: Paraphrasing; Open and closed questions; Assume positive intent
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Clarifying is a basic listening skill taught in communication and conflict-resolution courses. Research on misunderstandings in text and speech suggests people overestimate how clearly their meaning comes across, which is why checking helps.
try: “When you said ‘we need to talk about the weekend’, did you mean the plans, or how the chores went?”

A clarifying question asks about meaning before you react: “Do you mean…?”, “Which part…?”, “When you say ‘always’, what are you thinking of?” It treats a confusing comment as a puzzle to solve together rather than an attack to defend against.

The trick is tone. A clarifying question asked with a raised eyebrow sounds like a challenge. Asked with genuine curiosity, it tells the other person you want to get it right.

Program: WP-09 asks you to weigh what you heard before reacting: could a neutral meaning fit? A quick clarifying question is often the fastest way to find out.

## Open and closed questions
aka: open questions; closed questions; open-ended questions
see: Clarifying questions; The “and what else?” question; Talking side by side
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: The distinction is used widely in counseling, interviewing and teaching. It’s a practical tool, not a tested theory, and both kinds of question have their place.
try: Instead of “Did you have a good day?” (closed), try “What was the best bit of your day?” (open).

Closed questions can be answered with a word: yes, no, Tuesday. Open questions invite a fuller answer: “How did it go?”, “What was that like?”, “What would help?”

Open questions are good for connection and understanding. Closed questions are good for logistics and for tired people who don’t have many words left. A mix works best. If someone seems overwhelmed, switching to a simple either-or question can be a kindness.

Program: Turning Toward suggests small ways to reach for someone. A good open question about their world is one of the easiest.

## The “and what else?” question
aka: and what else; what else; is there anything else
see: Open and closed questions; Asking permission before giving advice
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Popularized by coach Michael Bungay Stanier in The Coaching Habit (2016). It comes from coaching practice rather than controlled research, but it’s simple and low-risk.
try: “That makes sense. And what else is going on with it?”

Often the first thing someone says isn’t the whole story. Asking “and what else?” once or twice, gently, gives them room to get to what’s really bothering them, before you jump to solving the first thing you heard.

It works because it slows you down as the listener. You’re not preparing your reply. You’re making space. Use it sparingly: asked five times in a row, it starts to feel like an interrogation.

Program: In a check-in, the first topic that comes up may not be the real one. If the real one appears, jot it down and give it its own time, keeping to one topic per talk.

## Asking permission before giving advice
aka: do you want advice; asking before advising; unsolicited advice
see: The “and what else?” question; Listening mode or fixing mode
go: /library/communication.html#advice-vs-support | Advice versus support (Library)
evidence: Research on social support suggests unasked-for advice can backfire, leaving people feeling less capable. Asking first is a practical way around that, though the research is about patterns, not every situation.
try: “Do you want ideas, or do you mostly want to vent right now? Either is fine.”

When someone shares a problem, many of us leap to solutions. Sometimes that’s exactly what they want. Often they first want to be heard. Asking “Do you want my thoughts, or do you just want me to listen?” lets them choose.

This small question prevents a common mix-up: one person feels dismissed (“I didn’t ask you to fix it”), while the other feels unappreciated (“I was trying to help”).

Program: The Library’s entry on advice versus support goes deeper. In a daily check-in (WP-13), the rule is simply to listen: nothing gets solved there.

## Listening mode or fixing mode
aka: fix it or listen; venting versus solving; solution mode
see: Asking permission before giving advice; Paraphrasing
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A widely used practical idea. It’s often framed as a gender difference, but that framing is oversimplified: individuals vary a lot, and the same person may want different things on different days.
try: “Right now I just need you to listen. I’ll ask for ideas later if I want them.”

Conversations go better when both people know which mode they’re in. In listening mode, the goal is understanding: nod, reflect, ask gently. In fixing mode, the goal is a plan: options, owners, next steps.

Many frustrations come from mismatched modes, not bad intentions. Saying which mode you want at the start (“I need to vent for five minutes, then maybe brainstorm”) saves both people guessing.

Program: Check-ins are for one topic, heard both ways, with one ask at the end. The Wiring Card can also note what you need in a hard moment: to be heard, or a plan.

## Using “and” instead of “but”
aka: and not but; the word but; yes but
see: Owning your part; Tentative language
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A common tip in communication and negotiation training. It’s practical rather than research-tested, and the tone behind the words still matters more than the word itself.
try: “I appreciate you cooking, and I’d love it if the pans got soaked before bed.”

“I love you, but…” tends to erase the first half of the sentence. The listener braces for what comes after the “but”. Swapping in “and” lets both parts stand: the appreciation and the request can both be true.

It won’t fix a harsh message on its own, and people notice if it’s used as a trick. As a habit, though, it keeps your good intentions from being cancelled by your grammar.

Program: WP-09’s fact, feeling and ask pairs naturally with “and”: “The bin wasn’t out, and I felt a bit unseen, and could we pick a fixed day for it?”

## Owning your part
aka: taking responsibility; my part in it; accountability in conflict
see: Using “and” instead of “but”; Anatomy of an apology; Self-serving bias
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: Taking responsibility for your own contribution is a common theme in conflict-resolution and couples research. It tends to lower defensiveness in the other person, though it works best when it’s genuine, not a bargaining chip.
try: “I think my part in this was waiting until I was fed up before saying anything.”

Most tangles have contributions on both sides. Naming your own part first (“I let this build up”, “I said it sharply”) makes it easier for the other person to lower their guard and look at theirs.

Owning your part isn’t taking all the blame, and it isn’t a trade (“I admitted mine, now you admit yours”). It’s just honest. It also keeps the focus on the setup and the pattern, not on who is the bad guy.

Program: The program looks at arrangements, not people. Owning your part fits Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person: what did each of us do that let this setup keep producing the problem?

## Tentative language
aka: softeners; hedging kindly; it seems to me
see: Using “and” instead of “but”; Clarifying questions
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Linguists and communication researchers describe how softeners change how requests land. Too much hedging can make a message unclear, so the balance matters.
try: “It seems like the kitchen is getting away from us lately. What do you think?”

Tentative language (“it seems to me”, “I wonder if”, “I might be wrong, but”) leaves room for the other person’s view. It’s useful when you’re making a guess about their feelings or motives, where certainty would be unfair.

Use it for interpretations, not for clear needs. “I wonder if maybe it might be nice to…” can blur an ask so much that it gets missed, especially by people who take words literally. State the ask plainly, and soften the guess.

Program: The Signal Translator shows how the same ask can land with more or less softening, and for different kinds of wiring.

## Assume positive intent
aka: benefit of the doubt; generous interpretation; most generous interpretation
see: Clarifying questions; Ladder of inference; Just-world thinking
go: /wired-differently.html | Wired Differently
evidence: A popular workplace and relationship principle. Research on attribution suggests we often judge others’ actions more harshly than our own, which is the problem this habit tries to counter. It isn’t meant to excuse repeated harm.
try: Before replying, ask yourself: “What’s the kindest reasonable reason they might have done this?”

Assuming positive intent means starting from the most generous reasonable explanation for what someone did, until you know more. “They forgot because they’re overloaded” rather than “They don’t care.”

It doesn’t mean ignoring a pattern, and it doesn’t replace asking. It just keeps your first reaction from being built on the worst guess, which tends to make the other person defensive and the conversation harder.

Program: The Library’s entries on attribution errors explain why we default to harsh guesses. Wired Differently adds that a mix-up between different kinds of wiring usually runs both ways.

## Looping for understanding
aka: looping; closing the loop; checking you understood
see: Paraphrasing; Summarizing a conversation
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A technique from mediation practice, described in Amanda Ripley’s book High Conflict (2021). It’s a structured form of paraphrasing; evidence comes mostly from practitioners’ experience.
try: “Let me see if I’ve got it: you’re not upset about the money itself, you’re upset that you found out after. Is that it, or is there more?”

Looping means listening, saying back what you heard, and then asking whether you got it right, and repeating until the other person says yes. The loop is only closed when they feel understood, not when you think you’ve understood.

It’s slower than a normal conversation, which is the point. It’s especially useful in conversations that keep going in circles, because people often repeat themselves when they don’t feel heard.

Program: This is the same move as the Check-ins guide’s “hear it back before you answer”. Try it once in your next check-in, then swap.

## Mirroring and labeling
aka: mirroring; labelling; tactical empathy
see: Paraphrasing; Naming feelings in others
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Popularized by former FBI negotiator Chris Voss in Never Split the Difference (2016). It draws on counseling skills; research on the specific techniques is limited, and it works best when sincere rather than as a tactic.
try: They say, “I’m just so done with this week.” You say, gently, “Done with this week?” and let them keep going.

Mirroring means repeating a few key words someone just said, often as a soft question. It invites them to say more without you steering. Labeling means naming the feeling you think you’re hearing: “It sounds like you’re really worn out.”

Both are simple and quick. The risk is sounding like you’re using a technique on someone. Use them to understand, not to win, and drop them if they feel forced.

Program: In Check-ins, saying back what you heard and how it landed is the heart of the ground rules.

## Naming feelings in others
aka: emotion labeling; it sounds like you feel; guessing feelings
see: Mirroring and labeling; The emotion wheel; Tentative language
go: /library/emotions.html#affect-labeling | Putting feelings into words (Library)
evidence: Research on “affect labeling” suggests naming feelings can reduce their intensity for the person feeling them. Naming someone else’s feelings is less studied, and a wrong guess can annoy, so offering it tentatively matters.
try: “I might be off, but you seem a bit flat since the call. Frustrated, or just tired?”

A gentle guess at what someone is feeling (“you seem disappointed”) can help them feel seen, and helps them find words if they’re struggling. Offering two options makes it easier for people who find feelings hard to name.

Always leave room for “no, that’s not it.” The goal isn’t to be right about their feelings. It’s to show you’re paying attention.

Program: Wired Differently notes that some people feel plenty but can’t find the word, and suggests asking about the body or actions instead: “more like tired, or more like annoyed?”

## Speaking for yourself
aka: speak for yourself; I language; not speaking for others
see: I-messages that aren’t blame in disguise; Owning your part
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A standard ground rule in facilitation, mediation and family meetings. It’s practical guidance rather than a research finding.
try: Instead of “Everyone thinks you’re always late,” try “I’ve been feeling anxious when we’re late.”

Speaking for yourself means talking about your own experience, not claiming to speak for “everyone”, “the family” or “any normal person”. Bringing in imaginary allies makes the other person feel ganged up on, and turns a conversation into a trial.

It also keeps you honest. You know your own experience. You can only guess at everyone else’s.

Program: Each workpaper asks you to fill in only your own side. The same rule works in conversation.

## I-messages that aren’t blame in disguise
aka: fake I-statements; I feel that you; I feel like you
see: Speaking for yourself; Feelings versus thoughts
go: /library/communication.html#i-statements | I-statements (Library)
evidence: A practical refinement of the well-known I-statement technique. There’s research support for I-statements lowering hostility in some studies, though effects are modest and tone matters more than grammar.
try: “I feel like you don’t care” is a judgment. “I feel lonely when we don’t talk in the evenings” is a feeling.

Starting a sentence with “I feel” doesn’t automatically make it an I-statement. “I feel that you’re selfish” is still a verdict on the person. The giveaway is the word “that” or “like” followed by a claim about them.

A true I-message names a feeling (lonely, stretched, worried), the specific situation, and ideally what would help. It’s about your experience, which the other person can’t argue with, rather than their character, which they will.

Program: WP-09’s three parts (fact, feeling and ask) are a practical way to build a real I-message.

## Feelings versus thoughts
aka: I feel that; thoughts dressed as feelings; feelings or judgments
see: I-messages that aren’t blame in disguise; The emotion wheel
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: The distinction is central to nonviolent communication training and to cognitive approaches. It’s a teaching tool, not a strict scientific line: thoughts and feelings are tangled together in real life.
try: “I feel ignored” includes a guess about what they did. “I feel lonely” is the feeling underneath it.

Some words sound like feelings but are really interpretations of what someone else did: ignored, manipulated, disrespected, abandoned. They’re understandable, but they point a finger, so the other person tends to defend.

Looking underneath for the plain feeling (hurt, lonely, scared, tired, frustrated) makes it easier to be heard. You can still describe what happened. Just keep the feeling about you.

Program: WP-09 asks for “the feeling underneath, like tired or unseen”, then the fact and the ask.
