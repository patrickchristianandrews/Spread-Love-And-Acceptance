# Thinking traps in relationships

These complement the thinking-traps entries in the Library.

## Self-serving bias
aka: self serving bias; credit for wins blame for losses
see: Owning your part; Fundamental attribution error at home
go: /library/fairness.html#egocentric-bias | Why we each think we do more (Library)
evidence: One of the best-documented findings in social psychology: people tend to credit themselves for successes and blame circumstances for failures.
try: “When the dinner went well, I thought it was my planning. When it flopped, I blamed the oven. Hmm.”

We tend to take credit when things go well and point to circumstances when they don’t. In shared lives, this means each person often sees their own contributions as bigger and their own slips as more excusable.

It’s normal, not a character flaw. Knowing about it makes it easier to give others the same benefit of the doubt you give yourself.

Program: The Library’s entry on why we each think we do more is closely related. Writing things down in WP-01 helps correct for it.

## Fundamental attribution error at home
aka: character versus circumstances; they’re just lazy; blaming personality
see: Self-serving bias; Assume positive intent
go: /library/thinking.html#fundamental-attribution-error | Fundamental attribution error (Library)
evidence: A classic finding in social psychology (Lee Ross named it in 1977), though how strong and universal it is has been debated.
try: Instead of “He’s so careless,” ask: “What in his day might have made this slip?”

We tend to explain other people’s behavior by their character (“she’s lazy”) and our own by circumstances (“I was exhausted”). At home, this turns setup problems into character judgments.

Asking “what about the situation might explain this?” often reveals a fixable cause, like no clear owner, bad timing or overload.

Program: This is why the program says: fix the setup, not the person (Pillar II).

## Sunk cost thinking
aka: sunk cost; sunk cost fallacy; we’ve already put so much in
see: Status quo bias; Reversible and irreversible decisions
go: /library/thinking.html | Thinking traps (Library)
evidence: The sunk cost effect is well documented in decision research (for example, Arkes and Blumer, 1985), though people vary in how strongly they show it.
try: “We’ve done the chore chart for months, but it’s clearly not working. Let’s change it anyway.”

Sunk cost thinking means sticking with something because of what you’ve already put in (time, money, effort), even when it no longer makes sense.

In households, it can keep a failing system in place (“but we spent a whole Sunday making that chart”). The better question is: from here, what works best?

Program: The monthly look-back (WP-04) asks you to fix the setup, not keep patching it. Changing a setup that isn’t working is a win.

## Status quo bias
aka: status quo; sticking with how things are; resistance to change
see: Defaults at home; Sunk cost thinking
go: /five-pillars.html#quiet-incentives | Pillar V: Notice the quiet incentives
evidence: Status quo bias (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988) is well documented: people tend to prefer the current state of affairs, partly because changes feel like losses.
try: “We’ve always split it this way, but let’s check whether it still fits our lives now.”

We tend to prefer things as they are, even when a change might be better. Existing arrangements feel safe and familiar, and any change feels like giving something up.

In shared homes, it keeps old splits in place long after life has changed: a new job, a baby, an illness.

Program: WP-03 says to change agreements on purpose at the weekly closing, not by drift. Pillar V is about noticing these quiet pulls.

## Loss aversion
aka: loss aversion; losses loom larger; fear of losing
see: Framing gains and losses; Status quo bias
go: /library/thinking.html | Thinking traps (Library)
evidence: A core idea in prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). Its size and generality have been debated in recent research, but the basic pattern is widely observed.
try: Giving up “my” Saturday morning can feel bigger than gaining a free Sunday afternoon, even if it’s the same time.

Losses often feel bigger than equal gains. Giving up a job you like, or some free time, can feel heavier than the benefit you get in exchange.

In negotiation at home, it helps to acknowledge what each person is giving up, not just what they’re gaining.

Program: When trading jobs in WP-03, say what each person is letting go of, and thank them for it.

## Hindsight bias
aka: hindsight; I knew it all along; should have seen it coming
see: Outcome bias; Self-forgiveness
go: /library/thinking.html | Thinking traps (Library)
evidence: Hindsight bias is well documented (Baruch Fischhoff’s research in the 1970s): once we know an outcome, we think it was more predictable than it was.
try: “It’s easy to say we should have left earlier now that we know about the traffic.”

After something goes wrong, it often seems obvious in hindsight: “you should have known”. But at the time, the information may not have been clear at all.

Hindsight bias makes us harsher on others and ourselves. Asking “what did we actually know at the time?” is fairer.

Program: Blameless reviews (in the Library’s teams section) are designed to counter hindsight when looking back at what went wrong.

## Outcome bias
aka: judging by results; outcome bias; good decision bad outcome
see: Hindsight bias; Reversible and irreversible decisions
go: /library/teams.html#blameless-reviews | Blameless reviews (Library)
evidence: Research (for example, Baron and Hershey, 1988) shows people judge decisions by their outcomes even when the outcome was partly luck.
try: “Booking the cheap flight was reasonable. The strike wasn’t anyone’s fault.”

We tend to judge a decision as good or bad based on how it turned out, rather than on whether it was sensible given what was known. A good decision can have a bad outcome through bad luck, and vice versa.

In shared decisions, separating the quality of the choice from the result keeps blame out of it.

Program: The Library’s blameless reviews entry shows how teams look at the process rather than the person.

## Recency bias
aka: recency effect; last week colors everything; recent memory
see: Memory drift after an argument; Hindsight bias
go: /book/chapter-2.html | Chapter II: Is the split working?
evidence: Research on memory shows recent events are easier to recall and often get extra weight in judgments.
try: One bad week of chores can make it feel like “you never help”, even after a good month.

What happened recently feels more important than what happened a while ago. One rough week can overshadow a good month, and one good gesture can overshadow weeks of imbalance.

Looking at a longer stretch, ideally written down, gives a fairer picture.

Program: Chapter II says it plainly: look at the month, not last night. The monthly look-back (WP-04) uses four weeks of logs.

## Memory drift after an argument
aka: remembering it differently; conflicting memories; we remember it differently
see: Recency bias; Written recaps after a talk
go: /library/thinking.html#memory-reconstruction | Memory reconstruction (Library)
evidence: Memory research shows memories are reconstructed each time we recall them, and emotional events are especially prone to shifting details.
try: “I think we remember that night differently. Instead of arguing about who’s right, can we agree what we want next time?”

Two people often remember the same argument quite differently, and both can be sincere. Memory rebuilds events each time we recall them, shaped by feelings and later conversations.

Arguing about whose memory is correct rarely helps. Focusing on what each person felt, and what to do next time, usually works better.

Program: This is why WP-01 says to write down the week before it turns into a story.

## Just-world thinking
aka: just world belief; they must have deserved it; everything happens for a reason
see: Fundamental attribution error at home; Assume positive intent
go: /library/thinking.html | Thinking traps (Library)
evidence: The “belief in a just world” was described by Melvin Lerner in the 1960s and 70s. It can lead people to blame others for misfortunes outside their control.
try: “Maybe she’s behind at work not because she’s disorganized, but because she was handed two jobs.”

Many of us like to believe the world is fair, so people get what they deserve. It’s comforting, but it can make us blame people for problems that aren’t their fault, including overload they didn’t choose.

Remembering that a lot of life is circumstance helps us respond with more compassion.

Program: Pillar II reminds you to look at the setup. The Library’s thinking-traps entries go deeper.

## Projection
aka: projecting; assuming they feel what I feel
see: False consensus at home; Clarifying questions
go: /library/thinking.html#false-consensus | False consensus (Library)
evidence: In everyday use, projection means assuming others share our feelings or motives. Research on false consensus supports the general tendency; the classic psychoanalytic idea is more debated.
try: “I’m assuming you’re annoyed because I would be. Are you?”

Sometimes we assume other people feel what we’d feel in their place, or we read our own mood into their words. If you’re irritated, a neutral text can seem irritated too.

Checking (“are you upset, or am I reading into it?”) clears up a lot of mix-ups.

Program: WP-09 asks: would this read the same if you weren’t already stretched?

## False consensus at home
aka: everyone would agree; normal people do it this way; obviously
see: Projection; Curse of knowledge in instructions
go: /library/thinking.html#false-consensus | False consensus (Library)
evidence: The false consensus effect (Ross, Greene and House, 1977) is well documented: we overestimate how much others share our views and habits.
try: “I assumed everyone washes pans straight away. Clearly that’s just my family’s way.”

We tend to think our own way of doing things is the normal way. Different families have different habits about cleaning, money, time and holidays, and each feels “obvious” to the people raised in it.

Recognizing that your normal is one of many makes it easier to agree on a shared way instead of arguing about the right way.

Program: The Library’s family scripts entry covers habits we bring from our families.

## Curse of knowledge in instructions
aka: curse of knowledge; obvious to me; unclear instructions
see: False consensus at home; Clear yes or no questions
go: /library/thinking.html#curse-of-knowledge | The curse of knowledge (Library)
evidence: The curse of knowledge is well documented: once we know something, it’s hard to imagine not knowing it, so our explanations leave out steps.
try: “Can you ‘do the bins’?” might mean three things to you (take out, rinse, new bags) and one to them.

When you know how something is done, it’s easy to give instructions that leave out the steps that seem obvious to you. Then the other person does it “wrong”, and both feel frustrated.

Spelling out what “done” means (once, kindly) often solves recurring “you did it wrong” arguments.

Program: WP-03 can include a short “what done looks like” note for jobs that keep going wrong.

## Ladder of inference
aka: ladder of inference; jumping to conclusions; the story I’m telling myself
see: Clarifying questions; Assume positive intent
go: /workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A thinking model developed by Chris Argyris and popularized by Peter Senge in The Fifth Discipline (1990). It’s a teaching tool rather than a research finding.
try: “The story I’m telling myself is that you don’t care about the plans. Is that fair?”

The ladder of inference describes how we climb quickly from what we saw to conclusions: we notice a detail, add meaning, make assumptions and reach a belief, often in seconds.

Climbing back down (“what did I actually see?”) separates the fact from the story you added, which makes a calm conversation much easier.

Program: WP-09 asks for the fact in one sentence with no adjectives. The Frequency Journey even has a level about telling what you saw from the story you added.

## Better-than-average effect
aka: better than average; illusory superiority; I’m a good driver
see: Self-serving bias; False consensus at home
go: /library/fairness.html#egocentric-bias | Why we each think we do more (Library)
evidence: Many studies show most people rate themselves above average on positive traits, which can’t be true for everyone. The effect varies by trait and culture.
try: “I think I’m the tidy one. Maybe we both think that?”

Most people see themselves as a bit above average: more considerate, more reasonable, more helpful. In a shared home, both people can sincerely believe they’re the more thoughtful one.

That’s a good reason to rely on shared records rather than impressions.

Program: WP-01 and the Lemonade Stand replace impressions with a written week.

## Zero-sum thinking
aka: zero sum; if you win I lose; fixed pie
see: Trading across issues; Splitting the difference
go: /library/conflict.html#integrative-distributive | Win-win and win-lose (Library)
evidence: Research on negotiation shows people often assume a “fixed pie” even when both sides could gain. Recognizing this opens up better options.
try: “Your free evening doesn’t have to be my lost evening. What if we swap nights?”

Zero-sum thinking assumes that any gain for one person is a loss for the other. In relationships, that’s rarely true: many arrangements can be better for both.

Looking for trades, different timing or new options often turns a tug-of-war into teamwork.

Program: The Library’s win-win entry and the negotiation notes on trading explain how to expand the options.

## Scarcity mindset
aka: scarcity; tunneling; bandwidth
see: Overwhelm as a feeling; Energy audit
go: /workpapers/wp-02-how-much-are-you-carrying.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Sendhil Mullainathan and Eldar Shafir described how scarcity of time or money narrows attention in Scarcity (2013). Some specific findings have been debated in replication work.
try: “We’re both stretched this month, so let’s not make any big decisions until it eases.”

When time, money or energy is very short, attention narrows to the urgent problem in front of you. That helps in the moment but makes it harder to plan ahead or notice others.

Knowing this helps you go easier on yourself and others in stretched seasons, and time important decisions for when things ease.

Program: WP-02 and Today’s Weather help you notice when you’re running low, so you can pace hard talks.

## IKEA effect
aka: IKEA effect; valuing what we made; my way
see: Gatekeeping around chores; Status quo bias
go: /library/fairness.html#gatekeeping | Gatekeeping (Library)
evidence: Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely (2012) found people value things more when they helped build them.
try: “I set up the kitchen system, so I might be overprotective of it. Let’s redesign it together.”

We tend to value things we built ourselves more highly, including household systems and routines. That can make it hard to let someone else do a job their way.

Involving everyone in designing a shared system makes it more likely everyone will value and keep it.

Program: WP-03 is filled in together for exactly this reason. The Library covers gatekeeping.
