# Growing up and the lens we carry

These add practical detail to Where your lens came from: how each stage of growing up shapes the understandings we use as adults.

## Developmental stages
aka: stages of development; developmental stages; erikson; erikson stages; psychosocial stages; stages of life; life stages
see: Why timing matters; Sensitive periods; Core beliefs
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#stages | The stages, with a table
evidence: Erik Erikson described eight psychosocial stages across the whole life in the 1950s. The ages and details are debated and reflect his culture, but the idea that development continues all through life is widely accepted.
try: Ask yourself: which big question was I busy answering when this rule started? Is the world safe, am I capable, where do I belong, or can I be close without losing myself?

Each stage of growing up is busy with its own big question. First years: is the world safe, will someone come? Toddler years: can I do things myself? Early childhood: is it okay to take up space? School years: am I capable, how do I measure up? Teen years: who am I, where do I belong? Young adulthood: can I be close without losing myself?

The answers we land on become understandings we carry forward and keep using as adults, often without noticing. We revisit them at big changes, like a new relationship, a move or becoming a parent.

## Why timing matters
aka: why childhood matters; does age matter; certain ages; when it happened; why some things affect me more; impact of childhood; childhood experiences; age it happened
see: Developmental stages; Sensitive periods
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#timing | Why timing matters
evidence: Research on sensitive periods finds the brain is especially ready to learn from certain experiences at certain ages, most clearly in early childhood and again in the teen years. Population findings never predict what will happen to any one person.
try: “Part of this feeling is older than today. I was about the age where belonging mattered most.”

An experience that touches the question you were answering at the time tends to become part of the answer, and to go deeper than the same event at another age. A move at 4 is mostly about routines; a move at 14, right in the middle of “where do I belong?”, can leave a longer feeling of being the outsider.

Other things that make an experience land harder: it happened again and again, you decided it meant something about you, nobody helped you make sense of it, or you were a sensitive child. Deeper isn’t permanent. People keep learning all their lives.

## Sensitive periods
aka: sensitive period; critical period; brain development; early brain development; teen brain; adolescent brain; serve and return
see: Why timing matters; Developmental stages
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#research | The research, in plain words
evidence: The Harvard Center on the Developing Child summarizes research on early brain development and responsive “serve and return” care. Sarah-Jayne Blakemore and colleagues describe adolescence as a period of heightened sensitivity to peers and belonging.
try: If you’re raising children, you don’t need to be perfect. Noticing a rough moment and making it right teaches a lot.

A sensitive period is a window when the growing brain is especially ready to learn certain things from experience. The clearest evidence is for early childhood, when the brain’s basic architecture is built through everyday back-and-forth with caring adults. A second strong wave comes in the teen years, especially for the social brain, which is why peer approval and exclusion leave such strong marks.

Sensitive doesn’t mean fixed. Later relationships, reflection and practice genuinely reshape early understandings.

## Core beliefs
aka: core belief; schemas; schema; beliefs about myself; negative beliefs about myself; why do i think im not good enough; i feel like a burden; i feel too much; im only okay when im useful
see: Developmental stages; Unspoken expectations
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#self | When the lens is wrong about you
evidence: Aaron Beck’s cognitive therapy and Jeffrey Young’s schema work describe deep, early beliefs about self and others that shape how we read events. This site uses the idea in everyday terms, not as therapy.
try: Write the old belief, where you think you learned it, and one fairer adult sentence. “I’m only okay when I’m useful” becomes “Helping is something I do, not the price of being loved.”

Many of the harshest things adults believe about themselves were concluded by a child with half the facts. A child can’t know the grown-ups were tired or worried; they only know how it felt. Common ones: “my needs are a burden,” “I’m too much,” “mistakes mean I’m bad,” “I have to be the strong one.”

As an adult you’re allowed to look again with the full picture, and to choose a fairer reading.

## Unspoken expectations
aka: unspoken expectations; unwritten rules; should just know; they should know what i need; should know without me saying; expectations of my partner; if you loved me you would know; mind reading; expecting them to know; family rules; rulebook; family rulebook; house rules from childhood
see: Core beliefs; Family roles; Gender scripts; Family scripts in daily life
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#expect | The expectations we place on others
evidence: Family systems writers such as Virginia Satir described unspoken family rules. Research on couples finds unmet expectations are a common source of resentment; this entry uses the idea in everyday terms.
try: “I think this is my old family rulebook talking. Where I grew up, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it. Can we work out our own rule?”

We all grew up with an unwritten rulebook: how love looks, what happens when someone’s upset, who does which jobs, when rest is allowed. Carried into adult life, those rules feel like facts about how things should be, not preferences we brought from somewhere. “If you loved me, you’d just know.” “A good partner plans the birthdays.” “Rest is earned.”

An expectation that’s never said out loud can’t be agreed to, only broken. Many resentments are two people following two different rulebooks, each sure theirs is normal. Swapping rulebooks on a calm day, then choosing your own rules on purpose, helps.

## Gender scripts
aka: gender roles; gender expectations; boys dont cry; women do the housework; men fix things; who does the chores; learned chores from parents
see: Unspoken expectations; Family roles
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#world | How the world shapes the lens
evidence: Studies following families over time have found that how parents split housework is linked with how their grown children later split it. Researchers widely describe gender-role messages children absorb from family, school and media.
try: List three jobs you each assumed “someone” would do. Beside each, write who did it in the home you grew up in. Then choose an owner on purpose.

Children absorb messages about what boys, girls and everyone else are supposed to be good at, feel and do: “boys don’t cry,” “girls keep the peace,” “men fix things, women remember birthdays.”

These scripts are one of the big quiet reasons invisible work at home still tends to fall unevenly, even between people who believe in sharing it.

## Comparing ourselves with others
aka: social comparison; comparing myself; everyone else has it together; social media comparison; instagram; compare my relationship
see: Core beliefs; Why timing matters
go: /growing-up-in-depth.html#world | How the world shapes the lens
evidence: Leon Festinger’s social comparison theory (1954) describes how we judge ourselves against others. Research links heavy comparison on social media with lower mood for some people, especially teens; findings vary.
try: When you catch a comparison, add the missing half: “I’m comparing my ordinary Tuesday with their best, edited moment.”

We work out how we’re doing partly by comparing with others. Today many people, especially teens, compare their everyday life with everyone else’s highlights.

That can make ordinary, good-enough partners, families and homes look like they’re failing, when they’re simply real.
