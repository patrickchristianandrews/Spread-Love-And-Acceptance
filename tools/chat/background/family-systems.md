# Family systems basics

These add to the Library’s family systems and family scripts entries.

## Triangulation
aka: triangulation; triangles in families; pulling in a third person
see: Informal mediation between friends or family; Shuttle conversations
go: /library/life.html#family-systems | Family systems (Library)
evidence: Triangulation comes from Murray Bowen’s family systems theory. It’s influential in family therapy; the theory as a whole has limited experimental testing, but the pattern is widely recognized.
try: Instead of venting to your sister about your mom, try talking to your mom directly, or asking your sister to stay out of the middle.

Triangulation happens when tension between two people gets routed through a third: a parent complains to a child about the other parent, or two siblings talk about a third instead of to them. It can relieve pressure for a while but keeps the real problem from being solved.

The person in the middle often feels stuck and stressed. Gently stepping out (“I love you both, but I’d rather you talk to each other”) usually helps everyone.

Program: For co-parents, never use children as messengers. The relationships page’s family section suggests agreeing who owns what, so nobody has to guess.

## Differentiation of self
aka: differentiation; staying yourself in a family; being close without losing yourself
see: Triangulation; Family roles
go: /library/life.html#family-systems | Family systems (Library)
evidence: A central idea in Bowen family systems theory. It’s widely used in family therapy; research support is mixed and mostly correlational.
try: “I understand you see it differently. I’m going to make a different choice, and I still love you.”

Differentiation means being able to stay close to your family while still thinking and choosing for yourself. You can hear their feelings without being swept up in them, and hold your own view without cutting them off.

It’s a lifelong process, not a finish line. It often shows up as calmer responses to family tension.

Program: Know your own wiring helps you separate what’s yours from patterns you learned growing up.

## Family roles
aka: family roles; the peacemaker; the responsible one; the scapegoat; the joker
see: Differentiation of self; Family scripts in daily life
go: /library/life.html#family-scripts | Family scripts (Library)
evidence: Popular descriptions of family roles come from family therapy and addiction-recovery writing. They’re useful as reflection prompts but aren’t scientific categories.
try: Ask yourself: “What role did I play growing up? Do I still play it with my own household?”

In many families, people fall into roles: the responsible one, the peacemaker, the funny one, the one who gets blamed. Roles can help a family cope, but they can also stick long after they’re needed.

Noticing your old role (and whether you still play it at work or at home) can explain why certain situations feel so heavy.

Program: The Library’s family scripts entry covers habits we carry from our families. Know your own wiring separates learned patterns from wiring.

## Family scripts in daily life
aka: how we did it growing up; family habits; the way my family did it
see: Family roles; False consensus at home
go: /library/life.html#family-scripts | Family scripts (Library)
evidence: A practical application of family scripts research (associated with John Byng-Hall). Couples commonly bring different family habits into a shared home.
try: “In my family, we always cleaned on Saturdays. What was it like in yours?”

Each of us grows up with unspoken rules about chores, money, holidays, arguments and affection. In a new household, these scripts collide, and each feels like the normal way.

Talking about your family scripts (without judging whose was better) makes it easier to write a new, shared one.

Program: The Library’s family scripts entry goes deeper. WP-03 is a good place to write down the new, shared way.

## Emotional cutoff
aka: cutting off family; emotional distance from family; no contact
see: Differentiation of self; Boundaries with parents as an adult
go: /library/life.html#family-systems | Family systems (Library)
evidence: A concept from Bowen family systems theory describing managing tension through distance. Decisions about family contact are deeply personal and depend on circumstances.
try: If you’re considering less contact, it may help to talk it through with someone you trust or a qualified professional first.

In family systems thinking, emotional cutoff means managing unresolved tension by reducing or ending contact. Sometimes distance is a healthy choice; sometimes it leaves old tension unresolved.

There’s no single right answer, and only you can weigh your situation. If family relationships feel very painful, a qualified professional can help you think it through.

Program: The program doesn’t tell anyone whether to keep or leave a relationship. It helps with the everyday setup and how you talk, within whatever relationship you choose.

## Sibling rivalry in adulthood
aka: adult siblings; sibling rivalry; siblings as adults
see: Family roles; Sibling care meetings
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Research suggests sibling relationships often become warmer in adulthood but can reignite around family events, inheritance or caring for parents.
try: “I know we see things differently. Can we focus on what Mum needs right now, and leave the old stuff aside?”

Old sibling tensions (who was favored, who did more) can resurface in adulthood, especially around holidays, parents’ needs or big decisions.

Focusing on a shared goal (like a parent’s care), dividing jobs clearly, and avoiding old comparisons can keep things calmer.

Program: WP-03 works well for siblings sharing responsibilities, like care for an aging parent.

## Birth order myths
aka: birth order; oldest child; middle child; youngest child
see: Family roles; Temperament and character
go: /library/wiring.html#big-five | The Big Five (Library)
evidence: Popular ideas about birth order and personality aren’t well supported. Large studies (for example, Rohrer, Egloff and Schmukle, 2015) found little or no effect of birth order on personality.
try: Rather than “you’re such a typical oldest child”, ask what roles each sibling ended up with.

Many people believe firstborns are responsible, middle children feel overlooked and youngest children are carefree. It’s a fun idea, but large studies find birth order has little effect on personality traits.

Family roles and circumstances probably matter more than birth order itself.

Program: The Library’s Big Five entry covers what research does say about personality.

## Adult children at home
aka: adult child living at home; boomerang kids; grown kids moving back
see: Chore charts that work; Money and fairness splits
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Adult children living with parents is common in many countries and cultures. Clear expectations are widely recommended by family educators.
try: A short written agreement: rent or contribution (if any), jobs owned, guests, and a check-in date.

When an adult child lives at home, old parent-child habits can clash with adult expectations. Unspoken assumptions about rent, chores, guests and schedules cause most of the friction.

Treating it more like a shared household (clear agreements, named jobs, regular check-ins) helps everyone feel respected.

Program: The Lemonade Stand and WP-03 work for any household of two to eight, including adult family members.

## Grandparents and childcare
aka: grandparents helping with kids; grandparent childcare; in-laws and kids
see: Boundaries with parents as an adult; Family meetings
go: /library/life.html#in-laws | In-laws (Library)
evidence: Grandparent childcare is common and often valued. Tensions around rules and expectations are widely reported; research on specific solutions is limited.
try: “We’re so grateful for your help. Could we agree on a few house rules, like screens and snacks, so the kids get the same message?”

Grandparents often provide wonderful help with children, but differences in rules and parenting styles can cause friction. Gratitude plus a few clear, agreed basics usually works better than correcting in the moment.

Keeping the agreements short and focused on what matters most avoids feeling controlling.

Program: The Library’s in-laws entry covers extended-family tensions.

## Family group chats
aka: family group chat; family WhatsApp; group chat drama
see: Texting norms that prevent fights; Tone indicators
go: /conversation-reader.html | The Conversation Reader
evidence: Practical guidance. Group messaging makes misunderstandings more likely because tone is hard to read and many people see each message.
try: For anything sensitive, move from the group chat to a private call.

Family group chats are great for photos and logistics, but tricky for anything sensitive: many readers, little tone, and a permanent record.

A simple rule helps: logistics and good news in the group; disagreements and feelings in private, preferably by voice.

Program: The Conversation Reader can help you see where a thread turned tense, and suggest a calmer reply.
