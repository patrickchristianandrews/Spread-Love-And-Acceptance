# Self-understanding

These add to Know your own wiring and the Library’s motivation and emotions entries.

## Values clarification
aka: values clarification; my values; what matters to me
see: Values-based decisions; Needs and wants
go: /library/motivation.html#values | Values (Library)
evidence: Values work is part of several approaches, including acceptance and commitment therapy. Research suggests reflecting on values can support wellbeing and motivation.
try: Pick five words from a list (kindness, fairness, adventure, security, creativity…) that feel most like you.

Clarifying your values means naming what matters most to you: kindness, fairness, adventure, security, creativity. Values give direction when choices are hard.

They also explain some conflicts: two people can both be acting on good values (security and adventure) that simply pull in different directions.

Program: The Library’s values entry goes deeper. Know your own wiring helps you understand what’s lasting about you.

## Needs and wants
aka: needs versus wants; underlying needs; what I need
see: Values clarification; Feelings versus thoughts
go: /workpapers/wp-09-tone-filter.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A practical distinction used in negotiation and nonviolent communication. It helps separate a specific request from the need behind it.
try: “I want you home by 6” might come from a need for predictability, which could be met in other ways.

A want is a specific thing (“I want you to call every night”). A need is what’s underneath (connection, reassurance, rest). Needs can often be met in more than one way.

Knowing your need gives both people more options.

Program: WP-09’s “ask” works best when it serves the real need.

## Hot buttons
aka: triggers; hot buttons; what sets me off
see: Body clues of emotion; Temperament and character
go: /wp-11.html | WP-11: The Calm-Down Kit
evidence: “Trigger” in everyday use means something that sets off a strong reaction. Noticing patterns is a common step in many approaches to emotion regulation.
try: Write down the last three times you reacted strongly. What did they have in common?

Most people have a few hot buttons: situations or words that set off a bigger reaction than expected. Often they connect to old experiences, tiredness or strong values.

Knowing yours doesn’t mean others must avoid them forever. It helps you notice the reaction early, and explain it to others.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit asks what tends to set you off, and where you feel it first.

## Temperament and character
aka: temperament; character; nature and nurture; personality basics
see: Hot buttons; Birth order myths
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: Research on temperament (for example, Jerome Kagan’s work) shows some tendencies appear early and are fairly stable, while character and habits develop through experience.
try: “I’ve always needed quiet to recharge. That’s temperament. Snapping when I’m tired is a habit I can work on.”

Temperament is the set of tendencies you’re born with: how sensitive, active or cautious you tend to be. Character and habits grow from experience.

Knowing which is which helps: you plan around temperament and work on habits.

Program: Know your own wiring separates three layers: wiring, learned patterns and today’s conditions.

## Self-talk
aka: self talk; inner voice; how I talk to myself
see: Self-forgiveness; Guilt after setting a boundary
go: /library/connection.html#self-compassion | Self-compassion (Library)
evidence: Research on self-talk suggests the way we speak to ourselves affects emotions and performance. Some studies suggest using your own name or “you” can create helpful distance.
try: Swap “I’m such an idiot” for “You made a mistake. What’s the next step?”

The way you talk to yourself shapes how you feel. Harsh self-talk (“I always mess up”) tends to drain motivation; kinder, practical self-talk (“that was hard, let’s try again”) supports it.

Noticing your inner voice is the first step. Talking to yourself as you would to a friend is the second.

Program: The Library’s self-compassion entry goes deeper.

## Johari window
aka: Johari window; blind spots; what others see in me
see: Asking for feedback; Personal user manual
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: Created by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955. It’s a reflection tool, not a research instrument.
try: Ask a trusted friend: “What’s one thing about how I come across that I might not realize?”

The Johari window divides self-knowledge into four parts: what you and others both know about you, what only you know, what others see that you don’t (blind spots), and what nobody knows yet.

Sharing more about yourself and asking for feedback both expand the shared area, which usually improves relationships.

Program: The Wiring Card is a way of sharing what only you know about how you receive words.

## Personal user manual
aka: user manual for me; how to work with me; personal README
see: Johari window; Explicit requests
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: Popular in workplaces as a way to share working preferences. Evidence is anecdotal, but it matches the idea of reducing guesswork.
try: A few lines: how I like to get information, what I need when stressed, and what helps me do my best work.

A personal user manual is a short document describing how you work best: communication preferences, what stresses you, how you like feedback. Some teams share them.

It’s like a Wiring Card for work: less guessing, fewer misunderstandings.

Program: The Wiring Card is the program’s version, focused on how you receive words.

## Moods and cycles
aka: mood patterns; tracking moods; my patterns
see: Energy audit; Hot buttons
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: Mood tracking can help people notice patterns. It’s a self-awareness tool, not a diagnostic one.
try: Keep a one-word mood note each day for two weeks, and look for patterns with sleep, work and people.

Moods often follow patterns: time of day, days of the week, sleep, workload or particular people. Noticing patterns helps you plan around them.

If mood changes are intense or worrying, it’s worth talking to a qualified professional.

Program: Today’s Weather keeps a private almanac on your device so you can spot your own patterns.

## Strengths spotting
aka: strengths; character strengths; what I’m good at
see: Values clarification; Pride
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: Positive psychology research (for example, the VIA character strengths work) suggests using personal strengths is linked with wellbeing. Effects are modest.
try: Ask: “When do I feel most like myself, and what am I doing then?”

Noticing your strengths (patience, humor, organizing, noticing details) helps you use them on purpose, and balance the natural focus on what’s going wrong.

Strengths are also useful in dividing household or team jobs: people often enjoy what they’re good at.

Program: Know your own wiring includes the strengths that come with different wiring.

## Getting to know your stress signature
aka: stress signature; my stress signs; how I act under stress
see: Body clues of emotion; Hot buttons
go: /workpapers/wp-02-battery-stress-meter.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: A practical self-awareness idea. People show stress in different ways; noticing your own early signs helps.
try: Finish the sentence: “When I’m stressed, I tend to…” Share it with someone close.

Everyone has a stress signature: some get quiet, some get busy, some get snappy, some get funny. Knowing yours (and telling others) helps them read you correctly.

It also gives you an early warning to check your battery.

Program: WP-02 helps you check how much you’re carrying; the Wiring Card lets you share your signature with others.
