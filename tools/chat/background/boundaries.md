# Boundaries

## Kinds of boundaries
aka: types of boundaries; time boundaries; emotional boundaries; physical boundaries
see: Requests versus boundaries; Boundary scripts
go: /library/communication.html#boundaries | Boundaries (Library)
evidence: A common framework from counseling and self-help. The categories are practical, not formal science.
try: Time: “I’m not free on Sunday mornings.” Space: “Please knock first.” Things: “Ask before borrowing my car.”

Boundaries can be about time (when you’re available), space (your room, privacy), things (belongings, money), emotions (what you’re willing to discuss) and digital life (messages, sharing photos).

Thinking about which kind of boundary you need makes it easier to say clearly.

## Requests versus boundaries
aka: request or boundary; boundary versus rule; boundaries are not rules
see: Kinds of boundaries; Boundaries without control
go: /library/communication.html#boundaries | Boundaries (Library)
evidence: A practical distinction discussed by many counselors. It helps avoid using the language of boundaries to control others.
try: Request: “Please don’t call during work.” Boundary: “I don’t answer calls during work; I’ll call you back after 6.”

A request asks someone else to change what they do. A boundary says what you will do. Both are fine, but they’re different.

Boundaries work because they’re in your control: you don’t need the other person to agree. Requests invite a conversation and may get a no.

## Boundaries without control
aka: controlling boundaries; boundaries as control; telling others what to do
see: Requests versus boundaries; Kinds of boundaries
go: /library/communication.html#boundaries | Boundaries (Library)
evidence: A common caution from counselors: the language of boundaries can be misused to control others’ behavior. Healthy boundaries focus on your own actions.
try: Not “You’re not allowed to see your friends,” but “I need us to have one evening a week that’s just ours.”

A boundary is about protecting your wellbeing, not about restricting someone else’s life. “You can’t…” statements aimed at another adult’s choices are rules, and rules imposed on a partner or friend usually harm the relationship.

If you notice a “boundary” is really about controlling someone, it may be a need worth expressing as a request instead.

## Holding a boundary calmly
aka: enforcing a boundary; following through; broken record
see: Boundary scripts; Guilt after setting a boundary
go: /workpapers/wp-01.html | WP-01: Kind ways to say no
evidence: Assertiveness training has long used the “broken record” technique (calmly repeating your position). Consistency is widely recommended.
try: “I understand. I’m still not able to host this year.” Repeat, kindly, as needed.

Setting a boundary is one step; holding it is another. People may push back, especially at first. Calmly repeating your position, without over-explaining, is usually more effective than arguing.

You don’t need to justify a boundary endlessly. One clear reason, if you want to give one, is enough.

## Guilt after setting a boundary
aka: guilty for saying no; boundary guilt; feeling bad after a boundary
see: Holding a boundary calmly; Self-talk
go: /library/emotions.html#shame-and-guilt | Shame and guilt (Library)
evidence: Many people report guilt after setting limits, especially if they were raised to put others first. Guilt alone doesn’t mean the boundary was wrong.
try: Tell yourself: “Feeling guilty doesn’t mean I did something wrong. It means I’m doing something new.”

It’s very common to feel guilty after saying no or setting a limit, especially with family. Guilt can be a sign you’re breaking an old habit, not that you’ve done harm.

It usually fades as the new boundary becomes normal. Being kind to yourself during that stretch helps.

## Boundaries with parents as an adult
aka: adult children and parents; boundaries with mom; boundaries with parents
see: Kinds of boundaries; Family roles
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Family research describes renegotiating relationships with parents as a normal part of adulthood. Specific approaches depend on culture and family.
try: “I love that you want to help. I’ll ask for advice when I need it.”

As adults, many people need to reshape how they relate to their parents: how often to talk, how much advice to take, how visits work. Old roles can pull you back into being treated (or acting) like a child.

Kind, consistent limits, together with warmth, often help both sides settle into an adult-to-adult relationship. Cultural expectations vary a lot, and there’s no single right way.

## Boundaries at work
aka: work boundaries; after hours messages; work life boundaries
see: Kinds of boundaries; Response time expectations
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#coworkers | Where coworkers and teams start
evidence: Research on work-life boundaries suggests clearer separation is linked with better recovery for many people, though preferences differ.
try: “I don’t check messages after 7. If it’s urgent, please call.”

At work, boundaries might mean not answering messages after hours, protecting focus time, or saying no to extra projects when you’re at capacity.

Framing them around doing good work (“I do my best work when I have focus time in the mornings”) usually lands well.

## Boundary scripts
aka: boundary scripts; what to say; boundary phrases
see: Holding a boundary calmly; Kinds of boundaries
go: /workpapers/wp-01.html | WP-01: Kind ways to say no
evidence: Practical phrases drawn from assertiveness and communication training. Wording should fit your own voice.
try: “That doesn’t work for me, but here’s what I can do…”

Having a few phrases ready makes boundaries easier to say in the moment. Some examples: “I’m not able to do that.” “That doesn’t work for me.” “I need to think about it.” “I’d love to help, and I can’t this week.”

Keep them short, kind and firm. Adding an alternative, where you have one, helps the other person move forward.

## Boundaries with kids
aka: limits with kids; setting limits with children; kind but firm
see: Natural and logical consequences; Emotion coaching
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Parenting research consistently links warm, consistent limits with good outcomes for children (often called authoritative parenting).
try: “I know you want to keep playing. It’s bedtime. You can choose which book we read.”

Children need clear limits that are held warmly. Acknowledging the feeling (“you’re disappointed”) while keeping the limit (“and it’s still bedtime”) teaches both empathy and structure.

Offering small choices within the limit helps kids feel some control.

## Porous and rigid boundaries
aka: porous boundaries; rigid boundaries; healthy boundaries balance
see: Kinds of boundaries; Boundaries without control
go: /library/communication.html#boundaries | Boundaries (Library)
evidence: A popular counseling framework describing boundaries as more open or more closed. It’s descriptive, not a measured scale.
try: Notice: do I say yes to everything (porous), or keep everyone at arm’s length (rigid)?

Some people have very open boundaries: they take on others’ problems, struggle to say no and overshare. Others have very closed ones: they keep people at a distance and rarely ask for help.

Most of us are a mix, and it varies by relationship. The aim isn’t a perfect setting but flexibility: open where it’s safe and helpful, firmer where it’s needed.
