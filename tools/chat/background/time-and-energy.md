# Time and energy

## Eisenhower matrix
aka: urgent important matrix; Eisenhower box; urgent versus important
see: Time blocking; Weekly planning together
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: Named after a remark attributed to Dwight Eisenhower and popularized by Stephen Covey. It’s a planning tool, not a research-tested method.
try: Sort today’s list into four boxes: urgent and important, important not urgent, urgent not important, neither.

The Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks by urgency and importance. Important but not urgent tasks (planning, relationships, rest) often get crowded out by urgent ones, which is why they’re worth protecting.

Urgent but unimportant tasks can often be delegated or simplified.

Program: Today’s Weather tells you what today is good for; the matrix helps you choose what to do with it.

## Time blocking
aka: time blocking; calendar blocking; blocking time
see: Eisenhower matrix; Buffers between tasks
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: A popular productivity technique. Research on planning suggests specific plans (when and where) help follow-through.
try: Block Sunday 10 to 11 for household admin, and put it in the shared calendar.

Time blocking means scheduling specific blocks for specific tasks, instead of working from an endless list. It helps important tasks actually happen.

For households, blocking shared time (a weekly planning slot, a cleaning hour) makes sure it doesn’t get crowded out.

Program: Implementation intentions (in the Library) explain why “when and where” plans work.

## Buffers between tasks
aka: buffer time; transition time; padding the schedule
see: Time blocking; Transition warnings
go: /library/thinking.html#planning-fallacy | The planning fallacy (Library)
evidence: The planning fallacy (tasks take longer than we expect) is well documented. Buffers are a practical response.
try: Add 15 minutes between appointments, and leave earlier than you think you need to.

Back-to-back plans leave no room for delays, so one late thing makes everything late, and stress climbs. Small buffers between tasks absorb surprises.

They also give time to switch gears, which some people find especially hard.

Program: The Library’s planning fallacy entry explains why we underestimate time.

## Weekly planning together
aka: weekly planning; Sunday planning; family planning meeting
see: Family meetings; Co-parenting calendar
go: /workpapers/wp-13-pll-protocol.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: Practical guidance used by many families and couples. Shared planning reduces surprise conflicts.
try: Sunday, 15 minutes: look at the week’s calendar, meals, who’s doing what, and one fun thing.

A short weekly planning session helps a household see the week ahead: appointments, deadlines, who’s doing drop-offs, what’s for dinner. Surprises cause a lot of friction.

Keep it short and practical, and end with something to look forward to.

Program: The program’s weekly closing is a similar short review. The daily check-in handles day-to-day.

## Weekly review
aka: weekly review; end of week review; closing the week
see: Weekly planning together; The done list
go: /workpapers/report-01.html | REPORT-01: Your progress, week by week
evidence: Popularized by David Allen’s Getting Things Done (2001). It’s a practical habit; formal research is limited.
try: Friday afternoon: clear your inbox, check your list, note what’s done, and plan next week’s top three.

A weekly review is a regular time to catch up: clear loose ends, check what’s done, and plan what matters next week. It reduces the feeling of things slipping.

For households, the same idea works as a short end-of-week closing.

## The done list
aka: done list; ta-da list; what I got done
see: Weekly review; Celebrating small steps
go: /library/motivation.html#small-wins | Small wins (Library)
evidence: A popular practice linked to research on small wins and progress. Evidence for the specific practice is anecdotal.
try: At the end of the day, write three things you got done, even small ones.

A to-do list is never finished. A done list records what you actually accomplished, which helps you notice progress, especially invisible work.

It can also help partners see each other’s effort.

## Batching errands
aka: batching; batch tasks; grouping errands
see: Time blocking; Energy audit
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: A common productivity tip. Research on task switching suggests grouping similar tasks reduces switching costs.
try: One trip on Saturday for groceries, pharmacy and post office.

Grouping similar tasks (errands, calls, emails, admin) saves time and mental energy spent switching between them.

It also makes it easier to hand off a whole batch to one person.

## Parkinson’s law
aka: Parkinson’s law; work expands to fill time
see: Time blocking; Buffers between tasks
go: /library/thinking.html#planning-fallacy | The planning fallacy (Library)
evidence: C. Northcote Parkinson wrote in 1955 that “work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion”. It began as a humorous observation, not a scientific law.
try: “Let’s give the garage clean-out two hours, not the whole Saturday.”

Tasks often stretch to fill whatever time you give them. Setting a reasonable time limit can make a task feel more manageable and leave room for rest.

It’s a rule of thumb, not a rule. Some tasks really do need more time.

Program: Timeboxing (in the conversation notes) applies the same idea to hard talks.

## Chunking big tasks
aka: break it down; chunking; smaller steps
see: Procrastination and the first step; Tiny habits
go: /library/motivation.html#procrastination | Procrastination (Library)
evidence: Breaking goals into smaller steps is widely recommended and consistent with research on goal setting and procrastination.
try: “Sort the garage” becomes: clear one shelf, then one box, then take one bag to donate.

Big tasks feel overwhelming, which makes them easy to avoid. Breaking them into small, concrete chunks makes starting easier and progress visible.

Each chunk should be small enough to finish in one go.

## Shared to-do lists
aka: shared list; family to-do list; shared task list
see: Weekly planning together; Planning, doing and following through
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Practical guidance. Shared lists reduce reliance on one person’s memory, a key part of the mental load.
try: One shared list for the household, with each item tagged with an owner.

A shared list (on the fridge or in an app) gets tasks out of one person’s head. Anyone can add to it, and everyone can see what’s pending.

Adding an owner to each item keeps the list from becoming one person’s job to manage.

Program: The mental load often lives in one person’s memory. A shared list with owners (like WP-03) spreads it.

## Protecting peak hours
aka: peak hours; best time of day; energy peaks
see: Energy audit; Time blocking
go: /library/wiring.html#chronotypes | Chronotypes (Library)
evidence: Research on chronotypes shows people differ in when they feel most alert. Matching demanding tasks to your better hours is common advice.
try: “I’m sharpest in the morning, so I’ll do the tax forms at 9, not 9 at night.”

Most people have times of day when they think best. Using those for demanding tasks (and hard conversations) makes a difference.

In households, partners may have different peak hours, which is worth knowing when planning talks.

Program: The Library’s chronotypes entry covers morning and evening types. Frequency Calibration maps shared rhythms.

## Different paces
aka: different paces; fast and slow; pace mismatch; out of sync
see: Impatience; Time attitudes across cultures
go: /tools/frequency-calibration.html | Frequency Calibration
evidence: The program’s Frequency Framework describes pace mismatch as a common, blameless source of friction. General research on individual differences (for example in chronotype and processing speed) supports the idea that people naturally run at different speeds.
try: “You like to decide fast and I like to think overnight. Could we agree that big things get a day?”

People run at different paces: some talk, decide and reply quickly; others need more time. Neither is wrong, but a mismatch can feel like pressure on one side and stalling on the other.

Naming the difference and agreeing a middle rhythm (a set time to decide, a regular check-in) usually works better than each person pushing their own speed.

Program: Frequency Calibration maps your natural pace across five areas and suggests agreements. Chapter I explains how small pace differences create a “squeal”.
