# Negotiation basics

## Anchoring in negotiation
aka: anchoring; first offer; anchor
see: Making the first offer; Framing gains and losses
go: /library/conflict.html#principled-negotiation | Principled negotiation (Library)
evidence: Anchoring is one of the best-replicated findings in judgment research, going back to Tversky and Kahneman (1974). Its size in real negotiations varies with how much people know about fair values.
try: If a housemate opens with “I’ll pay 20% of the rent for the small room,” that number becomes the reference point, even if 30% might be fairer.

The first number or proposal put on the table tends to pull the final agreement toward it. People adjust from the anchor, but usually not enough.

In everyday life, this means the first suggestion about a split, a price or a schedule carries extra weight. Knowing that helps you avoid being pulled too far by someone else’s opening, and reminds you to base proposals on something fair and explainable.

Program: The Lemonade Stand and a week of WP-01 give you real numbers to start from, so the anchor is a fact rather than a guess.

## Making the first offer
aka: first offer; who goes first; opening proposal
see: Anchoring in negotiation; Using fair standards
go: /lemonade-stand.html | The Lemonade Stand
evidence: Negotiation research generally finds that making the first offer can help when you have good information, because of anchoring. It can backfire if you know little about fair values.
try: “Based on the hours we logged, I’d suggest I take the cooking and you take laundry and bins. What do you think?”

Going first lets you set the frame, which is helpful when you’ve done your homework. If you’re unsure what’s fair, it can be wiser to ask for the other person’s view first or gather facts together.

At home, a first offer works best when it’s explained, not just stated: “here’s what I suggest, and here’s why”. It invites a counter-proposal rather than a standoff.

Program: Bring the Lemonade Stand split to the conversation, so any first offer is grounded in the week you both actually had.

## Walk-away point
aka: reservation point; bottom line; walkaway point; limit
see: Anchoring in negotiation; The zone of possible agreement
go: /library/conflict.html#batna | BATNA (Library)
evidence: A standard concept in negotiation teaching. It’s a planning tool rather than a research finding.
try: Before a talk about who hosts the holidays, decide privately: “I can do one of the two big days, but not both.”

Your walk-away point is the least you can accept before you’d rather take a different path. Knowing it before a negotiation keeps you from agreeing to something in the moment that you’ll resent later.

In relationships, this isn’t about threats. It’s about honesty with yourself: what can you genuinely live with? Saying it kindly (“I can’t do both days this year”) is fairer than agreeing and quietly resenting it.

Program: WP-01’s kind ways to say no help you express a limit calmly: say why the request is fair, what you have left, and what you can offer instead.

## The zone of possible agreement
aka: ZOPA; zone of possible agreement; overlap
see: Walk-away point; Trading across issues
go: /library/conflict.html#interests-vs-positions | Interests versus positions (Library)
evidence: A standard negotiation-teaching concept. Useful for thinking, not a measurement.
try: One roommate wants quiet by 10, the other by midnight. Anything from 10 to 11 might work for both on weeknights.

The zone of possible agreement is the overlap between what each side can accept. If there’s overlap, a deal is possible. If there isn’t, you need to change the question, often by adding other issues or looking at underlying interests.

Thinking this way turns a standoff into a search: where could we both be okay? It also reminds you that “no overlap” today might mean “different options”, not “impossible”.

Program: Frequency Calibration does something similar for rhythms: it shows the gap between your natural pace in each area, and suggests one agreement to close it.

## Trading across issues
aka: logrolling; trading; package deals; swaps
see: The zone of possible agreement; If-then packages
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Research on integrative negotiation shows that trading on issues people value differently often creates better outcomes for both sides than splitting each issue down the middle.
try: “I really hate cleaning the bathroom but don’t mind cooking. You hate cooking. Want to swap?”

People care about different things by different amounts. Instead of splitting every job 50/50, you can trade: each person takes more of what they mind less, in exchange for less of what they dread.

This often feels fairer than equal splits, because it respects preferences. The key is being honest about what you actually mind, and checking the overall load still feels reasonable.

Program: WP-03 is the perfect place to do this: when giving each job an owner, ask who minds it least, not just whose turn it is.

## If-then packages
aka: package proposal; if you then I; conditional offers
see: Trading across issues; One ask at a time
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A practical technique from negotiation training. It’s common sense more than research, and works best when both parts are specific.
try: “If you can take Tuesday and Thursday pickups, I’ll handle all the school emails.”

An if-then package links two things together so both sides get something: “If you do X, I’ll do Y.” It makes a request feel like a trade rather than a demand.

Keep it light and fair, and avoid turning every favor into a transaction. Used occasionally, it’s a friendly way to rebalance things without keeping score.

Program: Write any package deal into WP-03 so it doesn’t rely on memory, and look again in a month.

## Using fair standards
aka: objective criteria; fair standards; outside benchmarks
see: Anchoring in negotiation; Trading across issues
go: /library/conflict.html#principled-negotiation | Principled negotiation (Library)
evidence: Using independent standards is a central idea in principled negotiation, described by Roger Fisher and William Ury in Getting to Yes (1981).
try: “Let’s split rent by room size, measured in square feet, rather than just arguing about it.”

When two people disagree about what’s fair, pointing to a standard neither of you invented (room size, hours logged, income share, what’s typical) can take the heat out of it. You’re no longer arguing about who’s right, just about which standard fits.

Agree on the standard before you apply it. Picking the standard after you see the numbers invites cherry-picking.

Program: Chapter IV, Two kinds of fair, is about agreeing what “fair” means before you argue about it: results, or doing what each of you said.

## Separating ideas from decisions
aka: brainstorm then decide; inventing before deciding; no judging ideas
see: Using fair standards; Consensus and consent
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Recommended in principled negotiation and creative problem-solving. Research on brainstorming is mixed about group idea quality, but separating the stages reduces early defensiveness.
try: “Let’s list every idea for the Saturday problem, even silly ones, and only then pick.”

When every idea gets judged the moment it’s said, people stop offering ideas. Separating the stages (first list options freely, then decide) keeps a conversation creative and less tense.

It’s especially useful for stuck, repeating problems, where the obvious options have all been tried and argued about.

Program: WP-04, the monthly look-back, sorts each repeat problem by type first, before deciding what to change.

## Framing gains and losses
aka: framing effect; gain frame; loss frame
see: Loss aversion; Anchoring in negotiation
go: /library/thinking.html | Thinking traps (Library)
evidence: The framing effect was demonstrated by Tversky and Kahneman (1981). The same choice described as a gain or a loss can shift people’s preferences.
try: “If we get the chores sorted, we get our Sundays back” lands differently from “If we don’t sort this, Sundays are ruined.”

How you describe an option changes how it feels. Framing a change as something you’ll gain together tends to invite cooperation. Framing it as a loss or threat tends to trigger defensiveness.

This isn’t about spin. It’s about choosing honest words that point toward what you both want.

Program: Starting from a shared goal in a check-in is a gain frame: here’s what we’re building, together.

## Splitting the difference
aka: meet in the middle; split it 50 50; halfway
see: Trading across issues; Compromise and its limits
go: /library/conflict.html#integrative-distributive | Win-win and win-lose (Library)
evidence: Negotiation research suggests splitting the difference is quick but often leaves value on the table compared with exploring interests. It can be perfectly fine for small, low-stakes issues.
try: For choosing a movie, meeting in the middle is fine. For who does the night feeds, look at sleep, work and preferences first.

Meeting in the middle feels fair and is fast, which is why it’s so common. For small things, it’s often the right call.

For bigger or recurring issues, it can produce an arrangement nobody really likes. Looking at what each person actually needs (sleep, time, money, predictability) often reveals a better option than halfway.

Program: The Lemonade Stand shows the split as a fact. An even split isn’t the goal in itself: what matters is whether both of you agree it works.

## Negotiating with deadlines
aka: deadline pressure; time pressure in negotiation
see: Timeboxing a conversation; Sleep on it
go: /quick-checks.html#today | Today’s Weather
evidence: Negotiation research finds deadlines speed up concessions, sometimes leading to worse agreements. Stress and time pressure also narrow attention.
try: “We don’t have to decide the summer plan tonight. Can we both think and decide Sunday?”

Deadlines can help a stuck decision move, but artificial urgency (“we need to decide right now”) often leads to agreements that fall apart later.

If a decision doesn’t truly need to be made now, giving it a little time, and choosing a calmer moment, usually leads to something both people can stick to.

Program: WP-02’s advice for a high battery score is to put off anything that doesn’t need deciding in the next hour.

## Negotiating household jobs by preference
aka: chore preferences; who minds it least; dread ranking
see: Trading across issues; Chore charts that work
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: A practical application of integrative negotiation to housework. There’s no strong research on this specific method, but preference-based trades generally improve satisfaction.
try: Each of you ranks the jobs from “don’t mind” to “really dread”, then start by giving each person their “don’t mind” jobs.

Instead of dividing chores by turns or tradition, each person rates how much they mind each job. Jobs one person doesn’t mind (and the other dreads) are easy wins.

Dreaded-by-everyone jobs can rotate, be shared, be done together, or sometimes be outsourced. The time each job takes still matters, so check the overall split afterwards.

Program: Put the ranking next to the Lemonade Stand hours, then give each job one owner in WP-03.

## Concessions that build trust
aka: concessions; giving ground; reciprocity in negotiation
see: If-then packages; Trading across issues
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Research on reciprocity suggests concessions tend to be matched, which is why small good-faith moves can unlock a stuck conversation. It isn’t guaranteed.
try: “I’ll let go of the weekday dishes thing. Could you meet me on the Saturday cleaning?”

A small concession offered freely (“I can bend on this part”) often invites the other person to bend somewhere too. It signals that you’re trying to find a solution, not win.

Name concessions out loud so they’re noticed, and avoid giving up something that genuinely matters to you just to end the tension. That tends to come back as resentment.

Program: Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives: fairness and thanks keep things steady. A named concession, and a thank-you for theirs, both help.
