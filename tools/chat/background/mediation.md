# Mediation and third parties

## What a mediator does
aka: mediator role; what is a mediator; mediation explained
see: Informal mediation between friends or family; Reframing in mediation; When to bring in a third party
go: /library/conflict.html#mediation-basics | Mediation basics (Library)
evidence: Mediation is well established in family, workplace and community settings. Studies generally find good satisfaction rates, though results depend on the case and the mediator.
try: A mediator might say: “Let me check I have both views. Sam, you need the rent on the 1st. Alex, your pay comes on the 5th. So the question is timing, not whether it gets paid. Is that fair?”

A mediator is a neutral third person who helps people in a disagreement talk to each other and find their own solution. They don’t decide who’s right, and they don’t impose an answer.

Mediators mostly manage the process: ground rules, turn-taking, summarizing each side, spotting shared interests, and helping people move from positions (“I want X”) to what they actually need. Formal mediators are trained professionals; informally, a trusted friend or colleague sometimes plays a similar role.

Program: The Check-ins guide borrows mediator moves you can use yourselves: one topic, same side, hear it back before you answer.

## Ground rules for a tough talk
aka: ground rules; conversation rules; rules for fighting fair
see: What a mediator does; Fighting fair
go: /check-ins.html#ground | Check-in ground rules
evidence: Mediators and facilitators routinely set ground rules; research specifically on their effect is limited, but they give people a shared way to call a pause without blame.
try: “No interrupting, no ‘always’ or ‘never’, one topic, and either of us can call a twenty-minute break.”

Ground rules are a few simple agreements made before a hard talk: how you’ll take turns, what words to avoid, how to pause. Agreeing them in a calm moment gives you both something neutral to point to when things heat up.

Keep the list short (three to five rules) and make them about behavior, not attitude. “No raised voices” is easier to keep than “be respectful”.

Program: The program’s ground rules are on the Check-ins page, and the Signal Translator’s talking notes start with a similar opener.

## Reframing in mediation
aka: reframing; neutral reframe; restating neutrally
see: What a mediator does; Paraphrasing
go: /workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: A core mediator skill described in mediation training. Evidence is mostly from practice, but it maps closely onto research on how blame language raises defensiveness.
try: “He never lifts a finger” reframed as: “So you’d like more of the daily jobs shared.”

Reframing takes a charged statement and restates it in neutral terms that keep the need but drop the attack. “She’s so controlling about money” becomes “So how you make money decisions together really matters to you.”

You can reframe your own sentences before you say them, and gently reframe what you hear. It helps the conversation stay on the problem rather than on each other.

Program: WP-09 is essentially a self-reframe: fact, feeling and ask instead of a verdict on the person.

## Caucus
aka: caucus; private sessions; separate meetings in mediation
see: What a mediator does; Shuttle conversations
go: /library/conflict.html#mediation-basics | Mediation basics (Library)
evidence: A standard mediation technique. Practitioners report it helps people speak openly, though some mediators prefer to avoid it to keep things transparent.
try: In a family disagreement, a relative might chat with each sibling separately first: “What matters most to you here? What could you live with?”

A caucus is a private conversation between the mediator and one side, away from the other. People sometimes say things privately (fears, what they could actually accept) that they wouldn’t say in front of the other person.

At home, the informal version is simply talking to each person separately before bringing everyone together. It can lower the temperature, as long as nobody feels talked about behind their back.

Program: The monthly look-back (WP-04) and house meetings go better when each person has had a chance to think on their own first.

## Informal mediation between friends or family
aka: helping two people make up; being the go-between; friend mediator
see: What a mediator does; Triangulation
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: Informal third-party help is common and often useful; research also warns that go-betweens can get pulled into taking sides. Staying neutral is harder than it looks.
try: “I’m not going to take sides. Would you both be up for sitting down together, and I’ll just help keep it to one topic?”

Sometimes a friend, sibling or colleague helps two people talk. It can be very helpful if the helper stays neutral, sets a simple structure, and hands the solution back to the two people.

The risk is becoming a messenger or a judge. Passing messages back and forth, or quietly agreeing with each side separately, usually makes things worse. The healthiest role is helping them talk directly.

Program: The family systems idea of triangulation explains why the go-between role gets messy. With co-parents especially, never use children as messengers.

## Shuttle conversations
aka: shuttle diplomacy; passing messages; back and forth through someone
see: Caucus; Triangulation
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Used in formal mediation when people can’t yet sit together. At home, relying on messengers tends to distort messages and keep conflict going.
try: Instead of “Tell your dad…”, try messaging him directly: “Can we sort out the holiday plan, just us?”

In formal settings, a mediator sometimes shuttles between people who can’t be in the same room. It can be a useful first step when tensions are very high.

In families, though, message-passing through a third person often turns into a game of telephone, and puts the messenger in an unfair spot, especially if the messenger is a child. Moving to direct conversation as soon as possible is usually better.

Program: For co-parents, keep communication brief, factual and direct between the adults, and never through the kids.

## When to bring in a third party
aka: getting outside help; bring in help; outside perspective
see: What a mediator does; Informal mediation between friends or family
go: /is-this-for-you.html | Is this right for you?
evidence: General guidance from mediation and counseling practice. There’s no single rule; the right help depends on the situation.
try: “We keep getting stuck on this. Would you be open to having someone neutral help us talk it through?”

If the same disagreement keeps going nowhere, if talking always escalates, or if decisions have big consequences (money, separation arrangements, care for a family member), a neutral third party can help. That might be a trusted friend, a workplace mediator, a professional mediator or a counselor.

Asking for outside help isn’t failure. It’s a way of protecting the relationship from a pattern you can’t break alone. If anything about the situation feels unsafe, please talk with a qualified professional.

Program: The program isn’t therapy or mediation. It’s a practical, shared way to see the load and talk kindly, and it sits happily alongside professional support.

## Fighting fair
aka: fair fighting; fighting fair rules; argue respectfully
see: Ground rules for a tough talk; Conflict escalation ladder
go: /check-ins.html#ground | Check-in ground rules
evidence: “Fair fighting” lists come from popular psychology and couples work. The most consistent research-backed pieces are avoiding contempt and criticism of the person, and taking breaks when flooded.
try: Agree three rules today: one topic, no name-calling, and a twenty-minute break when either of you asks.

Fighting fair means disagreeing in a way that protects the relationship: talking about the problem, not the person’s character; no name-calling or contempt; no bringing up every old grievance; and taking breaks when things get too hot.

Disagreement itself isn’t the problem. Many close relationships have plenty of it. What matters most is how it’s done, and whether repair happens afterward.

Program: The Library’s entries on the four horsemen and repair attempts go deeper. The Check-ins ground rules are a ready-made fair-fighting list.

## Neutral language
aka: neutral words; loaded words; blame words
see: Reframing in mediation; Feelings versus thoughts
go: /signal-translator.html | The Signal Translator
evidence: Communication and mediation training consistently recommend avoiding absolutes and labels. The Conversation Reader looks for the same kinds of words.
try: Swap “You’re so lazy about the dishes” for “The dishes from last night are still in the sink.”

Some words reliably raise the temperature: always, never, lazy, selfish, ridiculous, typical. Neutral language describes what happened without adding judgments or labels.

It isn’t about being cold. You can still say how you feel. It’s about making sure the facts part of your message is something the other person can agree is true.

Program: WP-09 asks for the fact “in one sentence with no adjectives”. The Conversation Reader flags absolutes like “always” and “never”.

## Family meetings
aka: family meeting; household meeting; house meeting
see: Retrospective for households; House meetings for roommates
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Family meetings are recommended by many parenting approaches, including Adlerian parenting. Evidence is mostly practical, but they give children a voice and make household agreements visible.
try: Sunday, 20 minutes: something good from the week, the calendar ahead, one problem to solve together, and a treat.

A family meeting is a short, regular sit-down where everyone (kids included, at their level) looks at the week ahead, shares appreciations and solves one problem together.

Keep it brief and predictable, start with something positive, and let kids suggest solutions. When children help make a rule, they’re usually more willing to follow it.

Program: Use it to update WP-03 (one owner per job) and to agree age-appropriate jobs. For very busy families, even ten minutes a week helps.
