# Parenting and friendship

These add to the Library’s entries on parenting, teenagers and friendship.

## Emotion coaching
aka: emotion coaching; helping kids with feelings; naming kids’ feelings
see: Natural and logical consequences; Boundaries with kids
go: /library/life.html#parenting-styles | Parenting styles (Library)
evidence: John Gottman described emotion coaching in Raising an Emotionally Intelligent Child (1997). Research suggests children whose parents acknowledge and help name feelings tend to regulate emotions better.
try: “You’re really disappointed we can’t go to the park. That makes sense. We can go tomorrow.”

Emotion coaching means noticing a child’s feelings, naming them, accepting them, and then helping with limits and problem-solving. All feelings are allowed; not all behaviors are.

It helps children learn to understand and manage their own emotions over time.

Program: The Library’s parenting styles entry covers related research. The family road keeps things warm, short and practical.

## Connect before correct
aka: connect before correct; connection first; calm before correcting
see: Emotion coaching; Talking side by side
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: A popular phrase from parenting educators. It’s consistent with research suggesting children learn better when calm and connected.
try: Kneel down, make gentle eye contact: “Hey, you seem upset. Let’s take a breath.” Then talk about the behavior.

When a child is upset, trying to correct them straight away rarely works. A moment of connection first (a calm voice, getting to their level, naming the feeling) helps them settle enough to listen.

The same idea works with adults: people hear feedback better when they feel connected.

Program: Pillar III, Read your state first, applies to children too: calm first, then talk.

## Natural and logical consequences
aka: natural consequences; logical consequences; consequences for kids
see: Boundaries with kids; Emotion coaching
go: /library/life.html#parenting-styles | Parenting styles (Library)
evidence: These ideas come from Adlerian parenting (Rudolf Dreikurs and others). They’re widely used; research generally supports consistent, related consequences over harsh punishment.
try: “If the bike is left out in the rain, it’ll get rusty.” Or: “If the toys aren’t put away, they go on the shelf until tomorrow.”

Natural consequences happen on their own (forget your coat, feel cold). Logical consequences are set by adults but related to the behavior (misuse the tablet, lose it for the evening).

Both teach responsibility better than unrelated punishments, especially when delivered calmly.

Program: Giving kids real jobs they own (WP-03) pairs with calm consequences instead of reminders.

## Age-sized jobs for kids
aka: chores for kids; age appropriate chores; kids helping at home
see: Chore charts that work; Natural and logical consequences
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Parenting research suggests helping at home builds children’s competence and sense of belonging. Specific ages vary by child.
try: Young kids: set the table, feed the pet. Older kids: pack lunches, laundry. Teens: cook a meal a week.

Children can help from a young age, with jobs that grow with them. Owning a real job builds skills and a sense of belonging.

It works best when jobs are clear, consistent and theirs, not assigned randomly with nagging.

Program: WP-03 can include kids as owners of age-sized jobs. The program’s rule: never ask a child to keep track of what the adults do.

## Sibling fights
aka: siblings fighting; kids arguing; sibling conflict
see: Emotion coaching; Family meetings
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#family | Where families start
evidence: Research on sibling conflict suggests it’s very common. Parents helping children solve problems (rather than always judging) tends to build skills.
try: “You both want the tablet. What could we do so it works for both of you?”

Sibling fights are normal. Instead of acting as judge every time, parents can help kids name feelings and find solutions together.

Stepping in firmly when anyone is getting hurt is always appropriate.

Program: Family meetings are a good place to set shared rules for common flashpoints.

## Screen agreements with kids
aka: screen time rules; screen agreements; tech rules for kids
see: Family meetings; Natural and logical consequences
go: /library/calm.html#phones-and-presence | Phones and presence (Library)
evidence: Research on children and screens is mixed and debated. Many experts suggest focusing on content, context and balance rather than just hours.
try: Agree together: screens off at dinner and an hour before bed, and weekend time for a favorite show.

Rules made together with kids tend to stick better than rules handed down. Focusing on when and what (not just how long) usually works better.

Adults following the same rules (phones away at dinner) makes them fairer.

## Stepfamily basics
aka: stepfamily; blended family basics; stepparenting
see: New partners and co-parenting; Family roles
go: /library/life.html#stepfamilies | Stepfamilies (Library)
evidence: Stepfamily research (for example, Patricia Papernow’s work) suggests blended families take years to settle, and that stepparents often do better building a relationship before taking on discipline.
try: As a stepparent: focus on connection first, and let the biological parent lead on discipline at the start.

Blended families bring together different histories, habits and loyalties. It takes time, often years, to feel like one family.

Going slowly, keeping some one-on-one time between parents and their own kids, and building stepparent relationships gradually all help.

## Reaching out after drifting apart
aka: reconnect with a friend; drifted apart friend; getting back in touch
see: Friendship across life stages; Supporting a friend through a hard time
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#friends | Where friends start
evidence: Research (for example, Peggy Liu and colleagues, 2022) found people underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to.
try: “Hey, I was thinking about you today. How are you doing?”

Friendships often fade through busy lives, not conflict. Reaching out, even after a long gap, is usually welcomed more than we expect.

A simple, low-pressure message is enough to start.

Program: Turning Toward includes small ways to reach for someone, including old friends.

## Friendship across life stages
aka: friendships changing; friends and new babies; friends after moving
see: Reaching out after drifting apart; Uneven effort in friendships
go: /library/connection.html#friendship | Friendship (Library)
evidence: Research suggests friendships change with life stages (moves, jobs, children), and that maintaining them takes intentional effort.
try: “I know life is busy with the baby. Could I bring lunch over on Saturday?”

Friendships change as lives change. A friend with a new baby, a new job or a move may have less time, and it’s usually not personal.

Adapting (shorter visits, calls instead of nights out, joining their routine) keeps the connection going through changing seasons.

Program: The Library’s friendship entry covers what keeps friendships going.

## Uneven effort in friendships
aka: one sided friendship; always the one who reaches out; friendship effort
see: Friendship across life stages; Reaching out after drifting apart
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#friends | Where friends start
evidence: Research on friendship suggests reciprocity matters for satisfaction, though what feels balanced varies between people.
try: “I love our friendship, and I’ve noticed I’m usually the one who reaches out. Could you suggest the next plan?”

If you’re always the one who reaches out, it can feel lonely. Sometimes the other person simply has a different style or less capacity, and doesn’t realize.

Naming it kindly, without blame, gives them a chance to meet you. How they respond helps you decide how much to invest.

Program: The friends road suggests saying what you’d like plainly and kindly.

## Supporting a friend through a hard time
aka: supporting a friend; helping a friend; being there for a friend
see: Asking permission before giving advice; Validation before solutions
go: /library/connection.html#social-support | Social support (Library)
evidence: Research on social support suggests people value feeling listened to and practical help, and that unsolicited advice can backfire.
try: “I’m here. Want to talk, or want me to bring dinner?”

When a friend is going through something hard, being present often matters more than having the right words. Listening, practical help and staying in touch all count.

If you’re worried about a friend’s safety or wellbeing, encourage them to reach out to someone who can help properly, like a qualified professional.
