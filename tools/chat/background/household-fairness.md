# Fairness and household work

These add practical detail to the Library’s fairness entries.

## Chore charts that work
aka: chore chart; chore rota; chore schedule; cleaning schedule
see: Rotation or specialization; Negotiating household jobs by preference
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Practical advice drawn from household and parenting guidance. There’s little formal research on specific chart designs; clear ownership and visibility are the common threads.
try: One sheet on the fridge: each job, how often, who owns it, and what “done” looks like.

The chore charts that last tend to share a few features: every job has a named owner, the list is visible, “done” is clearly defined, and the chart is reviewed occasionally rather than rewritten weekly.

Charts fail when they list jobs without owners (“someone should clean the bathroom”), when they’re too ambitious, or when one person ends up policing everyone else.

Program: WP-03 is a chore chart with two names per job: who does it and who keeps an eye on it. Review it at the weekly closing.

## Rotation or specialization
aka: rotate chores; take turns; specialize; fixed jobs
see: Chore charts that work; Negotiating household jobs by preference
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Both approaches are common. Specialization lets people get good at a job and avoids re-deciding; rotation spreads dreaded jobs. No single approach is best for every household.
try: Fixed owners for most jobs, and a rotation just for the two jobs everyone dislikes.

Some households give each job a permanent owner (specialization). Others rotate jobs each week. Fixed owners reduce reminders and let each person build a routine. Rotation shares the unpleasant jobs and means everyone knows how to do everything.

Many households mix the two: fixed owners for most things, rotation for the jobs nobody wants.

Program: WP-03 works with either. If you rotate, write down whose week it is, so the rotation itself has an owner.

## Planning, doing and following through
aka: conception planning execution; plan do follow through; whole job ownership; CPE
see: The Fair Play card idea; Kinkeeping
go: /library/fairness.html#mental-load | The mental load (Library)
evidence: Eve Rodsky’s book Fair Play (2019) describes owning a task’s “conception, planning and execution”. It’s a practical framework rather than a research finding, but it matches research on the mental load.
try: Owning “school lunches” means noticing supplies are low, planning the week, shopping, and packing, not just packing when asked.

Many jobs have hidden parts: noticing it needs doing, planning it, and then doing it. When one person does the noticing and planning and hands out the doing, the load looks shared but isn’t.

Handing over a whole job, from noticing to finishing, is often what actually lightens the load.

Program: WP-03 asks for a Responsible and an Accountable name; for jobs like this, make sure the owner owns the noticing too.

## The Fair Play card idea
aka: Fair Play; Fair Play cards; Eve Rodsky
see: Planning, doing and following through; Chore charts that work
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Fair Play is a popular book and card system by Eve Rodsky (2019). It’s practical guidance, not a research study. Many households find the ideas useful; others find the system too detailed.
try: Write each household job on a card, deal them out based on preference and capacity, and revisit the deal every few months.

Fair Play turns household jobs into cards that partners “deal” between them, with each card including the full job from noticing to finishing. The idea is to make invisible work visible and to hand over whole jobs.

You don’t need the product to use the idea. A written list of jobs with full owners does much the same thing.

Program: The Lemonade Stand and WP-03 are the program’s way of doing this: see all the jobs, then give each one a whole owner.

## Kinkeeping
aka: kinkeeping; kin keeping; keeping in touch with family; birthday cards
see: Planning, doing and following through; Holiday load
go: /library/fairness.html#invisible-work | Invisible work (Library)
evidence: Anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo described “kinkeeping” in 1987: the work of maintaining family ties, often done by women. It’s a well-recognized form of invisible work.
try: Add “birthdays and family cards” as its own job in WP-03, with a named owner.

Kinkeeping is the work of keeping family connections going: remembering birthdays, sending cards, organizing visits, calling relatives, planning holidays. It takes real time and thought but rarely gets counted as work.

Naming it as a job, and deciding who owns it, can relieve one person of an unseen burden.

Program: WP-01 logs invisible jobs like this. The Library’s invisible work entry goes deeper.

## Holiday load
aka: holiday stress; holiday planning load; Christmas load
see: Kinkeeping; Planning, doing and following through
go: /library/life.html#holidays | Holidays (Library)
evidence: Research on holiday stress and household labor suggests holiday preparation often falls unevenly. Specific figures vary by study.
try: Six weeks before: list every holiday job (gifts, cards, food, travel), give each one an owner, and put it in the calendar.

Holidays bring a burst of extra work: gifts, cards, food, travel, hosting, family coordination. Much of it is planning and remembering, and it often falls to one person by default.

Listing holiday jobs early and assigning owners can turn a stressful season into a shared project.

Program: A holiday list works just like WP-03. The Library’s holidays entry covers family tensions too.

## School and kid admin
aka: school admin; permission slips; kid logistics; school emails
see: Planning, doing and following through; Co-parenting calendar
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Research on the mental load of parenting highlights children’s admin (forms, appointments, activities) as a large, often invisible category.
try: Split by child or by category: one person owns school emails and forms, the other owns activities and appointments.

Children bring a steady stream of admin: forms, emails, appointments, activities, clothes that don’t fit, birthday party invitations. It’s easy for one parent to become the default for all of it.

Dividing it into clear categories with owners, and sharing one family calendar, keeps it from falling on one person.

Program: WP-03 can include “school emails and forms” as a named job. For two homes, see the co-parenting notes.

## Pet care load
aka: pet care; dog walking; who feeds the cat
see: Chore charts that work; Rotation or specialization
go: /lemonade-stand.html | The Lemonade Stand
evidence: Practical guidance. Pet care is often left off household lists even though it can take significant daily time.
try: List every pet job (feeding, walks, vet, litter, grooming, meds) with an owner, including a backup for travel.

Pets add daily and occasional jobs that are easy to forget to count: walks, feeding, cleaning up, vet visits, buying food, arranging care when you travel.

Putting pet jobs on the same list as other chores keeps them from quietly landing on the most attached (or most guilty) person.

Program: Add pet jobs to the Lemonade Stand and give each one an owner in WP-03.

## Lowering standards on purpose
aka: good enough; lower the bar; good enough standards
see: Gatekeeping around chores; Different standards of clean
go: /library/motivation.html#perfectionism | Perfectionism (Library)
evidence: Research on perfectionism suggests very high standards can increase stress. Choosing “good enough” for some tasks is common advice, not a tested intervention.
try: “Towels folded any way is fine. They’re clean, that’s the job.”

Sometimes a household argument is about standards: one person wants things done a particular way. Deciding together which jobs need a high standard and which can be “good enough” frees everyone up.

It also makes it easier for others to take over jobs without being corrected.

Program: WP-03 can note what “done” looks like for each job. The Library covers perfectionism.

## Gatekeeping around chores
aka: redoing their work; my way or else; correcting how they do it
see: Lowering standards on purpose; IKEA effect
go: /library/fairness.html#gatekeeping | Gatekeeping (Library)
evidence: Research on “maternal gatekeeping” and household labor suggests criticism of how others do tasks can discourage their involvement. It applies to anyone who guards a job.
try: If it’s their job, let it be done their way, as long as it meets the agreed “done”.

When one person redoes or criticizes how someone else does a job, the other person often stops trying. Then the first person ends up doing it all, and resents it.

Agreeing on what “done” means, then letting the owner do it their way, breaks this cycle.

Program: The Library’s gatekeeping entry goes deeper. WP-03 owners own the how, within the agreed standard.

## Different standards of clean
aka: clean enough; tidy standards; mess tolerance
see: Lowering standards on purpose; Noticing thresholds
go: /library/fairness.html#anticipation-and-noticing | Noticing and anticipating (Library)
evidence: Research suggests people genuinely differ in how much mess they notice and tolerate. It’s not simply a matter of effort.
try: Agree three “baseline” rules for shared spaces: sink empty by bedtime, floor clear, bathroom wiped weekly.

People really do notice mess at different levels. The person who notices first often cleans first, and eventually cleans always, while the other person may truly not see the problem.

Agreeing a shared baseline for common spaces (and letting private spaces be private) usually works better than arguing about who is right.

Program: Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives: whoever notices first drifts into the job. WP-03 gives it a named owner.

## Noticing thresholds
aka: noticing threshold; who notices first; mess blindness
see: Different standards of clean; Defaults at home
go: /library/fairness.html#anticipation-and-noticing | Noticing and anticipating (Library)
evidence: Related research on attention and household labor suggests the person who notices tasks first often ends up doing them. The concept is well discussed, if not precisely measured.
try: Ask: “Which jobs do I do only because I notice them first?” Those are the ones to hand over as whole jobs.

A noticing threshold is how much needs doing before you notice it. If yours is lower, you’ll spot the full bin sooner, and do it sooner, again and again.

That’s not a personality flaw in either person. It’s a setup that quietly assigns jobs. Making the noticing part of someone else’s job is the fix.

Program: This is a textbook Pillar V pattern. WP-03’s Accountable name is the person who notices.

## Thanking for invisible work
aka: thank you for invisible work; appreciating mental load; noticing effort
see: Kinkeeping; Specific thanks
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: Research links appreciation with relationship satisfaction and with feeling that work is fair. Thanks for specific, unseen work may be especially meaningful.
try: “Thanks for remembering Mum’s birthday. I know that takes planning.”

Invisible work rarely gets thanked because nobody sees it. A specific thank-you for planning, remembering or smoothing things over shows you noticed, and noticing is half of what people want.

Thanks doesn’t replace sharing the load, but it keeps things steady while you sort out the split.

Program: Turning Toward’s daily idea is often a specific thank-you. The 90-second check-in includes one thing you appreciated.

## Outsourcing household jobs
aka: outsourcing chores; paying for help; delegating housework
see: Chore charts that work; Money and fairness splits
go: /lemonade-stand.html | The Lemonade Stand
evidence: Research (for example, Whillans and colleagues, 2017) found spending money to buy time was linked with greater life satisfaction. Whether it’s possible depends on budget, and the finding isn’t universal.
try: If budget allows, pick the most dreaded job and see if paying for it (or trading with a neighbor) frees up real time.

Some households pay for help with certain jobs (cleaning, laundry, meal kits, yard work) or trade with friends and neighbors. It can reduce friction when time is the scarcest resource.

It’s a budget decision and a values decision, and not everyone can or wants to. Even without money, a job can be simplified or dropped.

Program: The Lemonade Stand shows which jobs take the most time; that’s where outsourcing or simplifying would help most.

## Money and fairness splits
aka: splitting costs fairly; 50 50 or by income; proportional split
see: Yours, mine and ours accounts; Using fair standards
go: /lemonade-stand.html | The Lemonade Stand
evidence: Different couples and housemates use different fairness rules (equal, by income, by use). No one method is right; what matters most in research on fairness is that people agree the rule is fair.
try: Try both: calculate a 50/50 split and an income-based split, and talk about which feels fair to both.

Shared costs can be split equally, in proportion to income, or by use (for example, bigger room pays more). Each is fair by a different standard.

Agreeing on the rule before looking at the numbers avoids arguments that are really about which standard to use.

Program: The Lemonade Stand’s money side shows what each person actually paid. Chapter IV is about agreeing what “fair” means.
