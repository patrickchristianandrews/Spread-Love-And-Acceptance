# Conflict styles and de-escalation

These add practical detail to the Library’s conflict section.

## Conflict avoidance costs
aka: avoiding conflict; conflict avoidant; keeping the peace
see: Compromise and its limits; Choosing your battles
go: /library/conflict.html#thomas-kilmann | Conflict modes (Library)
evidence: Research on conflict styles suggests avoiding can protect relationships from small issues but, used for everything, can let resentment build.
try: “I tend to let things slide, and then they pile up. Can I raise one small thing now?”

Avoiding conflict can be sensible for small, passing issues. Used for everything, though, it lets problems and resentment build, until something small triggers a big reaction.

Raising things early, while they’re small, is often gentler than waiting.

## Compromise and its limits
aka: compromise; meeting halfway; when compromise fails
see: Splitting the difference; Trading across issues
go: /library/conflict.html#thomas-kilmann | Conflict modes (Library)
evidence: Conflict research treats compromise as useful but not always best; collaborating on underlying interests can produce better solutions for important issues.
try: For something that matters deeply to both of you, try asking “what do we each really need here?” before splitting the difference.

Compromise means each person gives up some of what they want. It’s quick and often fair, especially for small issues.

For important, recurring issues, compromise can leave both people partly unhappy. Exploring what each person really needs sometimes finds a solution that works better for both.

## Choosing your battles
aka: pick your battles; let it go; is it worth it
see: Conflict avoidance costs; One topic at a time
go: /workpapers/wp-02-how-much-are-you-carrying.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Common advice, and consistent with research suggesting relationships do better when small irritations are sometimes let go. It shouldn’t mean ignoring things that matter.
try: Ask: “Will this matter in a month? Is it a pattern or a one-off? Am I just tired?”

Not every irritation needs a conversation. Letting small things go, especially one-offs, can keep a relationship light.

The key questions: is it a pattern, does it really matter to me, and am I reacting to this or to my battery? Patterns and things that matter deserve a calm conversation.

Program: WP-02 helps with the last question: is this about the thing, or about what I was already carrying?

## Conflict escalation ladder
aka: escalation ladder; Glasl model; stages of conflict escalation
see: Fighting fair; De-escalation phrases
go: /library/conflict.html#escalation | Escalation (Library)
evidence: Friedrich Glasl described nine stages of conflict escalation (1980s). It’s widely used in mediation training as a descriptive model.
try: Notice early signs: “We’ve stopped talking about the bins and started talking about each other.”

Conflicts tend to escalate in steps: from a disagreement about a topic, to hardened positions, to talking about each other instead of the issue, to trying to win at any cost.

The earlier you notice the climb, the easier it is to step back down. A pause, a return to one topic, or a neutral helper can help.

## De-escalation phrases
aka: calming phrases; de-escalate; cool it down
see: Conflict escalation ladder; Humor that repairs
go: /carrier-wave-decoder.html | The Carrier Wave Decoder
evidence: Practical phrases from mediation and crisis-free conflict training. Tone and sincerity matter as much as the words.
try: “I think we’re both getting heated. I want to understand you. Can we slow down?”

A few calm phrases can slow a heated conversation: “I hear you.” “Let me make sure I understand.” “I don’t want to fight about this.” “Can we take a break and come back at 7?”

They work best said slowly, in a softer voice. Said sarcastically, they escalate things further.

## Agree to disagree
aka: agree to disagree; we see it differently; accepting differences
see: Compromise and its limits; Choosing your battles
go: /library/relationships.html#perpetual-problems | Perpetual problems (Library)
evidence: Relationship research (for example, John Gottman’s work on perpetual problems) suggests many disagreements in lasting relationships are never fully solved, and couples manage them rather than resolve them.
try: “We just see this differently, and that’s okay. How do we live with it kindly?”

Some differences (about politics, how clean is clean enough, how social to be) may never fully resolve. Agreeing to disagree, and finding a way to live alongside the difference, is often healthier than arguing endlessly.

It works best when both people still feel respected, and when practical decisions are agreed even if the underlying views differ.

Program: The Library’s perpetual problems entry explains why this is normal.

## Pursuing and distancing in everyday life
aka: pursue withdraw; chasing and retreating; the more I push the more they pull away
see: Conflict avoidance costs; Coming back after a break
go: /library/relationships.html#pursue-withdraw | Pursue and withdraw (Library)
evidence: The pursue-withdraw (or demand-withdraw) pattern is well documented in couples research. It’s a cycle, not one person’s fault.
try: If you pursue: say your need once, then give space with a time. If you withdraw: say when you’ll come back.

In a common cycle, one person pushes to talk and the other pulls back, which makes the first push harder, which makes the second pull back further. Both feel frustrated.

Naming the cycle together (“we’re doing our thing again”) and agreeing a pause-and-return plan often breaks it.

Program: The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) gives the withdrawing person a clear pause line, and the pursuing person a time.

## Conflict in front of others
aka: arguing in public; fighting in front of kids; conflict in front of friends
see: Talking kindly about the other parent; Choosing the channel
go: /check-ins.html#room | Check-ins: a good time and place
evidence: Research on children and parental conflict suggests calm, resolved disagreements are less harmful than hostile ones, and seeing repair can be reassuring.
try: “Let’s talk about this later, just us.”

Arguing in front of friends, colleagues or children adds pressure and embarrassment, and makes it harder to back down.

Mild disagreements handled calmly (and repaired) can actually teach kids healthy conflict. Hostile or unresolved fights are best taken somewhere private.

Program: Check-ins suggest a private place, phones down, at an agreed time.

## Cooling off before replying
aka: cool off; wait before responding; sleep on a reply
see: Don’t fight by text; De-escalation phrases
go: /workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands.html | WP-09: Say it so it lands
evidence: Research on emotion suggests intensity drops with time. Waiting before replying to a charged message is widely recommended.
try: Write the reply, don’t send it. Read it again in an hour.

When a message or comment stings, the urge to reply immediately is strong. Waiting even a little while lets the intensity drop and gives you a chance to reply to what was said, not to how it felt in the first moment.

Writing a draft and not sending it can release some of the pressure.

## Repair during a fight
aka: making up mid-argument; softening in a fight; calling a truce
see: Humor that repairs; De-escalation phrases
go: /library/relationships.html#repair-attempts | Repair attempts (Library)
evidence: John Gottman’s research highlighted “repair attempts” (small moves to de-escalate) as important in couples who handle conflict well. Whether a repair works depends on the relationship’s overall warmth.
try: “Wait, I’m on your side here. Let me try that again.”

A repair attempt is anything that slows down or softens a conflict mid-way: a joke, an apology, a touch, “I’m sorry, let me start over.” Noticing and accepting the other person’s repair attempts matters as much as making them.

Repairs land best early, before things get too heated, and when they’re sincere. If a repair is brushed off, it’s often a sign you both need a real break, then a calmer restart.
