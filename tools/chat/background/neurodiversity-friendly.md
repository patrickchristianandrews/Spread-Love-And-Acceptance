# Neurodiversity-friendly communication

Practical tips that help many people. Everyone is different: ask, don’t assume.

## Explicit requests
aka: be explicit; clear requests; say what you mean; direct asks
see: Clear yes or no questions; Curse of knowledge in instructions
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: Guidance from autistic self-advocates and communication research on the double empathy problem. Explicit requests help many people, not only neurodivergent people.
try: Not “It would be nice if the garage got cleaned sometime” but “Could you help me clean the garage on Saturday at 10?”

Hints, hopes and indirect suggestions (“the bins are getting full…”) are easily missed, especially by people who take words literally. An explicit request says who, what and when.

It isn’t rude to be clear. For many people, a clear ask feels respectful: it tells them exactly how to help.

Program: The Wiring Card lets people say “please ask me directly”. Wired Differently explains why hints can miss.

## Clear yes or no questions
aka: yes or no questions; closed questions for clarity; either or questions
see: Explicit requests; Open and closed questions
go: /wired-differently.html | Wired Differently
evidence: Practical guidance used in autism-friendly and accessible communication. It also helps anyone who is tired or overwhelmed.
try: Instead of “What do you want to do about dinner?” try “Pasta or stir-fry tonight?”

Open questions can be hard when someone is tired, overwhelmed or finds decisions draining. A clear either-or or yes-or-no question lowers the effort.

It isn’t about limiting choice. It’s about making the next step easy. You can always add “or something else?”.

Program: Wired Differently suggests asking about the body or actions (“more like tired, or more like annoyed?”) for people who find feelings hard to name.

## Processing time
aka: time to process; thinking time; delayed response; need time to think
see: Written follow-ups; Agendas in advance
go: /wiring-card.html | The Wiring Card
evidence: Many autistic people, people with ADHD and others describe needing extra time to process spoken information, especially under stress. Individual needs vary a lot.
try: “You don’t have to answer now. Can we come back to this after dinner?”

Some people need time to process before they can respond well, especially to emotional or complex topics. Pushing for an instant answer often gets a worse answer, or a shutdown.

Offering time (“think about it and let me know tomorrow”) is a small kindness that usually leads to a better conversation.

Program: The Wiring Card can say what silence means for you: for many, it means processing, not upset.

## Written follow-ups
aka: follow up in writing; put it in writing; send a text summary
see: Processing time; Written recaps after a talk
go: /workpapers/wp-03-raci-treaty.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: A common accommodation in workplaces and a frequent request from neurodivergent people. Written notes help memory for everyone.
try: After a talk: “Just to confirm: I’ll book the dentist, you’ll do Thursday pickup.”

Spoken information disappears quickly, and some people find it much harder to hold onto than written words. A short written follow-up gives everyone something to check later.

It also reduces arguments about what was said, which helps every kind of wiring.

## Agendas in advance
aka: agenda ahead of time; no surprises; heads up on topics
see: Processing time; A simple agenda for a hard talk
go: /check-ins.html | Check-ins
evidence: A widely recommended accommodation for autistic people and others who find surprises stressful. It generally helps meetings run better for everyone.
try: “Tomorrow at 7, can we talk about the holiday plan? Nothing bad, just want to decide dates.”

Knowing the topic ahead of time lets people prepare, which lowers anxiety and leads to better conversations. Surprise “we need to talk” moments can be especially hard for people who need to process.

A one-line heads-up is often enough.

Program: Check-ins are agreed talks at an agreed time, with one topic.

## Sensory-friendly spaces for talks
aka: quiet room; sensory friendly; low stimulation space
see: Noise, light and stress; Processing time
go: /library/wiring.html#sensory-profiles | Sensory profiles (Library)
evidence: People differ widely in sensory sensitivity. Reducing noise and visual clutter is a common accommodation and generally helps focus.
try: Turn off the TV, dim the lights, and sit somewhere without an echo before a hard talk.

For some people, noise, bright light, strong smells or a busy room use up a lot of energy, leaving less for the conversation. A quieter, calmer space can make a real difference.

Asking “where would you be most comfortable talking?” is simple and respectful.

Program: The Library’s sensory entries go deeper. Check-ins suggest choosing a good time and place.

## Info-dumping as connection
aka: info dumping; infodumping; sharing special interests
see: Special interests as connection; Parallel play
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: Described widely by autistic people as a way of sharing and bonding. Research on autistic social connection increasingly recognizes it as a genuine form of connection.
try: When a loved one excitedly explains their interest, try: “Tell me more. What’s the coolest part?”

Info-dumping means sharing lots of details about something you love. For many autistic people, it’s a way of showing care and inviting connection, like saying “this matters to me, and I want to share it with you”.

Receiving it warmly, even briefly, is a powerful form of turning toward. It’s okay to say kindly when you need a break.

Program: Turning Toward is about answering small reaches for connection, and this is one of them.

## Special interests as connection
aka: special interests; passions; deep interests
see: Info-dumping as connection; Parallel play
go: /library/wiring.html#monotropism | Monotropism (Library)
evidence: Autistic people often describe intense interests as a source of joy and regulation. Research on monotropism explores how attention can focus deeply.
try: Ask about their interest when they seem stressed. It can be a calming topic.

Deep, focused interests can be a source of joy, calm and identity. Showing interest in someone’s passion, even one you don’t share, tells them you value who they are.

Shared activities built around an interest can also be a low-pressure way to spend time together.

Program: The Library’s monotropism entry explains how some attention works in deep channels.

## Parallel play
aka: parallel play; being together separately; quiet company
see: Body doubling; Info-dumping as connection
go: /turning-toward.html | Turning Toward
evidence: The term comes from child development research; adults (especially many autistic people) use it for being together while doing separate activities. It’s a described preference rather than a studied intervention.
try: Sit together on the sofa, each reading or gaming, with no pressure to talk.

Parallel play means spending time in the same space while doing separate things. For many people it’s restful connection: company without the demands of conversation.

It can be a lovely way to be close after a draining day, and it counts as time together.

Program: Connection doesn’t have to be talk-heavy. Turning Toward includes many low-key ways to be close.

## Body doubling
aka: body doubling; body double; working alongside someone
see: Parallel play; The two-minute start
go: /library/wiring.html#adhd-basics | ADHD basics (Library)
evidence: Widely reported by people with ADHD as helpful for starting and staying on tasks. Formal research is limited.
try: “Could you sit with me while I do the paperwork? You don’t have to help.”

Body doubling means doing a task while someone else is present, either doing their own thing or just keeping you company. Many people with ADHD find it makes starting and sticking with tasks much easier.

It’s a simple, kind way to help someone who struggles to get going, without taking the task over.

Program: For situations like “I can’t get myself to start”, a body double can be a great first step.

## Time blindness
aka: time blindness; losing track of time; always late ADHD
see: Transition warnings; Planning, doing and following through
go: /library/wiring.html#adhd-basics | ADHD basics (Library)
evidence: A term widely used in the ADHD community for difficulty sensing time passing. Research on ADHD and time perception supports the general idea.
try: A visible timer, alarms for leaving (not just for arriving), and building in buffer time.

Some people, especially many with ADHD, find it hard to feel time passing. Ten minutes and forty minutes can feel the same until suddenly it’s late.

External supports (visible clocks, timers, alarms, calendars) help much more than trying harder. It’s a wiring difference, not a lack of caring.

Program: Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person: build timers and reminders into the routine. Wired Differently goes deeper.

## Transition warnings
aka: transition warning; heads up before switching; 10 minute warning
see: Time blindness; Agendas in advance
go: /wired-differently.html | Wired Differently
evidence: A common strategy for autistic children and adults, and for people with ADHD. It helps many people switch tasks more smoothly.
try: “We’re leaving in ten minutes. Five-minute warning coming.”

Switching from one activity to another can be hard, especially when deeply focused. A warning before a transition (“ten minutes until dinner”) gives time to wrap up.

It works for kids and adults alike, and helps avoid the frustration of being pulled away suddenly.

Program: The Library’s autistic inertia entry explains why starting and stopping can both be hard.

## Tone indicators
aka: tone indicators; tone tags; slash j; slash s
see: Texting norms that prevent fights; Explicit requests
go: /conversation-reader.html | The Conversation Reader
evidence: Tone indicators (like /j for joking or /srs for serious) became popular online, especially among neurodivergent people. They’re a social convention, not a researched tool.
try: “You’re such a menace /j” makes clear it’s a joke.

In text, tone is easy to misread. Some people add short tags to show their intent: /j (joking), /s (sarcastic), /srs (serious), /gen (genuine question).

They’re not for everyone, but agreeing on a few within a friend group or household can prevent a lot of misunderstandings.

Program: The Conversation Reader looks for moments where a message may have been heard differently than meant.

## Recovering after socializing
aka: social hangover; social recovery; after a party
see: Recovery after a hard day; Energy audit
go: /library/wiring.html#introversion | Introversion (Library)
evidence: Many people (introverted, autistic or not) describe needing recovery time after social events. Needs vary a lot between people.
try: “I had a great time, and I need a quiet evening tomorrow to recharge.”

For many people, socializing uses energy even when it’s enjoyable. Afterward they need time alone or quiet to recharge.

Saying this openly (“it was lovely, and now I need a quiet day”) helps others not read it as rejection.

Program: Frequency Calibration includes rest and recovery rhythms. The Wiring Card can note what you need after busy days.

## Direct is not rude
aka: directness; blunt communication; autistic directness
see: Explicit requests; High-context and low-context communication
go: /library/communication.html#directness | Directness (Library)
evidence: The double empathy research and cross-cultural studies both suggest directness is often misread as rudeness by people who expect indirect communication.
try: “I say things plainly. If it ever sounds harsh, tell me, and I’ll soften it.”

Some people communicate very directly: they say exactly what they mean, without softening. Others hear directness as cold or rude. Neither style is wrong. They’re different ways of sending the same care.

Talking about it openly (“I’m direct; it’s never personal”) helps both people tune in.

Program: Pillar IV: a mismatch is tuning, not a moral failing. The Wiring Card is made for this.

## Demand sensitivity
aka: demand avoidance; pathological demand avoidance; PDA; pushback against requests
see: Explicit requests; Asking permission before giving advice
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: Pathological (or persistent) demand avoidance is a proposed profile discussed mainly in the UK. It is not a formal diagnosis in major diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it. General sensitivity to demands is more widely recognized.
try: Instead of “You need to do the dishes,” try “The dishes need doing tonight. When works for you?”

Some people feel intense resistance to direct demands, even for things they want to do. It can come from a strong need for autonomy or from anxiety.

Offering choices, explaining reasons and framing requests as shared problems often helps. This is general information, not a diagnosis.

Program: Know your own wiring helps you notice patterns like this without judgment. The program’s approach is to fix the setup, not the person.
