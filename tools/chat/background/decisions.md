# Making decisions

These add to the Library’s entries on joint decisions and decision fatigue.

## Reversible and irreversible decisions
aka: two way doors; one way doors; reversible decisions
see: Satisficing; Sleep on it
go: /library/life.html#joint-decisions | Joint decisions (Library)
evidence: A decision heuristic popularized by Amazon’s Jeff Bezos (“one-way and two-way doors”). It’s a practical rule of thumb rather than a research finding.
try: “This is a two-way door: let’s try the new chore split for a month and switch back if it’s not working.”

Some decisions are easy to undo (a new routine, a restaurant choice); others aren’t (moving house, a big purchase). Reversible decisions can be made quickly and tested. Irreversible ones deserve more time and care.

Treating reversible decisions as experiments takes pressure off both people.

Program: The monthly look-back (WP-04) treats household changes as experiments to check, not permanent verdicts.

## Satisficing
aka: good enough decision; satisficer; maximizer
see: Reversible and irreversible decisions; Analysis paralysis
go: /library/motivation.html#decision-fatigue | Decision fatigue (Library)
evidence: Herbert Simon introduced “satisficing” in the 1950s. Later research (for example, Barry Schwartz and colleagues) suggested “maximizers” who seek the best option are often less satisfied than “satisficers”.
try: “Any restaurant that’s nearby and has a veggie option is fine tonight.”

Satisficing means choosing the first option that’s good enough, rather than searching for the best one. For many everyday decisions, it saves time and stress, and people are often just as happy with the result.

Save the careful searching for the decisions that really matter.

Program: The Library’s decision fatigue entry explains why many small decisions wear people out.

## The 10-10-10 question
aka: 10 10 10; ten minutes ten months ten years; 10/10/10
see: Reversible and irreversible decisions; Sleep on it
go: /library/life.html#joint-decisions | Joint decisions (Library)
evidence: A decision tool popularized by writer Suzy Welch (10-10-10, 2009). It’s practical advice, not a research-tested method.
try: “How will I feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years?”

Asking how you’ll feel about a choice in ten minutes, ten months and ten years helps separate short-term feelings from long-term priorities.

It’s especially useful for decisions clouded by strong feelings in the moment.

## Pre-mortem
aka: premortem; imagine it failed; what could go wrong
see: Reversible and irreversible decisions; Retrospective for households
go: /workpapers/wp-04-deficit-audit.html | WP-04: What keeps coming back?
evidence: Gary Klein described the pre-mortem (Harvard Business Review, 2007). It’s widely used in project planning; evidence is mostly practical.
try: “Imagine it’s a month from now and the new chore plan has failed. What went wrong?”

A pre-mortem imagines a plan has already failed and asks why. It surfaces likely problems before they happen, when they’re easy to plan around.

It works well for new household routines, trips and projects.

Program: The monthly look-back (WP-04) is the after version; a pre-mortem is the before version.

## Sleep on it
aka: sleep on it; wait before deciding; decide tomorrow
see: The 10-10-10 question; Negotiating with deadlines
go: /workpapers/wp-02-battery-stress-meter.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Common advice. Research on emotion and decision-making suggests strong emotions and tiredness can affect choices; claims about “unconscious thought” helping decisions are debated.
try: “This is a big one. Can we both sleep on it and decide tomorrow evening?”

For big or emotional decisions, waiting a day often leads to a calmer choice. Strong feelings fade, and new considerations surface.

It doesn’t work for every decision, and endless postponing becomes avoidance. Set a time to decide.

Program: WP-02 suggests putting off anything that doesn’t need deciding in the next hour when your battery is high.

## Consensus and consent
aka: consensus; consent decision making; no objections
see: Decision rights at work; Deciding by domain at home
go: /library/life.html#joint-decisions | Joint decisions (Library)
evidence: Consent-based decision-making comes from sociocracy and similar approaches. It’s a practical method; research is limited.
try: Instead of “does everyone love this?”, ask “can everyone live with this for a month?”

Consensus means everyone agrees. Consent means nobody has a strong objection. Consent is often faster, especially in groups, because it asks “is this good enough to try?” rather than “is this everyone’s favorite?”.

It works well for households and teams trying something new.

Program: Treating changes as a trial for a month, then reviewing with WP-04, pairs well with a consent approach.

## Deciding by domain at home
aka: who decides what; decision domains; dividing decisions
see: Consensus and consent; Planning, doing and following through
go: /tools/frequency-calibration.html | Frequency Calibration
evidence: A practical approach. Research on couple decision-making suggests shared decision-making is linked with satisfaction, and dividing some areas can reduce friction.
try: “You decide weekday dinners, I decide the kids’ activities, and we decide money over $200 together.”

Not every decision needs two people. Some couples and households divide decisions by area: one person decides dinners, another decides the weekend schedule. Bigger decisions are made together.

It saves time and reduces friction, as long as both people agree the split is fair.

Program: Frequency Calibration includes decision rhythms, and WP-03 can list decision owners too.

## Analysis paralysis
aka: overthinking decisions; can’t decide; decision paralysis
see: Satisficing; Decision journals
go: /library/life.html#joint-decisions | Joint decisions (Library)
evidence: Research on choice overload (for example, Iyengar and Lepper, 2000) suggests too many options can make choosing harder, though the effect varies across studies.
try: Narrow it to three options, set a deadline, and pick.

Too many options or too much information can make it hard to decide at all. The search feels productive, but nothing moves.

Limiting options, setting a deadline and accepting “good enough” all help.

Program: The Library covers choice overload and decision fatigue.

## Decision journals
aka: decision journal; recording decisions; why we decided
see: Analysis paralysis; Outcome bias
go: /workpapers/report-01.html | REPORT-01: Your progress, week by week
evidence: Recommended by some decision researchers and investors to reduce hindsight bias. Evidence is mostly practical.
try: Write down: the decision, the options, why you chose, and what you expect to happen.

A decision journal records what you decided, why, and what you expected. Later, you can look back fairly, without hindsight rewriting the reasons.

It’s useful for big household decisions and for teams.

Program: REPORT-01 keeps a week-by-week record of your household numbers, which serves a similar purpose.

## Values-based decisions
aka: deciding by values; what matters most; values in decisions
see: Values clarification; The 10-10-10 question
go: /library/motivation.html#values | Values (Library)
evidence: Values-based approaches (for example, in acceptance and commitment therapy) suggest aligning choices with values supports wellbeing. This is general information.
try: “We both value time together more than money right now. So the cheaper holiday, with more days, makes sense.”

When options seem equal, asking “which fits what we care about most?” can break the tie. Naming shared values also makes it easier to accept a decision you didn’t fully love.

It helps to name your values before the options are on the table, so you’re not tempted to pick values that justify the choice you already wanted.

Program: The Library’s values entry goes deeper.

## Pros and cons that actually help
aka: pros and cons list; weighing options; decision matrix
see: Values-based decisions; Analysis paralysis
go: /library/life.html#joint-decisions | Joint decisions (Library)
evidence: A simple, long-standing decision tool. Weighting items by importance generally makes it more useful than simply counting.
try: List pros and cons, then give each a weight from 1 to 3 for how much it matters.

A plain list of pros and cons can mislead if every item counts equally. Weighting each item by importance, and noticing gut reactions to the result, makes the list more useful.

Doing it together also shows what matters to each person.

Program: Useful for joint decisions; the Library’s joint decisions entry covers deciding together.

## Default decisions
aka: default decisions; decide once; standing decisions
see: Defaults at home; Deciding by domain at home
go: /five-pillars.html#quiet-incentives | Pillar V: Notice the quiet incentives
evidence: Research on decision fatigue and defaults suggests reducing repeated choices can ease daily stress, though the size of decision-fatigue effects is debated.
try: “Taco Tuesday, pasta Thursday. Two fewer dinner decisions every week.”

Making a decision once and letting it stand (a weekly meal plan, a set chore day, a regular date night) removes repeated negotiation.

Defaults can be changed, but they don’t need to be decided every time.

Program: Pillar V is about the power of defaults. WP-03 is a set of standing decisions about who owns what.
