# Caregiving logistics

Practical, non-medical notes. For health questions, talk with a qualified professional.

## Care calendar
aka: care calendar; caregiving schedule; shared care schedule
see: Care notebook; Asking for specific help
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Shared care calendars are widely recommended by caregiver organizations. Evidence is practical.
try: One shared calendar with appointments, visits, meals and who is covering each one.

A care calendar shows who is doing what and when: appointments, visits, meals, errands, overnight stays. It helps share the load and makes gaps visible.

It also makes it easier for others to offer help, because they can see exactly where it’s needed.

Program: WP-03 turns the care tasks into named owners. The caregiver road starts with your own battery.

## Care notebook
aka: care notebook; care binder; caregiving information
see: Care calendar; Care handoff notes
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Caregiver organizations commonly recommend keeping key information in one place. It’s a practical tool.
try: One notebook or shared document: contacts, appointments, questions for next time, and who’s helping with what.

A care notebook keeps important information in one place: contacts, upcoming appointments, questions to ask professionals, and notes from each visit. It saves repeated searching and helps anyone who steps in.

Keep it where others helping can find it, and respect the privacy of the person you care for.

Program: This is invisible work made visible (Pillar I). A care notebook helps others take over tasks.

## Asking for specific help
aka: asking for help; how to ask for help; specific requests for help
see: Care calendar; Explicit requests
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Caregiver guidance often notes that specific requests are more likely to get a yes than general ones. It matches research on helping behavior.
try: “Could you take Mum to her Thursday appointment and stay for lunch?”

“Let me know if you need anything” is kind, but hard to act on. Specific requests (“could you bring dinner on Tuesday?”) are easier to say yes to, and easier to follow through.

Keeping a list of small, concrete tasks ready makes it easy to answer when someone offers.

Program: The caregiver road suggests asking for one specific kind of help, with a time.

## Sibling care meetings
aka: siblings caring for parents; family care meeting; sharing care between siblings
see: Sibling rivalry in adulthood; Care calendar
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Research on family caregiving finds care often falls unevenly among siblings, frequently on one person. Regular family meetings are commonly recommended.
try: A monthly call: what’s changed, what’s needed, who owns what next month.

When siblings share care for a parent, it often falls mostly on one person, usually whoever lives closest or started first. A regular meeting to divide jobs helps.

Long-distance siblings can own tasks that work remotely: bills, calls, paperwork, research.

Program: WP-03 gives every care job one owner; the Lemonade Stand can show how the time splits.

## Respite and breaks for caregivers
aka: respite; caregiver break; time off from caring
see: Asking for specific help; Recovery after a hard day
go: /workpapers/wp-02-how-much-are-you-carrying.html | WP-02: How full is your battery?
evidence: Caregiver research links regular breaks with lower strain. Availability of formal respite varies by place.
try: Book one regular break each week, even a few hours, with a named person covering.

Caregivers often feel guilty taking time off, but regular breaks help you keep caring over the long run. Breaks can be short and simple: an afternoon out, a walk, time with friends.

Friends, family, community services or paid help may be able to cover. Local caregiver organizations can often point to options.

Program: WP-02 helps you notice when your battery is running low, before you’re running on empty.

## Long-distance caregiving
aka: caring from far away; long distance caregiver; remote caregiving
see: Sibling care meetings; Care notebook
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Long-distance caregiving is common. Guidance focuses on coordination, communication and supporting local caregivers.
try: Own the remote jobs: bills, paperwork, calls to services, and a weekly call with the local caregiver.

Caring from a distance brings its own stresses: worry, guilt and feeling out of the loop. Taking on tasks that can be done remotely helps both the person cared for and the local caregivers.

Regular check-ins with whoever is nearby help you stay informed and share the load.

Program: WP-03 works across distance: owners don’t have to be in the same house.

## Dignity and preferences
aka: respecting preferences; autonomy in care; asking what they want
see: Care notebook; Care roles reversing
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Person-centered care is a central principle in caregiving guidance. Involving people in decisions about their own lives supports dignity.
try: “Would you prefer I come in the mornings or afternoons? And what would you like to keep doing yourself?”

The person being cared for still has preferences, routines and pride. Asking what they want, and letting them do what they can, helps them keep their dignity and sense of self.

It can be slower, but it usually makes care feel less like a takeover.

Program: Pillar IV: tune how you send and receive. Care goes better when it’s shaped around the person, not just the tasks.

## Care roles reversing
aka: caring for a parent; role reversal; parent child role reversal
see: Dignity and preferences; Caregiver identity
go: /library/life.html#ageing-parents | Ageing parents (Library)
evidence: Research on adult children caring for parents describes complex feelings, including grief and role confusion. These feelings are common.
try: “It’s strange for both of us that I’m helping you now. Can we figure out together what works?”

When adult children care for parents, roles can feel turned upside down. Both people may feel awkward, sad or resistant, even when the care is loving.

Naming the change gently, and keeping as much of the old relationship as possible (a shared hobby, a joke, a routine), helps.

Program: The Library’s ageing parents entry goes deeper. Ambiguous loss, also in the Library, covers the grief of change.

## Caring for a partner
aka: partner caregiving; caring for my husband; caring for my wife; spouse caregiver
see: Care roles reversing; Respite and breaks for caregivers
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Research on spousal caregiving describes shifts in roles and intimacy. Keeping some of the couple relationship alive is commonly recommended.
try: Protect one small ritual that’s just about being a couple, not about care.

When one partner becomes the other’s caregiver, the relationship changes: more tasks, different roles, and sometimes less time just being a couple. Both people may miss how things were.

Sharing care tasks with others where you can, and protecting small moments of partnership, helps.

Program: The caregiver road starts with your battery (WP-02) and one owner per job (WP-03), so you’re not doing all of it alone.

## Caregiver identity
aka: identity as a caregiver; losing myself in caregiving; who am I besides a caregiver
see: Respite and breaks for caregivers; Values clarification
go: /know-yourself.html | Know your own wiring
evidence: Caregiver research describes how caring can take over a person’s identity. Keeping other roles and interests is linked with wellbeing.
try: Name one thing that’s just yours this week, and protect time for it.

Caregiving can gradually take over your identity until you’re “the caregiver” and little else. Keeping some parts of your life that aren’t about caring (friends, a hobby, work you enjoy) helps you stay yourself.

It isn’t selfish. It’s part of being able to keep caring.

Program: Know your own wiring and the self road in the Workpaper Suite are about understanding and caring for yourself.

## Care handoff notes
aka: care handover; notes for the next helper; caregiver handover
see: Care notebook; Handoff notes at work
go: /workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job.html | WP-03: One owner per job
evidence: Structured handoffs are used widely in professional care to reduce errors. The same idea helps family caregivers.
try: “Today: ate well, walked to the garden. Tomorrow: appointment at 2. Watch out for: tired after lunch.”

When care passes from one person to another, a short note helps the next person know what’s happened and what’s coming. It avoids repeated questions and missed tasks.

Keep it simple, factual and in a shared place.

Program: Clear handoffs are part of Pillar II: fix the setup, not the person.

## Coordinating visitors
aka: too many visitors; visitor schedule; managing visits
see: Care calendar; Kinds of boundaries
go: /relationships-in-depth.html#caregivers | Where caregivers start
evidence: Practical guidance. Visits can lift spirits but also tire people who are unwell or recovering.
try: A simple visit schedule in the care calendar, with suggested lengths and a named contact person.

Visitors can be wonderful, but too many at once can be tiring for the person cared for and for the caregiver who hosts. A simple schedule spreads visits out.

A named contact person can handle requests so the main caregiver doesn’t have to.

Program: This is a boundary about time, and a job worth giving an owner in WP-03.
