# Habits and behavior change

## Habit stacking
aka: habit stacking; stacking habits; anchor habits; after I do X
see: Tiny habits; Environment design at home
go: /library/motivation.html#habits-and-cues | Habits and cues (Library)
evidence: The idea builds on research on implementation intentions and cues. The phrase “habit stacking” was popularized by writers including James Clear (Atomic Habits, 2018). Evidence for the specific technique is mostly indirect.
try: “After I pour my morning coffee, I’ll check the family calendar for today.”

Habit stacking means attaching a new habit to one you already do every day. The existing habit becomes the cue: “After I brush my teeth, I’ll…”, “When I sit down for dinner, we’ll…”.

It works because it removes the need to remember. The old habit reminds you. Choose an anchor that happens reliably at the right time and place.

## Tiny habits
aka: tiny habits; start small; make it tiny; small habits
see: Habit stacking; The two-minute start
go: /library/motivation.html#habit-formation | Habit formation (Library)
evidence: BJ Fogg describes the approach in Tiny Habits (2019): start with a very small behavior, anchor it, and celebrate. Research generally supports starting small, though Fogg’s specific method has limited formal testing.
try: Instead of “clean the kitchen every night”, start with “wipe one counter after dinner”.

A tiny habit is a version of a behavior so small it feels almost silly: one push-up, one sentence in a journal, one dish. Tiny habits are easy to start on low-energy days, and they can grow naturally.

The point isn’t the tiny action itself. It’s building the routine. Once it’s automatic, making it bigger is much easier.

## The two-minute start
aka: two minute rule; just two minutes; start for two minutes
see: Tiny habits; Procrastination and the first step
go: /library/motivation.html#procrastination | Procrastination (Library)
evidence: A popular productivity guideline. David Allen’s “two-minute rule” (Getting Things Done, 2001) says to do tasks taking under two minutes right away; James Clear suggests scaling a habit down to two minutes. Both are practical tips rather than research findings.
try: “I’ll just do two minutes of the laundry pile. I can stop after that.”

Starting is often the hardest part. Committing to just two minutes lowers the barrier, and once you’ve started, continuing is often easier than you expected.

Separately, a common rule says that if a task takes less than two minutes, doing it now saves the effort of remembering it later.

Program: Situations like “I can’t get myself to start the dishes” often ease with a tiny, time-boxed start.

## Procrastination and the first step
aka: making the first step obvious; first step; next action
see: The two-minute start; Tiny habits
go: /library/motivation.html#procrastination | Procrastination (Library)
evidence: Research on procrastination links it to how unpleasant or unclear a task feels. Breaking tasks into concrete next steps is a widely used strategy.
try: Not “sort the taxes” but “find last year’s folder and put it on the table”.

Big, vague tasks invite avoidance. A clear, concrete first step (“open the email”, “put the gloves by the sink”) is much easier to start.

Writing the very next physical action often unsticks tasks that have been sitting for weeks.

## Environment design at home
aka: environment design; make it easy; friction; choice architecture at home
see: Habit stacking; Defaults at home
go: /library/motivation.html#habits-and-cues | Habits and cues (Library)
evidence: Research on habits and on choice architecture suggests small changes in the environment (what’s easy, visible or in the way) can shift behavior more reliably than intentions alone.
try: Put a laundry basket where clothes actually land, and a hook where keys actually get dropped.

Our surroundings nudge us all day. If the laundry basket is in another room, clothes pile on the chair. If the bin is full and awkward, the rubbish waits.

Making the helpful thing easy (and the unhelpful thing a little harder) often works better than reminders. It also takes blame out of it: the setup is doing the work.

## Defaults at home
aka: defaults; default choices; who does it by default
see: Environment design at home; Kinkeeping
go: /five-pillars.html#quiet-incentives | Pillar V: Notice the quiet incentives
evidence: The default effect (people tend to stick with whatever happens if they do nothing) is well documented in behavioral economics, for example in organ donation and retirement savings research.
try: Ask: “What happens if nobody decides?” That’s your default, and it’s often one person doing it.

A default is whatever happens when nobody makes an active choice. At home, defaults are powerful: the person who noticed first last time often becomes the person who does it forever.

Changing a default on purpose (“bins are Jordan’s now, noticing included”) is often more effective than asking for help each time.

Program: Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, is all about this. WP-03 turns defaults into named owners.

## Streaks and tracking
aka: habit tracker; streaks; tracking habits; don’t break the chain
see: Never miss twice; Tiny habits
go: /workpapers/report-01.html | REPORT-01: Your progress, week by week
evidence: Self-monitoring is linked with behavior change in many studies. Streaks can motivate, but some research suggests breaking a streak can be discouraging.
try: A simple calendar on the fridge with a tick for each day you did the daily check-in.

Tracking a habit (a tick on a calendar, a note in your phone) makes progress visible, which can be motivating. Many people like seeing a streak grow.

The danger is all-or-nothing thinking: one missed day and the whole thing feels ruined. Treat the tracker as information, not a scorecard.

## Never miss twice
aka: never miss twice; getting back on track; missed a day
see: Streaks and tracking; Restarting after a break
go: /workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: A popular guideline from habit writers. Research on habit formation (for example, Lally and colleagues, 2010) found that missing a single day didn’t significantly harm habit building.
try: Missed the check-in yesterday? No problem. Just do it tonight.

Missing one day is normal and doesn’t undo a habit. What matters is getting back to it quickly, ideally the next day, so the gap doesn’t become the new routine.

This takes the pressure off being perfect, which helps habits last.

Program: The daily check-in is designed to be small enough to pick straight back up.

## Restarting after a break
aka: starting again; fell off the wagon; relapse is normal
see: Never miss twice; Fresh starts
go: /prog-01.html | PROG-01: Six gentle weeks
evidence: Research on behavior change (for example, the stages-of-change model) treats lapses as a normal part of the process rather than failure.
try: “We stopped the daily check-ins over the holidays. Let’s just start again tonight.”

Most people who build a habit fall off at some point: holidays, illness, a busy season. Restarting is part of the process, not a sign it didn’t work.

When you restart, start smaller than before, and don’t try to make up for lost time.

## Fresh starts
aka: fresh start; new beginnings; start of the month
see: Restarting after a break; Habit stacking
go: /library/motivation.html#fresh-start-effect | The fresh start effect (Library)
evidence: Research by Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis (2014) found people are more likely to start goals after temporal landmarks like new weeks, months or birthdays.
try: “The first of the month is Sunday. Want to start the monthly look-back then?”

Landmarks like a Monday, the first of the month or a birthday can make it feel easier to begin something new, as if you’re turning a page.

You can use this on purpose: pick a meaningful start date for a new household routine, and mark it.

## Keystone habits
aka: keystone habit; one habit that changes others
see: Habit stacking; Tiny habits
go: /workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: Popularized by Charles Duhigg in The Power of Habit (2012). The idea is intuitive, but evidence that specific habits reliably trigger wider change is limited.
try: For some couples, a daily check-in becomes a keystone: it catches small problems early and makes other routines easier.

A keystone habit is one that seems to make other good changes easier. Regular family dinners, a morning planning moment or a daily check-in can play this role.

It isn’t guaranteed. It’s a useful question, though: which one small routine would make the biggest difference for us?

Program: The 90-second daily check-in often acts as a keystone for households using the program.

## Celebrating small steps
aka: celebrate; celebrating progress; reward yourself
see: Tiny habits; Small wins at home
go: /library/motivation.html#small-wins | Small wins (Library)
evidence: BJ Fogg recommends an immediate small celebration to help habits stick. Research on small wins (Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer) links noticing progress with motivation.
try: After finishing the laundry: a fist pump, a “nice!”, or a cup of tea.

A small moment of celebration right after doing a new habit (a smile, a “yes!”, a tick on the chart) helps connect the action with a good feeling.

For households and teams, celebrating together (“we did the check-in every day this week!”) builds shared pride.

Program: The Library’s small wins entry explains why progress you notice keeps you going.

## Small wins at home
aka: small wins; quick wins; tiny progress
see: Celebrating small steps; Helplessness
go: /library/motivation.html#small-wins | Small wins (Library)
evidence: Research on small wins suggests visible progress, even minor, supports motivation and mood at work. Applying it at home is a reasonable extension.
try: Pick the easiest fix from your monthly look-back first, not the biggest.

When a household is stuck on big problems, starting with a small, fixable one builds momentum and trust. Each small win shows that change is possible.

It also makes the harder conversations easier, because you’ve proved to each other you can agree.

## Routines versus habits
aka: routines; habits and routines; daily routines
see: Habit stacking; Environment design at home
go: /library/motivation.html#habit-formation | Habit formation (Library)
evidence: Researchers generally define habits as automatic responses to cues, while routines are sequences we still choose. The two overlap in everyday life.
try: A morning routine (make bed, breakfast, check calendar) can slowly turn into a habit that runs on autopilot.

A routine is a regular sequence you choose to follow; a habit is something that has become automatic. Routines are often how habits start.

For people who find switching tasks hard, predictable routines can reduce the mental effort of each day. For people who love variety, a light routine with room for change often works better.

## Shared habits for households
aka: household habits; family routines; couple habits
see: Habit stacking; Weekly planning together
go: /workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in.html | WP-13: The 90-second daily check-in
evidence: Research on family routines links regular shared routines with family wellbeing. Specific routines are a matter of fit.
try: One shared habit to start: a five-minute tidy together before bed, with music.

Some habits work better done together: a quick tidy after dinner, a weekly plan, a daily check-in. Doing them together makes them lighter and turns chores into connection.

Start with one, keep it short, and attach it to something that already happens.
