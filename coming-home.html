Coming home after time apart

One of you has been away for months: a deployment, a long contract far from home, or a long stay in hospital. Now you are under one roof again. That is good news, and it can still be bumpy. The one who stayed built a way of running the house. The one who was away comes back to a home that changed. Nobody is doing it wrong. This page is for both of you.

In short

  • The first weeks back are a handover, not a return to how things were.
  • The one who held the fort picks 2 or 3 whole jobs to hand back first.
  • The one coming home takes whole jobs, and leaves the other systems alone for now.
  • Feeling resentful, or like a stranger in your own house, is common. Say it as a need, not a score.
  • Bad sleep, nightmares or feeling on edge after a deployment are worth raising with a doctor. Where to get support.

Handover week: a plan for both of you

Most homecoming friction is not about love. It is about jobs: who does bedtime now, whose way the dishwasher gets loaded, who answers the school. A short, planned handover saves weeks of small fights.

  1. Sit down once in the first week. Twenty minutes, at a quiet time. Not the first night home.
  2. The one who held the fort picks 2 or 3 whole jobs to hand back. Whole jobs, like bedtime, the bins, the school run or the food shop. Not “helping with” them.
  3. Agree what “done” means for each one. For example, bedtime done means teeth, one story, lights out by eight. Say it once, then let it go.
  4. No quiet redoing. If a job is done differently but done, leave it. If something really matters, raise it at your next check-in, not in the moment.
  5. Add more after a week or two. Two jobs that go well beat five that turn into a fight.

If you held the fort

You carried everything for months. You made the calls alone, and you got good at it. It is normal to feel proud, tired and a little resentful all at once, and to find it hard to let go of jobs you know how to do.

  • Resentment shows how much you carried. It is not a flaw. Say it as a need, not a score.
  • Hand back the jobs you mind least first, not the ones you care about most.
  • Let it be done differently. Their way of packing a lunch is not wrong, just theirs.
  • Ask to be thanked if you need it. That is a fair thing to want.

Words you could use

“I got used to doing it my way while you were gone. Can we pick which jobs you take back?”

“I’m so glad you’re home. I’m also tired, and some days I’m cross about how much I carried. It’s not about you. Can we talk about it on Sunday?”

Instead of “I managed fine without you,” which lands as a door closing, try: “I learned to do it all, and I don’t want to keep doing it all.”

If you’re the one coming home

You may come back to routines you don’t know, kids who have grown, and a partner who runs things smoothly without you. Feeling like a stranger, or a guest, in your own house is very common in the first weeks. It usually eases as you find your jobs again.

  • Ask, don’t fix. Ask which jobs your partner would like handed back, rather than changing how things are done.
  • Take whole jobs, start to finish, so you are not “helping” on someone else’s job.
  • Leave their systems alone in week one. Rearranging the kitchen or the bedtime routine can land as “you did it wrong.”
  • Say thank you, specifically. Name what they carried.
  • Small moments count. A cup of tea, noticing a sigh, a hand on the shoulder. Turning toward has more.

Words you could use

“What would you like me to take back first? I’ll do it start to finish, your way for now.”

“Thank you for keeping the kids’ routines going all those months. I can see how much work that was.”

“I feel a bit like a guest here at the moment. I’m not blaming anyone. I just want to find my place again.”

Talking about it: two different stories

You each lived months the other didn’t see. One of you held everything together. The other was away, working, in hospital or in danger. Both of you may feel the other doesn’t understand, and both are right. A talk about jobs can quickly turn into “You have no idea what it was like.”

  • Hear their story before you tell yours. Ask one question, and listen to the whole answer.
  • Not on day one. Keep the first few days light. Bigger talks go to a set time, once you’ve both slept.
  • Don’t compare hard. “It was hard for me too” lands better than “At least you…”
  • Ask for what you need in one sentence. Hints are easy to miss when you’re both adjusting.
  • Agree a pause word. If either of you is on edge, stopping early is a kindness, not running away.

Words you could use

“I want to hear what it was like for you. Tell me as much as you want, and I’ll just listen.”

“I’m getting wound up. I need twenty minutes. Then I’ll come back and we’ll finish this.”

The kids and coming home

  • Kids may be shy, clingy or cross at first, even when they are thrilled. Little ones may not remember the routine with you.
  • Join in before you take over. Let the parent who stayed keep running the day for a while.
  • Agree the rules in private first, so the kids hear one plan.
  • Find a small thing that is just yours with each child: a bedtime story, a Saturday breakfast, a walk to school.

“I missed you so much. It might feel a bit strange having me back. That’s okay. We’ll get used to it together.”

Bonding tips for the first weeks back

After months apart, each of you changed in ways the other didn’t see. Feeling close again usually comes back through small shared moments, not one big reunion talk.

  • Ask for the stories, a little at a time. “What was a good day like while I was away?” works both ways. Each person shares as much as they want, and no more.
  • Do one ordinary thing together each day: the school run, walking the dog, cooking dinner. Ordinary time is what you both missed.
  • Go back to one habit from before, like your Friday takeaway or a favourite walk. Then try one new thing that belongs to now.
  • Go gently with touch and time alone. One of you may want closeness straight away, the other may need a few days. Ask, and let the slower pace set the speed.
  • Notice how the other person has grown. “You’ve got so good at handling the school” tells them you see who they are now.

Words you could use

“I don’t need to hear it all at once. Tell me one good thing from while I was away, and I’ll tell you one of mine.”

Closeness is offered, never owed. Nobody has to make up for lost time on a schedule.

Sleep, nightmares and feeling on edge

After a deployment, or any time that was frightening or very hard, many people sleep badly for a while. Some have nightmares, jump at sudden noises, feel on edge, or get angry faster than before. A long hospital stay can do this too.

This site can’t tell you what it is, and nobody can diagnose it from a list. What we can say: it is common, it is not a weakness, and it is worth raising with a doctor. Help tends to work better when it starts early.

  • Make a pause plan together while things are calm: a word that means “I need a break,” and a time you will come back. The Calm-Down Kit walks you through it.
  • For the partner: you can’t fix it alone, and you don’t have to. Look after your own sleep and your own support too.

“You’ve been sleeping badly since you got back. Would you be willing to talk to a doctor about it? I’ll come with you if you want.”

Where to get support

  • Your doctor or GP is a good first step for sleep, nightmares or feeling on edge.
  • US military families: Military OneSource, 800-342-9647. Free and confidential, any time.
  • US veterans and service members in crisis, and their families: call 988, then press 1 (Veterans Crisis Line).
  • Anyone in the US in crisis: call or text 988.
  • UK veterans: Combat Stress helpline, 0800 138 1619.
  • Somewhere else: findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country.

If you are ever afraid of your partner, or of what they might do, see Not safe at home? In danger now, call your local emergency number.

A small daily habit for the first month

Pick one moment each day, like after the kids are in bed. Each of you says one thing that went well and one thing that felt heavy. That is all. The 90-second daily check-in shows how. Bigger topics wait for a calm, agreed check-in.

Questions people often ask

Is it normal to feel resentful when my partner comes home?

Yes. You carried everything for a long time, and now things are changing again. Resentment usually shows how much you held. Say it as a need, such as “Can we pick which jobs you take back?”, rather than as a score.

Why do I feel like a stranger in my own house?

Because the house learned to run without you. That is very common after a long time away, and it usually eases as you take back whole jobs and find small, regular moments with each person.

Do nightmares after a deployment mean PTSD?

Not necessarily, and this site can’t tell. Bad sleep, nightmares and feeling on edge are common after a deployment. They are worth raising with a doctor. US military families can also call Military OneSource on 800-342-9647.
