Pauses

One of you wants to talk now, the other needs space

This is one of the most common patterns between two people. One of you wants to sort it out now. The other needs to step away first. The more one pushes, the more the other pulls back, and the more one pulls back, the more the other pushes. Neither of you is doing it wrong. You are both trying to make things feel safe again, in opposite ways.

Researchers such as Andrew Christensen have studied this “demand and withdraw” pattern for decades. It shows up in many couples, and either partner can be in either role. Who pushes often depends on whose topic it is. Many people also find that when they are very upset, their body needs time to settle before they can talk well. John Gottman called this being “flooded,” and suggested a break of at least twenty minutes.

If you’re the one who wants to talk now

Being walked away from in the middle of something that matters can really hurt. It can feel like being dismissed, as if the problem, and you, don’t count. Wanting to sort it out isn’t nagging. It usually means you care, and you can’t settle while it is still open.

  • The return time is your guarantee. A pause with a clock time is not the end of the talk. It is a promise that the talk will happen, at a time when it can go well.
  • Let them go, even though it is hard. Following someone from room to room, or texting through the break, usually makes the break longer.
  • Use the time for yourself. Write down the one thing you most want them to understand. Settle your own body too: a walk, a glass of water, or the breathing pacer on Upset right now?
  • Ask for the time if they forget to give one. “Okay. When will we come back to it?”

Words you could use

“I can wait. I just need to know when. Can we say eight?”

“I’ll stop now. I’m going to write down what I want you to hear, so I don’t have to hold it all till then.”

If you’re the one who needs space

When things get heated, your mind may go blank, your heart may race, or you may feel you are about to say something you’ll regret. Stepping away can be the most caring thing you can do. It isn’t running away. But to the other person, leaving without a time can feel like being shut out.

  • A pause needs a return time. Say when you will come back, every time: “I need to stop. I’ll come back at eight.”
  • Say why, in a few words. “I’m too worked up to listen well” tells them it is about your state, not about them.
  • Coming back on time is the hard part. When the break is over, it is tempting to let it drift and hope it has blown over. Coming back on time, even if you don’t feel ready to solve anything, is what makes the next pause easier for both of you.
  • Use the time to calm down, not to build your case. Try not to rehearse the argument.

Words you could use

“I’m not leaving you. I’m leaving the argument for half an hour. I’ll be back at quarter to nine, and we’ll pick it up.”

“I’m back. I still don’t have all the answers, but I’m here and I’m listening.”

For both of you

Agree how pauses work while you are calm, not in the middle of one. That is what the plan below is for. Once it is agreed, a pause is just the plan, not a rejection, and coming back is just the plan, not giving in.

Questions people often ask

What if my partner leaves and doesn’t come back when they said?

Give it a little grace first: a few minutes late is not breaking the plan. If the time has clearly passed, send one calm message, not ten: “It’s eight. I’m ready when you are. What time works?” Then do something kind for yourself while you wait. Later, when things are calm, talk about it as a problem with the plan, not with them. Was the break too short? Was the time too soon? Would a different first sentence make coming back easier? If pauses keep ending without anyone coming back, and the topic is never picked up again, that is worth raising on its own at a check-in, or with a counselor.

What if I need longer than they can wait?

Say so, and give a time anyway: “I need until tomorrow morning. I’ll bring it up at breakfast.” A long pause with a time is easier to wait through than a short one without.

What if they won’t let me take a break?

Say calmly what you are doing and when you will be back, then go, and come back on time. Keeping that promise is how pauses come to feel safe. If you are ever afraid to leave the room, or afraid of what will happen when you come back, that is not a communication problem: see Not safe at home?

Is one of us the problem?

No. The pattern is between you. Each move makes sense on its own, and each one sets off the other. Changing your own side, by naming a return time or by letting go for twenty minutes, is often enough to soften the whole thing.
