Texting styles and pace

In the early weeks, you have very little to go on. So small signals carry a lot of weight: how fast someone replies, how long their messages are, whether they suggest the next date. It’s natural to read meaning into all of it. Often the meaning isn’t there.

Why styles differ

People text differently for ordinary reasons. Shift work, a busy job, children at home, or a phone left in a bag. Some people think out loud in many short messages. Others write one careful reply. Some find texting tiring and would much rather talk. For many people with ADHD, a reply can be meant and then forgotten. For many autistic people, a short, plain message is simply clear, not cold.

Pace is the same. Some people know quickly that they like someone and want to say so. Others need many dates before they feel sure. A slower pace is not a lack of interest, and a faster one is not automatically a problem. It becomes one when someone pushes you to match their pace (see Just different, or a real concern?).

In everyday life

Say your style once, plainly, early on. Ask theirs. Then believe what they tell you, rather than the gaps between messages.

For example (made up): Marcus, 52, recently divorced, texted his new date Leila good morning and good night every day. Leila, a teacher, sent a reply at lunch and one in the evening. Marcus began to wonder if she was losing interest. He nearly sent “Everything OK?” for the third time. Instead he wrote: “I’m a chatty texter. Tell me if that’s too much. I don’t mind if you’re not.” Leila replied, “Not too much, I like it. I just can’t text in class. Call tonight?”

Words you could use

“I’m a chatty texter. Tell me if that’s too much. I don’t mind if you’re not.”

“I’m better on the phone than by text. Could we talk instead sometimes?”

“I like you, and I like going slowly. Both are true.”

“When I go quiet, I’m usually just busy. If it’s ever more than that, I’ll say.”

Try this: Take the 2-minute style quiz to find how you think, talk and listen. If you both take it, compare your codes over coffee. For a single message you’re unsure about, the Chat analyzer shows how it might land.

How close, how fast: attachment as degrees

You may have seen people sorted into “anxious”, “avoidant” and “secure” types online. The idea comes from real research on attachment, but the sorting into boxes is too simple.

What research suggests

Adult attachment research began with Hazan and Shaver (1987), who looked at romantic love as an attachment process. Later, Brennan, Clark and Shaver (1998) brought many questionnaires together and found that they mostly measure two broad things: attachment anxiety (how much someone worries about being left or not loved enough) and avoidance (how uncomfortable someone is with closeness and depending on others). Fraley and colleagues (2015) tested whether people fall into distinct types and found that they don’t: these differences work more like dials, with each person somewhere along each one.

That matters. Nobody is a fixed “type.” Most people sit somewhere in the middle, and where you sit can shift with a different relationship, a calmer time, or a partner who is reliably kind.

In everyday life

Early dating tends to turn the dials up. Someone higher on anxiety may feel a slow reply as a small alarm. Someone higher on avoidance may feel a lot of contact as pressure, and pull back. Neither is wrong. What helps is noticing your own pattern and saying what helps you, without asking the other person to be your whole reassurance.

Words you could use

“I get a bit anxious when things are new. A quick ‘busy, talk later’ really helps me.”

“I need some time on my own to recharge. It doesn’t mean I’m pulling away from you.”

Small moments count most

Most of the time you spend with someone is ordinary. A joke, a photo sent from the bus, a sigh about work. Each one is a small reach for attention: a “bid.”

What research suggests

Driver and Gottman (2004) watched 49 newlywed couples during an ordinary day in a lab apartment, and later during a disagreement. Couples who turned toward each other’s everyday bids more often showed more humour and affection when they later disagreed. It was a small, observational study, so it can’t prove cause, but it fits a wider pattern: warmth in small moments seems to carry into hard ones. Gable and colleagues (2004) found something similar for good news: when someone shares something good and the listener responds with real interest, people report feeling closer.

In everyday life

Early on, answering small bids is easy and fun. The habit is worth keeping. Reply to the meme. Ask about the interview. Be glad with them about good news, out loud.

Words you could use

“Wait, you got the job? Tell me everything. What did they say?”

“Can’t talk right now, but I want to hear about it. Call at eight?”

Just different, or a real concern?

Early on, it’s hard to tell a quirk from a real concern. Most things are quirks. Here are common examples side by side. One item on its own rarely tells you much; a pattern of several on the right does.

Often just differentWorth taking seriously
They ask you to text when you’re home safe, or not to flirt with an ex.They track where you are, or read your messages without agreement.
They text less than you.They demand you reply fast, or get angry when you don’t.
They like a lot of time alone.They push you to give up time with your friends, or sulk every time you go.
They say “I like you” sooner or later than you.They push you to commit or move in fast, and go cold when you slow down.
They’re close to their family.They cut you off from yours, or speak badly of everyone close to you.
They’re careful with money.They ask you for money, especially if you’ve only met online.
They want to wait before sex, or before meeting friends.They push after you say no, or sulk and punish you for saying it.
You disagree sometimes.You feel afraid of their mood, or plan your words around it.

Saying no, or asking for something out of care or respect for the relationship, is not control. That’s true for partners, family and friends alike. A request leaves room to talk it through. Control takes away someone’s freedom more broadly: cutting them off from people, watching them without agreement, controlling their money, threats, or leaving them afraid. That is the pattern behind the right-hand column. Early intensity can feel wonderful at first. It becomes a concern when it comes with pressure, and when warmth is taken away the moment you say no.

A quick test: around them, do you feel more like yourself, or less? Do you feel free to disagree? If you feel smaller, quieter or more careful, trust that, even if you can’t yet explain it. When it’s more than a rough patch lists the signs in full.

Safer dating, especially online

  • Meet somewhere public for the first few dates. Get yourself there and back.
  • Tell a friend where you’ll be and who with.
  • Never send money, gift cards or bank details to someone you haven’t met. This is the most common pattern in romance scams, and it can happen at any age.
  • Keep your address and workplace to yourself until you know someone well.

Words you could use

“I’m not going to share my location. It isn’t about trust. It’s just how I like things.”

“I said no, and I need that to be okay.”

To a friend: “Can I text you when I get home tonight?”

The big questions, a little at a time

Many people avoid big questions early because they don’t want to seem too serious. But finding out late that you want very different things hurts more.

What research suggests

Closeness tends to grow as people share more about themselves, step by step. In a well-known lab study, Aron and colleagues (1997) gave pairs of strangers questions that slowly got more personal. Those pairs felt closer afterwards than pairs who made small talk. It created closeness for an afternoon, not a relationship, but it shows the shape of things: a little more each time, and both sides sharing.

In everyday life

Match the size of the question to how close you are. Early: what you’re looking for in general, and whether you’re seeing other people. As things grow: children, faith, money, where you might live. When things are physical: what feels right, and checking in as you go.

  • Exclusivity. Don’t assume. Ask, and say where you are first. If you’re both open to more than one relationship, agree what that means in practice.
  • Children. Wanting them, having them, not wanting them, or not sure. Each is a real answer, and it’s kinder to know.
  • Faith and culture. Not just belief, but daily life: food, festivals, family expectations.
  • Money. Your general style, any debts once things get serious, and who pays for what now.
  • Sex and closeness. Consent is ongoing: a yes yesterday isn’t a yes today, and anyone can stop at any time. A good partner is glad to check in, and glad when you do.

For example (made up): Dee, 29, was dating Morgan and loved it. She also knew she wanted children one day. On the fourth date she said, “This might be early, but kids matter a lot to me. Is that something you think about?” Morgan laughed: “I was hoping you’d bring it up first.” They didn’t have to agree on everything that night. They just knew where to start.

Words you could use

“This might be early, but kids matter a lot to me. Is that something you think about?”

“I’m not seeing anyone else, and I’d like it to stay that way. Where are you with that?”

“My faith is a big part of my week. Can I tell you what it looks like?”

“Is this okay? Tell me if you want to slow down. I won’t mind.”

A first disagreement, done kindly

The first disagreement can feel bigger than it is, because you don’t know yet how the other person fights, or how you’ll make up.

What research suggests

Research on couples suggests that how a hard talk starts shapes how it goes. Carrère and Gottman (1999) found that the first three minutes of newlywed couples’ disagreements predicted a lot about how things went over the following years: harsher starts went with worse outcomes. That was one research group’s sample, so it isn’t a precise forecast for anyone. Other work, such as Gottman and Levenson (1992), found that what matters is not whether couples argue, but whether warmth and repair outweigh the negative moments.

In everyday life

  • Start with how you felt and what you’d like, not with what they always do.
  • Stay on one topic. Early on, it’s easy to bring in every small doubt at once.
  • Say back what you heard before you answer.
  • Take a break if either of you is flooded, and name a time to come back.
  • Notice how repair goes. Can they say sorry? Can you? This is one of the most useful things you can learn early.

For example (made up): Jae and Ren had been dating three months when Ren cancelled plans twice for work. Jae felt hurt and nearly said, “You obviously don’t care.” Instead: “I was really looking forward to Friday, and I felt let down. I know work is mad right now. Can we find a time that’s safe from it?” Ren apologised, and blocked out Sunday mornings.

Words you could use

“I felt let down on Friday. Can we talk about it?”

“So what I’m hearing is you felt rushed. Have I got that right?”

“I’m too wound up to talk well. Can we pick this up after dinner?”

“I’m sorry. I was sharp with you, and you didn’t deserve that.”

Meeting friends and family

Meeting someone’s people is a step into their life. It can be lovely and nerve-wracking at once. It also tells you a lot: how they talk about you, and how they are around the people who know them best.

  • Go at the slower person’s pace. Some people need to be sure before introducing a partner, especially to children or older relatives.
  • Brief each other. Who’ll be there, any topics to avoid, any family tension to know about.
  • Agree an exit signal and a rough leaving time.
  • If you aren’t out to your family, talk with your partner about what that means for meeting them, and what you each need. Coming out: with a partner covers this.
  • Two faiths or cultures? Families may have worries they don’t say to you directly. Each partner leads with their own family. See Each of you tells your own family.
  • When someone disapproves, listen for the worry underneath. Then decide together what you’ll do, without asking your partner to choose between you and their family.

Words you could use

“My dad can seem gruff. He’s nervous with new people. Just ask about his garden.”

To a parent: “I know you worry because you love me. I’m taking this slowly, and I’d like you to give them a chance.”

To your partner: “My family’s reaction isn’t a verdict on you. I chose you, and I’m staying chosen.”

Dating as a parent

Dating as a parent means two timelines: yours, and your children’s. That isn’t a burden on a new relationship. It’s part of who you are, and the right person will respect it.

What research suggests

Studies of children whose parents separate and re-partner, such as Hetherington and Kelly (2002), found that most children of divorce and remarriage turn out reasonably well in the long run, though it varies a lot from child to child. Going slowly with introductions, keeping routines steady and keeping conflict between the parents low are widely recommended ways to help. Amato (2001) also found that, on average, differences between children of divorced and married parents were small, which is reassuring for parents who worry they’ve already done harm.

In everyday life

  • Keep early dating separate. Children don’t need to meet everyone you see.
  • Introduce once it’s steady. Keep the first meeting short, neutral and fun.
  • Tell the other parent first, if you share parenting. They should hear it from you, not from the children.
  • No secrets for children to keep, and no questions to them about the other home.
  • Teenagers may be embarrassed, protective or hostile. Let them have feelings about it without making them responsible for yours.
  • If both of you have children, introductions between the children can come later still.

Words you could use

To a teen: “I’ve been seeing someone. You don’t have to like it, and you don’t have to meet them yet. I just didn’t want you to find out another way.”

To a young child: “Sam is my friend. We’re all going to the park on Saturday.”

To someone new: “I can’t do last-minute plans on my weeks with the kids. Can we plan ahead?”

Moving in: decide, don’t slide

What research suggests

Stanley, Rhoades and Markman (2006) described a pattern they called “sliding versus deciding.” Many couples drift into living together without ever making a clear choice: more nights at one flat, a shared pet, a lease that runs out. Once you share a home, money and a lease, leaving becomes harder, even if you are no more sure about the relationship than before. Their suggestion is not that living together is wrong, but that deciding on purpose, and talking about what it means, tends to go better than sliding.

In everyday life

Before you move, talk through the practical and the personal:

  • Why now? Because you’re ready, or because a lease is ending?
  • What does it mean to each of you? A step towards a future, or a way to save rent? Both are fine if you both know.
  • Bills and money. Half each, or by income? One owner per bill. See Money together: moving in.
  • Jobs at home. Who does what, written down, so it doesn’t drift onto one person. Use Who does what? to see it together.
  • House rules. Guests, quiet time, tidiness, pets. Write the few you agree in House rules for us.
  • If it doesn’t work out. Whose name is on the lease, and who keeps what. It’s not unromantic to ask. It’s kind to both of you.
  • Children. If either of you has children, moving in changes their home too. Read Blended families and stepfamilies first.

Words you could use

“I’d love to live with you. Can we spend an evening talking about why and how, before we look at flats?”

“I’m not saying no. I’m saying not yet. Can we look at it again in the spring?”

“Let’s each write down how we’d split the bills and jobs, then compare.”

Living in a shared flat where one of you is always staying over? See Sharing a room for being fair to housemates.

Keeping your own life

In the rush of something new, it’s easy to drop everything else. A little of that is normal. But friends, hobbies, family and time alone keep you steady, and they keep you interesting to each other.

  • Keep one regular thing that is just yours.
  • Keep seeing friends, including friends who knew you before.
  • Notice if one of you wants more together-time than the other. That’s an ordinary difference. Talk about it before it becomes a sore spot.
  • If someone resents your time with others, or makes you feel guilty for it, look again at what’s worth taking seriously.

Words you could use

“I love our weekends. I’d also like one evening a week with my friends. Does that feel okay?”

To a friend: “I’ve been rubbish at keeping in touch since I met Ana. I miss you. Coffee this week?”

Ending it kindly, and being ended with

Not every relationship should last, and ending one that isn’t right is not a failure. How you end it matters, for both of you.

If you are ending it

  • Be clear. “I don’t want to keep seeing each other” is kinder than a slow fade that leaves them guessing.
  • Match the method to the time together. After a date or two, a short message. After months, a call or a meeting somewhere quiet.
  • Give a reason only if it helps, and keep it about you: “I don’t feel it’s right for me.” A list of their faults doesn’t help anyone.
  • Don’t leave the door half open if you don’t mean it.
  • Practical things: return belongings, sort shared bills, and agree what you’ll tell mutual friends.

If you’re afraid of how they’ll react

Your safety comes first. End it by message or phone, from somewhere safe. Tell a friend. Don’t agree to a private meeting to “talk it through.” Ending a controlling relationship can be a risky time, so a helpline can help you plan. See Not safe at home?

If it ends for you

It’s allowed to hurt, even after a short time. You don’t need a full explanation to move on, and asking for one rarely helps. Lean on friends. Keep to your routines. If you share children, keep your messages about the children short and calm. On my own has more.

Words you could use

“Thank you for the evenings. I don’t feel it’s a match for me, and I wanted to tell you rather than go quiet. I wish you well.”

“I’ve thought about this a lot. I don’t want to go on with this relationship. I care about you, and I’m sorry this hurts.”

“I’d rather not go over the reasons. I’m sure, and I’m sorry.”

“Thanks for telling me straight. I’m sad, but I’d rather know.”

For kind, clear ways to say no in general, see Say no kindly.

Your situation

Dating after a divorce or long relationship

It can feel like the rules changed while you were away. Apps, texting, how fast things move. You don’t have to adopt anyone else’s pace. Old hurts can surface in a new relationship: a partner’s late reply can echo an ex’s lies, for example. Name it rather than act on it: “That’s an old sore spot for me, not something you did.” See On my own after a breakup or divorce.

Dating after losing a partner

Loving someone new doesn’t replace the person you lost. Many people carry both. Grown-up children may worry about loyalty, money or being forgotten; tell them early, kindly, and give them time. A new partner may wonder how much room there is for them. Say plainly that there is.

“I loved Rob for forty years, and I always will. I’m also enjoying getting to know you.”

To a grown-up child: “Nobody is replacing your mum. I’d like you to meet someone who makes me happy, when you’re ready.”

Later-life dating

Later in life, practical questions come sooner: health, homes, pensions, wills, and grandchildren. Many couples choose to “live apart together,” keeping their own homes. Talk about money and inheritance openly, and early enough that nobody feels ambushed. If grown-up children are worried, listen to the worry, and keep the decisions yours. When families join, adult stepchildren has more.

Dating when you’re neurodivergent

Dating leans heavily on unwritten rules: hints, timing, reading faces. Many autistic people and people with ADHD find it tiring to guess. You can make the rules explicit. Say how you communicate, what you need from dates, and what you find hard. The right person will find that clear and kind, not strange.

  • Ask directly rather than guessing: “Did you have a good time? Would you like to meet again?”
  • Choose dates that suit your senses: a walk, a museum, a quiet café.
  • If you forget to reply, say so early, and agree what a gentle nudge looks like.
  • If you mask a lot, notice whether you can relax around this person over time.

“I take things literally, so please just tell me directly. I’ll do the same.”

“I’m autistic. I might not make much eye contact, but I’m really listening.”

“I forget to reply, not because I don’t care. A quick nudge is fine.”

Every kind of couple

Everything here applies whatever your genders and whoever you date. Some LGBTQ+ people date in smaller communities, where everyone knows everyone and an ex may be at every party. Kindness in ending matters even more there. If you aren’t out to everyone, agree together what each of you says to whom. If you are dating more than one person openly, agreements about honesty, time and safer sex need saying out loud: see More than two. Exercises

1. Your texting and talking style

Write three lines: how often you like to hear from someone, by what means, and what a slow reply means when it comes from you. Share it if you’d like to.

2. Matters and flexes

List three things that really matter to you in a relationship, and three you could flex on. Notice whether any “matters” is something you haven’t mentioned yet.

3. More or less myself?

After your next few dates, jot one word for how you felt about yourself afterwards. Look back at the words after a month.

4. Your repair words

Write the sentence you’d like to be able to say after you’ve been sharp with someone. Practise it out loud once, so it’s there when you need it.

5. Before moving in

Each of you writes down, separately, why you want to live together, how you’d split bills and jobs, and what happens if it doesn’t work out. Then compare.

Questions people often ask

How soon should we talk about being exclusive?

When you’d like to know, ask. There’s no rule about timing. Saying where you are first makes it easier for them to answer honestly.

Am I “anxious” or “avoidant”?

Probably neither, as a type. Research suggests these are degrees, not boxes, and most people sit somewhere in the middle. Notice what helps you feel steady, and say it.

Is falling for someone fast a bad sign?

No. Some people simply know quickly. It becomes a concern when it comes with pressure: pushing you to commit, move in or cut off others, or going cold when you say no.

How long should we date before moving in?

There’s no right number. What matters more is deciding on purpose, talking about why, how you’ll share money and jobs, and what happens if it doesn’t work out.

Should I tell someone I’m autistic or have ADHD?

That’s your choice, and your timing. You don’t have to share a diagnosis to say what helps you: “I like plans made ahead” or “Please tell me directly.”

My grown-up children don’t like that I’m dating. What can I do?

Listen for the worry underneath, which is often loyalty to a parent who died or left, or worry about money. Reassure them about what won’t change. Then keep the decision about your own life with you.

The limits of this page

This page gives general, everyday guidance. It can’t know your situation, and it isn’t therapy or legal advice. The research it mentions mostly studied couples in a few countries, often newlyweds or students, so treat it as a guide to patterns, not a rule for any one relationship. If you feel afraid, controlled or pressured, go straight to Not safe at home?. For support with old hurts or a pattern that keeps repeating, Help beyond this site lists ways to find a counsellor.
