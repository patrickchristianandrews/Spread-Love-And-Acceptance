Retirement

Retired and both home now

For years, one or both of you left the house most days. Now you are both home, all day. Many couples look forward to this and are still surprised by how much rubbing there is at first. It doesn’t mean anything is wrong between you. Two people are sharing a house and a day that used to belong mostly to one of them. Read the part you need.

Two people, one house, all day

When one of you has run the house for years, the house has a way of working: when the shopping happens, where the scissors live, how the dishwasher is loaded. The partner who has just retired walks into a system that already works, without a map of it. The partner who has run it can feel invaded. The one who has just arrived can feel like a guest in their own home.

Both feelings are fair. A few things help:

  • Say what each of you needs to keep. A quiet morning, a room or a corner that is yours, the kitchen to yourself at certain hours. Say it as your need, not as a rule for the other person.
  • Agree a loose shape for the day. Not a timetable. Just what usually happens in the morning, around lunch and in the evening, so neither of you is guessing.
  • Keep some of the old rhythm. If the house was always quiet until eleven, that habit was holding something up for someone. Talk about it before you drop it.
  • Expect a settling-in time. Research on couples and retirement, for example by Phyllis Moen and colleagues in the US, suggests the first stretch can be a bumpier time for a marriage, especially when partners retire at different times, and that it often settles as new routines form. These studies show patterns across many couples, not what will happen to you.

Re-dividing the jobs: whole jobs, not “helping”

If one partner has always run the house, the newly retired partner often starts by helping: carrying things, asking “What can I do?”, doing a bit here and there. It is kindly meant. But helping still leaves one person holding the whole list in their head: noticing, deciding, checking and reminding. Studies have found that the split of housework often changes only a little after retirement. Unless you decide otherwise, the person who ran the house tends to keep running it.

What works better is handing over whole jobs. A whole job is the noticing, the planning and the doing. “You do the grocery shopping” is helping. “You own food: you notice what’s low, plan the meals, keep the list and shop” is a whole job.

Some whole jobs that often move well:

  • Food for the week: meal ideas, the list, the shopping, putting it away.
  • The garden, from deciding what to plant to booking someone for the trees.
  • Bills, the post and the paperwork.
  • Appointments and prescriptions, for both of you.
  • Laundry, from the basket to the drawer.
  • Birthdays and cards for the wider family.

The One owner per job sheet helps you write the list and give each job one owner. The Lemonade Stand shows the jobs and hours side by side, so you can both see how the week is really split before you change it.

Fair doesn’t have to mean half each. One of you may still work part-time, have a health condition or care for a parent. What matters is that you both agree the split works for this new season.

Handing over a job without “you’re doing it wrong”

This is the hard part for the partner who has run the house. When someone takes over a job you have done for thirty years, they will do it differently, and sometimes not as well at first. If you step in, correct it or quietly redo it, the job slides back to you, and the other person learns it was never really theirs.

This site’s idea

A handover in three parts:

  1. Show the job once, with the hidden parts. Not just the shop, but how you know what’s low, which brand the grandchildren like, which day the store is quiet.
  2. Agree what “done” means. Only the few things that really matter: the bins go out on Tuesday night, the tablets never run out. Everything else is the new owner’s to decide.
  3. Then let go. Different isn’t wrong. If something truly matters, raise it once, at a calm time, not in the moment.

If you are taking the job over: ask your questions early, then own it. Keep your own list instead of asking every time. If you change how it is done, that is allowed.

Words you could use

If you have run the house: “I’d like you to take food off my plate completely, the planning and the list too. I’ll show you how I do it once, and then it’s yours, even if you do it differently.”

If you have run the house: “I’ve noticed I keep checking on the laundry. I’m going to stop. Tell me if you want my help.”

If you’re newly retired: “I don’t want to just help. Which whole job could I take off you?”

If you’re newly retired: “I’d like to do this my way for a month. If something really bothers you, can we talk about it on Sunday instead of in the moment?”

Feeling useless, or in the way

Leaving work can take away more than a job. It can take away a reason to get up, people to talk to, the feeling of being good at something, and a place to be every day. Some people feel lost for a while, or quietly low. At home, that can look like hovering, taking over the kitchen, rearranging the cupboards, or following the other person from room to room.

That is not a character flaw. It usually means you need a life outside the house as well as a role inside it. The house can’t be your whole new job, and your partner can’t be your whole social life.

  • Pick one thing with a regular time and other people. A class, a club, volunteering, a walking group, a part-time job, a place of worship, or set days with the grandchildren.
  • Find something you are good at. Teaching a skill, fixing things, mentoring, or a project you never had time for.
  • Move your body most days. A walk counts.
  • Keep in touch with work friends you liked. A monthly lunch is plenty.

If you feel low most days for more than a couple of weeks, or stop enjoying things you used to, talk to your doctor. It is common after a big change, and it can be helped.

If you are the partner who has been home all along, it can help to say plainly that you are glad they are home, and that you also need some of the day to yourself. Both can be true.

Words you could use

If you’re newly retired: “I think I’ve been hovering because I don’t quite know what to do with myself yet. It isn’t about you. I’m going to find something for Tuesday and Thursday mornings.”

If you have been home all along: “I love having you home. I also need my mornings, the way I had them before. Could we keep them, and have lunch together most days?”

Time apart and time together

When you are both home all day, time together stops being special by itself, and time apart has to be made on purpose. Both matter.

  • Time apart. Each of you has some hours, a room or an activity that is just yours. Wanting time on your own is not rejection.
  • Time together, on purpose. Something you plan and look forward to: a walk, a weekly lunch out, a trip, learning something new side by side. Being in the same house isn’t the same as being together.
  • Small moments in between. Telling each other something you read, a cup of tea brought without asking, a hand on the shoulder. Turning toward has seven small, everyday ways to do this.

Say how much of each you would like, instead of guessing. One of you may want to spend most days together, and the other may want more space. Neither is wrong. Look for a mix that works for both of you.

Talking about it without blame

Most retirement rubs come from the setup, not from either person. Nobody planned how two people would share a house all day, so it fell into place by accident. You can redesign it together.

  • Pick a time, not a moment. Talk once a week, at a set time, about how the new arrangement is going. Check-ins shows a simple way: one topic, hear it back, then answer.
  • Start with what is working, then pick one thing to change.
  • Talk about the setup, not the person. “The kitchen gets crowded at five” lands better than “You’re always under my feet.”

Words you could use

Either of you: “Can we have half an hour on Sunday to talk about how we’re sharing the house now? It isn’t a complaint. I think we’ve both been guessing.”

Either of you: “What’s one thing about being home together that you love, and one thing you’d change?”
