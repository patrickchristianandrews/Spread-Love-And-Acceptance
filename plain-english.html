IN PLAIN TERMS

This program borrows words from accounting, radio engineering and nervous-system science. The borrowed words are shorthand, not a requirement. Every one of them has a plain meaning, and this page gives it in one sentence.

How to use this page: if a term stops you anywhere on the site, look it up here and carry on. You will never need the technical name to use a worksheet.

Terms are grouped by what they describe rather than alphabetically, so related ideas sit together. Each one links to the page where it actually gets used.

Words about you

Inner weather
How you are right now: settled, revved up, or shut down. Called weather because it changes on its own and says nothing about your character. Today’s Weather →
Nervous system ladder
A simple picture of three body states stacked like rungs. Top rung: calm enough to listen. Middle: revved up. Bottom: shut down. Check yours →
Ventral vagal
The clinical name for the top rung: settled, able to hear hard things without going on the defensive. Chapter III →
Sympathetic
The clinical name for revved up: heart rate up, everything feels urgent, small things land as personal. Chapter III →
Dorsal vagal
The clinical name for shut down: flat, far away, foggy, words hard to find. Chapter III →
Polyvagal theory
The body of work those three names come from. It is a useful map, and it is debated among researchers. You never need the theory to use the ladder. Chapter III →
Autonomic saturation
How full your stress tank already was before this moment. A full tank makes small things feel big. Plain version: how much you were already carrying. WP-02 →
Autonomic arousal
Your body revving up: faster heart, tighter chest, sharper voice. It happens before you decide anything. WP-11 →
Sensory gating
Letting a comment land for a second before you react to it, instead of answering the first sting. Chapter IV →
Alexithymia
Difficulty naming your own feelings, even strong ones. Not the same as not having them. Wired Differently →
Neurotype / wiring
The way a particular brain handles noise, change, conversation and stress. Autistic, ADHD, anxious, neurotypical and others are all wirings. Wired Differently →

Words about the two of you

Carrier wave
The tone underneath your words. Two people can say reasonable things in tones that clash, and the clash is what gets heard. Chapter I →
Heterodyne squeal
What two radio signals do when they are close but not identical: a squeal. Borrowed here for the noise two reasonable people make when slightly out of step. Chapter I →
Static
The noise that appears in the gap between two people. Nobody emits it alone. How it works →
Frequency
Your pace, tone, urgency, and how much closeness or space you need. Frequency Calibration →
Phase-locked loop (WP-13)
An engineering term for two signals staying in step by checking in constantly. Here: a 90-second daily check-in so small things stay small. WP-13 →
Double empathy
The finding that misunderstanding between different neurotypes runs both ways. Neither person is the broken one. Wired Differently →
Mood arbitrage
Changing the feel of a tense moment on purpose, usually with warmth or humour, so the actual point can be heard. Mood Arbitrage →
Transducer (WP-09)
A device that converts one kind of signal into another. Here: turning a raw reaction into fact, feeling and a clear ask. WP-09 →

Words about the work

Unbilled debt
Work that gets done but never counted: remembering, planning, noticing, smoothing over. It builds up like a debt only one person can see. The Preface →
Invisible labour
The same idea in the usual words. The noticing and remembering that keeps a household or a team running. The Preface →
RACI (WP-03)
A project-management habit: for every recurring task, name who does it and who notices if it did not happen. Plain version: one owner per chore. WP-03 →
Deficit audit (WP-04)
A monthly look back at what keeps going wrong, to tell a structural gap from a bad week. WP-04 →
Solvency / P(Solvency)
Borrowed from accounting: can this arrangement keep running as it is? It scores the arrangement, never a person. Chapter II →
Epistemic
To do with how you know something. Used here to insist that a number is only as good as what went into it. Chapter II →
Deontological parity
Agreeing in advance what counts as fair, before you argue about whether something was. Chapter IV →
Ocular vectors
Seven angles two people can be looking at the same friction from. Plain version: which part of the elephant each of you is touching. Chapter III →
Neutral refusal
A ready-made, kind way of saying no that does not start a fight. WP-01 →
Heuristic
A rule of thumb that is useful without being exact. The whole program is one, which is why nothing here is a diagnosis. How it works →
