Wired Differently

How different neurotypes receive the same words, and how to speak so the message you meant is the message that arrives.

Full version← Back to the simple version

A text, read twice

“Fine.” One of you meant it literally: that is fine, go ahead. The other read a full stop and heard a door closing. Same four letters, two entirely different messages.

Chapter I describes a household argument as two radios tuned close to, but not exactly on, the same frequency. This page adds a second layer to that picture. Even when two people are tuned to the same station, the receivers themselves are built differently. One receiver picks up every word literally. One loses the second half of a long sentence. One hears a flat tone as anger, and another hears a period at the end of a text as a slammed door. None of these receivers is broken. They were built to different specifications, and the static comes from nobody knowing what spec the other person runs on.

Below you'll find how each common wiring tends to take in information, a set of examples showing how the same words can sound different to different people, and practical ways to talk to someone wired differently than you. An honest note before you read on

Everything here describes tendencies that research and lived experience report across groups. It does not describe any one person. Two autistic people can be as different from each other as any two neurotypical people, and many people carry more than one of these wirings at once. This page is not a diagnostic tool and can't tell you what anyone has. The most reliable source of information about how someone receives words is that person, so use this page to find better questions to ask, not to answer those questions for them.

A few words, used carefully

Neurotype means the general way a brain is built to take in, sort and respond to information. Neurotypical describes the wiring most social customs were designed around. Neurodivergent describes wiring that differs from that design in a meaningful way. Estimates suggest 15 to 20 percent of people worldwide are neurodivergent, a figure that is likely low because many people never get assessed (Understood). For scale, the CDC's latest count identifies about 1 in 31 U.S. children as autistic (HHS), and an estimated 15.5 million U.S. adults, 6 percent, have a current ADHD diagnosis (CHADD, citing Staley et al., 2024).

This page covers three kinds of wiring, and labels which is which. Some are neurodevelopmental, meaning present from early life: autism, ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia, auditory processing differences, developmental language disorder and Tourette syndrome. Some are traits that run across every group: alexithymia and high sensitivity. And some are conditions that change how words land even though they aren't neurotypes in the strict sense: anxiety, trauma and OCD. They're included because they reshape the receiver just as much, and because they often travel with the others.

On language: in a large international survey of 654 autistic adults, most preferred identity-first terms like "autistic person" to "person with autism" (Keating et al., 2023), and this page follows that preference. The same study's main recommendation is the one to follow in your own home: ask each person which words they prefer.

The problem runs in both directions

When two people are wired differently and misunderstand each other, the usual assumption is that the neurodivergent person "has trouble communicating." The research says something more even-handed: both people have trouble reading each other, because each is using a decoder built for their own kind of signal.

Dr Damian Milton named this the double empathy problem: when people with very different experiences of the world interact, the breakdown in understanding is mutual, not a deficit sitting inside one person (National Autistic Society). The evidence behind it is worth knowing, including where it's still being tested:

  • In a 2020 experiment, 72 adults passed a 30-detail story down chains of eight people. All-autistic chains passed on as much detail as all-non-autistic chains, while mixed chains lost detail faster and reported lower rapport (Crompton et al., 2020).
  • A much larger preregistered replication, with 311 participants across Edinburgh, Nottingham and Dallas, did not find the extra information loss in mixed chains. It did find that rapport was lower in mixed and autistic chains than in non-autistic chains (Nature Human Behaviour, 2025). The honest reading: mixed pairs can pass facts along fine, but the conversation tends to feel harder to both people.
  • Non-autistic observers form less favorable first impressions of autistic people within seconds. A 2025 review of 29 studies found the bias appeared across audio, video and combined recordings, but was not significant when observers read only transcripts of the words (systematic review of first impressions). The judgment was about the delivery, not the content.
  • Non-autistic people also find it harder to read autistic people's emotions. In one study, 81 non-autistic adults tracking spoken accounts were less accurate at following the feelings of autistic narrators, especially sadness and happiness (Cheang et al., 2024).

The practical lesson applies to every pairing on this page, not only autistic and neurotypical. When a message goes wrong between two different wirings, don't open a case against either person. Assume two decoders, both partly accurate, and compare notes.

Three channels, tuned differently

Chapter I splits every message into three channels: pace (speed and timing), register (the tone underneath the words) and urgency (how soon something matters). Each wiring is more or less sensitive on each channel, and most mismatches happen on one of them.

Neurotypical

THE DEFAULT MOST SOCIAL RULES WERE BUILT FOR

How information arrives

Neurotypical listeners decode a message on several channels at once: the words, the tone, the face, the timing and what the relationship has been like lately. When those disagree, they tend to trust the tone over the words. They also expect a certain amount of indirectness, because softening a request or an opinion is how their culture protects the other person's feelings. Research on indirect speech shows people choose less direct wording precisely when they expect disagreement, to avoid friction (Achimova, Franke & Butz, 2025). The flip side is that neurotypical receivers read hidden meaning into messages that didn't contain any.

How things can sound

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"No."Rejection, maybe angerWithout a softener or reason, a bare no reads as cold. The speaker may simply have answered the question.
"ok." (text)Annoyed or passive-aggressiveIn a study of 126 students, one-word text replies ending in a period were rated less sincere than the same replies without it (Gunraj et al., 2016).
A long pause before answeringReluctance, disapproval or a lie being preparedNeurotypical conversation runs on quick turn-taking, so silence gets read as a signal.
"That's not how you load a dishwasher.""You're incompetent."A correction about a task gets heard as a judgment about the person.
No eye contact while listeningNot listening, or hiding somethingEye contact is a listening signal in neurotypical culture. For many people it isn't how they listen.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Add the social layer a blunt message is missing: "No, not tonight. I'm wiped out, it's not about you."
  • Say out loud what your face or tone might not be showing: "I'm not upset, this is just my thinking face."
  • Announce your pauses: "Give me a minute to think about that."
  • If your texts are short by habit, say so once, in advance, so they stop being decoded.

When they're the one talking

  • A hint is often a request. "It's cold in here" can mean "please close the window."
  • "Maybe" and "we'll see" are often a soft no.
  • "I'm fine" after a hard moment may mean "ask me again later."
  • If you can't tell, it's fair to ask: "Is that a request, or just an observation?"

Autistic

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL

How information arrives

Many autistic people take words at their stated meaning first, and find it harder to extract meaning from body language, facial expressions, tone, speed, interruptions and non-literal language like idioms, jokes, irony and sarcasm. Many prefer direct, precise language, may use fewer gestures, and may not make eye contact (National Autistic Society). A meta-analysis found autistic participants had more difficulty with figurative language than non-autistic controls on average, with the gap mostly disappearing when groups were matched on language ability (Kalandadze et al., 2018).

That doesn't mean autistic adults can't read hints. In a 2024 study, autistic and non-autistic college students used context to interpret hints like "It's cold in here" equally well (Frost, Nagano & Zane, 2024). The difference is often cost, not capacity. Decoding hints, tone and faces at the same time takes deliberate effort. Monotropism theory describes autistic attention as concentrating deeply on a few things at a time, which makes it hard to process faces, tone, unspoken rules and a reply all at once (National Autistic Society). Many autistic adults spend so much effort masking, monitoring eye contact, expressions and turn-taking, that they miss the conversation itself. In one study, 86 of 92 autistic adults reported camouflaging, and exhaustion was the most consistent cost (Hull et al., 2017).

How things can sound

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"It'd be nice if someone took the trash out."A true statement about trash. No request detected.The request is in the subtext. Decoding it is possible, but not automatic when tired or focused elsewhere.
"Oh great, that's just perfect."Something is greatThe sarcasm lives in the tone. In one study, autistic adults identified ironic intent 72.5 percent of the time versus 80 percent for comparison adults (Zalla et al., 2014).
"Clean up a bit before my parents come."An unclear instruction that creates anxiety"A bit" has no defined finish line. Which rooms? To what standard? By when?
"We'll see."A real maybe, still under considerationWhen it actually means no, the later "no" can feel like a broken promise.
"Don't give me that tone."Confusing, and unfairAutistic prosody can sound flat or sharp without meaning to. Guidance for first responders notes voice tone "may sound angry, rude, or bored" when the person feels none of that (Autism Society of America).
"Look at me when I'm talking to you."A demand that makes listening harderFor many autistic people, eye contact competes with processing the words.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Say the request as a request: "Could you take the trash out before 9?"
  • Define the finish line: what, where, how much and by when.
  • Drop the sarcasm in serious conversations, or flag it: "I'm joking."
  • Say how you feel in words rather than expecting it to be read from your face: "I'm hurt, not angry."
  • Give processing time. Guidance for first responders suggests 7 to 10 seconds before repeating a question (Autism Society of America).
  • Offer writing for complex or emotional topics. In a survey of 245 autistic adults, text and face-to-face ranked highest with family, and phone calls were among the least preferred (Howard & Sedgewick, 2021).
  • During a shutdown, stop asking questions. Guidance says to avoid questions and instructions until the person has recovered (Leicestershire Partnership NHS).

When they're the one talking

  • Directness is usually efficiency, not coldness. "That's wrong" means the fact is wrong, not you.
  • Honesty is often the point. Autistic adults in one study described sarcasm, social white lies and hints as confusing or even dishonest (Sutherland et al., 2025).
  • A long explanation about a favorite topic ("info-dumping") is often an invitation to connect, not a lecture.
  • Silence can mean "I'm processing," not "I'm refusing to answer."
  • A flat tone isn't a reliable signal of their mood. Ask.

ADHD

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL

How information arrives

ADHD attention comes in bursts. During brief lapses, pieces of a message can drop out. CHADD's example: hearing "Let's meet at noon" instead of "Let's meet at the park at noon" (CHADD). Working memory is often stretched, so multi-step spoken instructions are harder to hold. Children with ADHD recalled six-step instructions less accurately than their peers in every condition tested, and physically doing the steps helped both groups (Yang et al., 2017). Time is experienced differently too, with inaccurate estimates of how long things take and trouble with prospective memory, meaning remembering to do something later (Weissenberger et al., 2021). "Later" and "sometime this week" can quietly evaporate.

Emotion runs hot. A systematic review of 22 studies found adults with ADHD had lower emotion-regulation scores than controls, and cites estimates that 34 to 70 percent experience emotional dysregulation (Soler-Gutiérrez et al., 2023). Many describe rejection sensitivity, sometimes called rejection sensitive dysphoria. It isn't a formal diagnosis, but people describe reading vague or neutral interactions as rejection, with intense emotional pain (Cleveland Clinic). In a 2026 qualitative study, students with ADHD described an unanswered text being read as rejection, and one stayed up until 4 a.m. checking their phone (Rowney-Smith et al., 2026).

How things can sound

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"Can you grab milk, drop off the package, and call your mom back?""Grab milk." The other two are gone by the parking lot.Three spoken tasks exceed what working memory holds while the mind moves on.
"Do it later."Not now, which becomes neverWithout a time or a cue, a future task has nothing to trigger it.
"You forgot again.""You're a failure and I'm tired of you."Criticism can hit with far more force than intended, especially when it echoes years of similar comments.
No reply to a text for three hoursThey're upset with meSilence is ambiguous, and rejection sensitivity fills ambiguity with rejection.
A long, careful emailSkimmed. The key ask in paragraph four was missed.Dense text competes with attention. Put the ask first.

Speaking to this wiring

  • One request at a time, or send the list in writing.
  • Swap "later" for a time or a cue: "after dinner," "by 6."
  • Lead with the ask, then the reasons.
  • Separate the behavior from the person: "The bill slipped. Can we set a reminder together?"
  • If you'll be slow to reply, say so: "Busy until 5, all good."
  • Try CHADD's "echo": ask them to repeat back what they heard (CHADD).

When they're the one talking

  • Interrupting is often fear of losing the thought, not disrespect. CHADD lists interrupting and rapid speech as impulsivity, not intent (CHADD).
  • A forgotten task is rarely a message that you don't matter.
  • A sharp reply can be a flash of emotion that passes quickly. Give it a minute before treating it as their position.
  • A tangent is often how they're connecting ideas. Ask where it leads.

Autistic and ADHD together

A COMBINATION, OFTEN CALLED AuDHD

Autism and ADHD commonly occur together. Autistica reports about 28 percent of autistic children meet ADHD criteria, and about 21 percent of children with ADHD are also autistic. Shared traits include trouble following conversations, sensory differences, executive dysfunction and rejection sensitivity, and the combination can create an internal pull between ADHD's need for novelty and autism's need for routine and structure (Autistica).

For communication, that means someone can need blunt, literal clarity and also lose the second half of a long explanation. They may crave a predictable plan and still forget it if it isn't written down. And they may take words literally while reacting intensely to perceived criticism.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"Let's play it by ear this weekend."No plan, which feels unsafe, plus nothing to rememberIt's idiomatic, unstructured and vague on timing all at once.
"Why didn't you do what I asked?"A literal question they may try to answer, while also feeling accusedIt's a rhetorical blame question, which hits two sensitivities at once.
A sudden change of plansDistressing, even if the new plan sounds funNovelty can appeal while the disruption to routine costs a lot.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Be direct, short and written: "Saturday: groceries at 10, free after 1."
  • Give changes early, with the reason and the new plan together.
  • Criticize the process, not the person, and say you're still on their side.

When they're the one talking

  • An abrupt topic jump can be both an ADHD tangent and an autistic deep dive. Both are engagement.
  • "I need a minute" is a real need, not a brush-off.

Dyslexic

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL · ABOUT 1 IN 14 PEOPLE

How information arrives

Dyslexia disrupts the processing of written language. People with dyslexia often understand spoken words easily but read slowly, reread text to understand it, and rely on memory, context or listening instead of reading (Cleveland Clinic). It has nothing to do with intelligence. In daily life, that makes the channel matter: a phone call and a wall-of-text email can carry the same message at very different costs.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
A five-paragraph text about a disagreementExhausting before they start, and easy to misread under stressThe emotional weight and the reading effort stack on top of each other.
"Did you even read my message?"ShamingThey may have read it three times and still missed a line.
"You spelled that wrong."Being judged as careless or unintelligentSpelling errors are a feature of the wiring, not effort.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Keep written messages short, with one idea per line.
  • Put anything important in the first line.
  • Offer a voice note or a call for anything emotional or complex.
  • Never comment on spelling in a personal conversation.

When they're the one talking

  • A short, blunt text may be short because typing costs effort, not because they're curt.
  • A slow reply may be a reread, not a cold shoulder.

Dyspraxic (DCD)

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL · ABOUT 5 PERCENT OF SCHOOL-AGE CHILDREN

Dyspraxia mainly affects movement and coordination, but it also shows up in conversation. Some people struggle to keep up with fast talk and leave long pauses before responding, and many have difficulty with organization, attention and memory (Dyspraxia Foundation). Handwriting is often hard, so typing is usually easier.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"It's easy, just do it like this.""Something is wrong with you if you can't.""Easy" and "just" minimize a task that genuinely costs more for their body and planning.
Rapid-fire questionsPressure, then a freezeThe reply takes longer to organize than the questions take to arrive.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Drop "just," "easy" and "obviously."
  • Give steps in order, and show rather than describe when you can.
  • Let the pause happen.

When they're the one talking

  • A long pause is them building the answer.
  • Being late or disorganized is not a statement about how much they care.

Auditory processing differences

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL · OFTEN WITH NORMAL HEARING

With auditory processing disorder, the ears work but the brain has trouble making sense of speech, especially in noisy places, when someone talks quickly or has a strong accent, with similar-sounding words, and with spoken instructions (NHS). ASHA adds longer response times, frequent requests for repetition, difficulty following complex spoken directions, and difficulty detecting the changes in tone that signal sarcasm or jokes (ASHA).

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
A request called from the next room with the TV onNoise with a few words in itBackground sound competes directly with speech.
"I already told you that."Blame for something they couldn't hear properlyIt was said, but it didn't arrive intact.
A joke delivered deadpanA serious statementThe tone shift carrying the joke is what gets lost.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Talk face to face, and don't cover your mouth.
  • Turn off the TV or move somewhere quieter for anything that matters.
  • Rephrase rather than repeat louder. The NHS recommends avoiding long, complicated sentences and speaking neither too quickly nor too slowly (NHS).
  • Follow up important plans in text.

When they're the one talking

  • "What?" is a real question, not stalling.
  • An answer that seems off-topic may be an answer to what they thought they heard.

Developmental language disorder (DLD)

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL · ABOUT 1 IN 14 CHILDREN

DLD affects learning, understanding and using language, and its effects can continue into adulthood. Adults may use few complex sentences, have trouble finding words or understanding figurative language, and produce disorganized stories and writing (NIDCD). It is common and often missed. In one autism cohort, 46 percent of adults who could self-report screened positive for DLD (action-focused review, 2025).

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"The ball's in your court."Confusing, or literalIdioms have to be learned one by one. They can't be decoded from the words.
A long sentence with two "unless" clausesLost after the first clauseComplex grammar takes longer to unpack than the conversation allows.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Short sentences, one idea each.
  • Concrete words over abstract ones.
  • Check understanding kindly: "Tell me what you're taking from this so I know I said it well."

When they're the one talking

  • A vague or roundabout answer may be a word-finding problem, not evasion.
  • Give them time to find the word before offering one.

Tourette syndrome and tic disorders

NEURODEVELOPMENTAL

Tourette syndrome involves sudden, repetitive movements or vocal sounds that are involuntary or semi-voluntary. Tics can be briefly suppressed and are often suggestible, so talking about them can bring them on, which leads observers to wrongly assume they're deliberate. Shouting obscenities happens only in a minority of people with Tourette, and more than half also have OCD and/or ADHD (Tourette Association of America). In communication, the static usually sits on the listener's side. People mistake a tic for a reaction to what was just said.

What happenedMay be misread asWhat's really going on
An eye roll or grunt during a serious talkContemptA tic, which stress makes more frequent
"Can't you just stop?"Being told they're choosing it, which adds stress and often more ticsSuppression is possible briefly, at a cost

Speaking to this wiring

  • Respond to the words, not the tic.
  • Don't comment on tics mid-conversation unless they've asked you to.
  • If a tic lands at an awkward moment, let it pass without meaning.

When they're the one talking

  • A sound or movement isn't a comment on you.
  • If they tell you a word or gesture was a tic, believe them.

Alexithymia

A TRAIT · FOUND IN ANY WIRING, ESPECIALLY COMMON WITH AUTISM

Alexithymia means difficulty identifying and describing your own feelings, with a tendency to focus on external facts over internal states. Body arousal and reported emotion can come apart. About half of autistic people meet criteria for clinically significant alexithymia, roughly ten times the general population rate (Hogeveen & Grafman, 2021). Research suggests that some of the emotion-reading difficulties blamed on autism are better explained by co-occurring alexithymia (Bird & Cook, 2013). It can make someone seem unfeeling when they feel plenty. They just can't find the label.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"How does that make you feel?"An exam question with no answerThe feeling is there, but there's no label for it.
"You know what you did."Nothing actionableEmotional subtext without facts gives them nothing to work with.
"I just need you to be more supportive."A request with no instructions"Supportive" is a feeling-word. What does it look like on Tuesday?

Speaking to this wiring

  • Ask about the body or actions: "Is your chest tight? Do you want space or company?"
  • Offer options instead of open questions: "More like tired, or more like annoyed?"
  • Translate feelings into actions: "Supportive, for me, means asking how the appointment went."

When they're the one talking

  • "I'm fine" may be the honest answer they have access to right now.
  • "I'm tired" or "I have a headache" may be how distress shows up for them.
  • Actions are often their clearest emotional language.

Highly sensitive (sensory processing sensitivity)

A TRAIT · ABOUT 20 PERCENT OF PEOPLE

Sensory processing sensitivity is a temperament trait found in roughly 20 percent of people. In an fMRI study, higher sensitivity scores were linked to stronger activity in brain regions for awareness, empathy and integrating sensory information when viewing partners' and strangers' facial expressions (Acevedo et al., 2014). Highly sensitive people often pick up tone and mood that others miss. The problem isn't that too little gets through. Too much does.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
A slightly clipped "Sure."Irritation, noticed immediately and felt for hoursSubtle shifts register strongly and get processed deeply.
A hard talk in a loud restaurantOverwhelmingNoise and emotion compete for the same capacity.
"You're too sensitive.""Your whole way of being is a problem."It dismisses the trait instead of the moment.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Soft start, quiet setting, one topic.
  • Name your tone if it's off for other reasons: "I'm short because of work, not you."
  • Allow time to process before expecting a decision.

When they're the one talking

  • "Something felt off" is often accurate data about mood, even if they misread the cause.
  • Needing to leave a loud place isn't rejecting the company.

Anxiety

A CONDITION THAT CHANGES HOW WORDS LAND

Anxious minds resolve ambiguity toward threat. In an experiment with ambiguous social sentences like "People laugh after something you said," socially anxious participants endorsed the threatening meaning 59 percent of the time versus 30 percent for non-anxious participants, and were slower to reject threatening readings (Beard & Amir, 2009). The more a message leaves unsaid, the more room the worst reading has.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"We need to talk."Breakup, firing or disaster, for however many hours until the talkThe topic is missing, so the mind supplies the worst one.
"Call me when you can."Something is wrongNo reason given means threat until proven otherwise.
"k."They're angry with meShort and punctuated reads as cold, and anxiety amplifies it.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Lead with the reassurance, then the topic: "Nothing's wrong. Can we talk about the weekend plan tonight?"
  • Give the reason for a call or meeting up front.
  • Close loops: "Got it, thanks!" beats silence.

When they're the one talking

  • "Are you mad at me?" is a real question. Answer it plainly.
  • Over-explaining is often a way to prevent being misunderstood.

Trauma (PTSD and complex trauma)

A CONDITION THAT CHANGES HOW WORDS LAND

After trauma, the nervous system can stay on watch. Hypervigilance involves reading neutral or ambiguous stimuli as threatening, scanning for danger, and attending to "the slightest noise or sound." In one study of 145 people, PTSD symptoms independently predicted hypervigilance (Kimble et al., 2013). For communication, that means volume, sudden movement and a hard edge in someone's voice can register before the words do. The content only gets processed once the body has decided it's safe.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
A raised voice, even excited or jokingDangerVolume is processed as a threat signal before its meaning.
"Come here. Now."TroubleUrgency without a reason echoes past threat.
"Calm down.""Your reaction is the problem."It ignores the alarm instead of addressing it, and alarms get louder when ignored.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Keep volume steady and give the purpose first: "Can you come look at this? It's a good surprise."
  • Avoid approaching from behind or starting serious talks late at night.
  • If they're activated, co-regulate first. Try slow breathing together, or the Calm-Down Kit, before talking.

When they're the one talking

  • A startle or defensive reply may be the alarm, not their opinion of you.
  • Needing to leave a heated conversation is often self-protection. Agree on a time to come back.

OCD

A CONDITION THAT CHANGES HOW WORDS LAND

OCD makes uncertainty sticky. Reassurance seeking, asking the same question repeatedly to quiet a doubt, is common, and caregivers in one study described giving reassurance because they didn't know what else to do, even though they knew it kept the cycle going. The guidance offered to families: "Once is a question, anything more is reassurance" (Cardiff and Vale University Health Board). The communication skill here is agreeing, in a calm moment, on how you'll both respond to repeat questions.

What was saidMay be heard asWhy
"I think I locked it.""The door might be open."Hedged answers leave the doubt open.
"Stop asking me that."Rejection, with the doubt still thereIt denies the answer without addressing the loop.
"Whatever, it's probably fine."Proof it might not be fine"Probably" is the exact word OCD attaches to.

Speaking to this wiring

  • Answer the first time clearly and without hedging.
  • For repeat questions, use the script you agreed on together: "I answered that one. I'm here with you while the doubt passes."
  • Speak to the person, not the doubt, and keep your tone warm.

When they're the one talking

  • A repeated question isn't distrust of you. It's the doubt talking.
  • Precision about "exactly" is often a need for certainty, not pickiness.

Mixed pairs: where the static usually comes from

Most homes, friendships and workplaces mix several wirings. These are the pairings where crossed signals come up most, and one adjustment that tends to help each side.

Neurotypical and autistic

The static: One person speaks in hints and reads tone. The other speaks literally and reads words. Each thinks the other is being unclear.

What helps: The neurotypical partner says the request outright. The autistic partner says, once, "I take things literally, so if it's a request, just ask me." Both agree that the words, not the tone, are the official message.

Neurotypical and ADHD

The static: A forgotten task gets read as not caring, and the criticism that follows lands as rejection. It becomes a loop.

What helps: Move tasks out of memory and into a shared system, like WP-03's single named owner and a written list. Criticize the system, not the person.

Autistic and ADHD partners

The static: One needs routine and predictability. The other thrives on spontaneity and changes plans. One needs quiet to recover, the other needs stimulation.

What helps: Build predictability with room for spontaneity inside it, like a fixed Saturday block that's "free choice." In a 2025 study of neurodivergent couples, all four couples preferred blunt, explicit communication, and they read extended processing time and requests for solitude as needs, not rejection (Schmidt, 2025).

Anxiety and a slow texter

The static: Silence gets filled with worst-case readings, and anxious follow-up texts feel like pressure.

What helps: A standing agreement: "No reply means busy, never mad. If I'm upset, I'll say so in words."

A loud, expressive talker and a trauma-wired or highly sensitive listener

The static: Enthusiasm and volume read as anger. Pulling back reads as coldness.

What helps: Keep the volume steady for serious topics. The sensitive partner names the signal: "Your voice got loud. I know you're not angry, but my body doesn't."

A long-message writer and a dyslexic reader

The static: Careful paragraphs meant as respect arrive as a wall.

What helps: Put the headline in the first line, keep the rest to three short lines, and offer a call for the full version.

OCD and a loved one

The static: Repeated questions wear the other person out, and their frustration increases the doubt.

What helps: Agree in a calm moment on a kind, consistent response to repeat questions, ideally with a clinician's input.

Two people with the same wiring

The static: Often less of it. Research suggests people with similar wiring tend to understand each other more easily (National Autistic Society). But two ADHD partners can both forget the same bill, and two anxious partners can escalate each other's worry.

What helps: Shared strengths still need an outside system for shared blind spots. That's what the workpapers are for.

The relationship changes the landing

Wiring says how a brain takes words in. A relationship is the arrangement under the words: what you share, what you did not choose, and what it would cost to be direct. The same sentence can be a loose plan between friends and a broken agreement between partners. That is not a trick of tone. It is the dynamic.

People often speak indirectly when they are not sure the other person agrees. An indirect sentence leaves room to retreat, which protects the bond and lowers the social cost of a disagreement (Achimova, Franke & Butz, 2025). That cost is not equal in every arrangement. A hint between friends risks awkwardness. The same hint between co-parents can sound like a record being built. The seven dynamics below are the ones this program is built around. A fuller map of how the tools fit each one is on How it fits your relationships.

Partners

What it is: Two people sharing a life, whether married, dating, living together or not. Love, money, chores and a future are in the same conversation.

Why it exists: Two people chose an ongoing bond and then built a life on it. Most of the work that keeps that life running is invisible, and it is the work least likely to be said out loud. A plain ask can feel like a verdict on the relationship, so people hint.

How the same words land: "Whenever" can sound like the shared life is not a priority. "You forgot again" can sound like "I cannot rely on you in this life." A one-word "fine" can sound like withdrawal from the bond.

Family

What it is: Parents and adult children, siblings, in-laws and grandparents. People who share a history, and often a roof, a group chat or a holiday table.

Why it exists: Kinship, not a fresh agreement. Old roles stay in the room after everyone is grown. The relationship is hard to leave, and the history is already written, so a new sentence arrives wearing an old one.

How the same words land: A small correction can sound like the verdict this person has been hearing for years. "Clean up" can mean a family standard nobody ever wrote down.

Co-parents

What it is: Two people raising a child, in one home or two, together or separated. The work is constant, and most of it is invisible to the other house.

Why it exists: A child's needs are bigger than one adult, and the adults may no longer share a life. The stake is the child. A short message can be read as a criticism of parenting, or as something that might be saved.

How the same words land: "When you get a chance" can sound like "this will be late, and I will be blamed." "Why didn't you" can sound like the start of a case. If there is a court order, it comes first. These readings are never for building one.

Friends

What it is: A chosen relationship with no job description. It runs on company, and on give and take that is usually unspoken.

Why it exists: People want closeness without a contract. Nobody owns the planning or the reminders, so one person can feel unseen while the other has no idea a ledger exists. Naming it feels like ending the ease that made the friendship worth having.

How the same words land: Here, vague timing is often just a loose plan. The same words at home can be a broken agreement. A direct ask between friends can sound like the friendship has turned into a debt. A hint can sound like cooling off.

Roommates

What it is: People sharing a home without sharing a life. Housemates, flatmates, or a friend in the spare room.

Why it exists: Housing is shared for cost or convenience, not intimacy. The contract is the space, the bills and the chores. Goodwill is the whole relationship, and it runs out when the kitchen is a guess. People hint because a direct ask feels like starting a feud with someone they have to see tomorrow.

How the same words land: "A bit" is a different kitchen to each person, and there is no shared life to cushion it. A comment about a mess can sound like a judgment of character.

Coworkers

What it is: People who share tasks, hours and credit, not a private life. Some of them have more power than others.

Why it exists: Work needs coordination, and pay, reviews and reputation sit under the words. People hint, or go formal, because being direct can cost the working relationship, and being direct with a boss can cost the job.

How the same words land: A casual phrase can sound like a status threat or a hidden performance note. "Noted" can sound like cold compliance. Urgency without a reason sounds like a priority change nobody agreed to. This is for peers who agree to use it, never a manager's review.

Caregivers

What it is: People sharing care for an aging parent, a relative who is ill, or someone with a disability, often alongside siblings or other family.

Why it exists: One person's needs are bigger than one caregiver can hold. Love, guilt and geography decide who actually does it. The person who lives closest gets the appointments and the 2 a.m. calls. Saying the load out loud can feel like complaining about someone they love.

How the same words land: "Whenever you can" can sound like "not you." "Help more" can sound like rest is never allowed. If the person doing the care is near empty, the next step is more support, not a sharper sentence.

The Signal Translator lets you pick one of these dynamics along with both people's wiring, then shows how that arrangement may change the landing of a real sentence. Wiring and relationship are different layers. A phrase can be clear for someone's wiring and still loud in the dynamic, or the other way around.

Situation, environment, and caring for both needs

The same sentence changes when the room changes. A calm kitchen can hold "can we talk about the dishes." A parking lot, a group chat, a child in the doorway, a 1 a.m. text, or a third drink cannot. Situation is what is happening. Environment is where the bodies are. Both sit on top of wiring and relationship.

A hard moment also has two conflict needs, and they often do not match. One person may need the bond confirmed immediately, because silence feels like losing you. The other may need space before their body can think. Neither need is the childish one. Forcing the talk before the body settles makes a second spike. Taking space with no signal makes the pause feel like an ending. The care is to do the small thing first, then the pause, then the return.

A BRIDGE THAT HOLDS BOTH"I am not leaving this. I need 20 minutes, until 8:15, because I cannot think clearly yet. We are ok. This is a pause, not an ending. If you want a hug first, I can do that. I will come back, and we will finish one point." The person who fears the loss can say, "I can wait if I know you are coming back." Then the pause actually happens. No second essay during it.

A flooded body often needs at least 20 minutes before it can hear again, and the return should not slip past the next day. Name the break. Do something that actually settles you. Come back. A walk-out with no return time is a different act from a pause (Gottman Institute, on the length of the break).

Little things, matched to the room

Bond fear plus a need for space: signal, then a welcome hug or a hand if that person wants it, then the clock time, then the return. Words can carry the hug when you are apart: "I am not done with you. I will reply at 8."

Around children: one sentence to them first. "You are not in trouble. The adults will sort this and come back." Then leave the room. They borrow the alarm.

Public: do not have the real talk for an audience. "Not here. Ten minutes, in the car." A public correction lands as shame.

Depleted: water, a snack, sitting down, or a lower light before another sentence. A low body hears care as an attack.

Substances: stop. Name that you care and name the morning. Nothing decided now counts.

At work, or between coworkers: the settle is logistical. "Task offline at 3, just the two of us." No hug. No character read.

Shut down: no questions and no list. Quiet, and a later time. A mobilized body gets a shorter sentence, or a wait.

Autistic, ADHD, anxiety, or trauma in the mix: "later" is not a plan. Say the clock time. Write it down. Close the loop before the pause, because silence is the loudest signal for some wirings.

Care for their need

  • If they fear losing you, do not make them guess from a closed door. Say whether the bond is intact.
  • If they need to be heard, give two minutes with no fix and no defense.
  • If they need a plan, give one owner, one step, one time.
  • If they need quiet company, stay without questions.
  • Offer touch. Never take it. "Hug, hand, or words?" A no is complete.

Keep your own

  • Needing space is not rejection. Say so, and come back.
  • You do not have to resolve the whole life to prove you are safe to love.
  • A hug you do not want is not kindness. Words can do the job.
  • If you are shut down, a fair talk is not available yet. The honest sentence is the return time.
  • Your limit and their fear can both be true. The nurture is the signal plus the return.

When the situation is volatile

An active fight, a blow-up that just happened, a public room, children present, work, substances, or a shut-down body: do not send the long version, and do not demand an answer. Fear of losing someone is not the same as fear of someone. If you are afraid of the other person, this is not a communication problem to solve with a better sentence or a hug. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or thehotline.org. If you are in crisis, call or text 988.

Ten rules that work across every wiring

These rules are how a sentence arrives. They are not permission to pile topics, blame a person, or treat a miss as proof of bad intent. A hard conversation still needs the ground rules: one topic, no criticism of the person, say that you are not against them, and stop when the body leaves the talk.

  1. Say the request as a request. "Could you…" beats "It would be nice if…" for almost every receiver.
  2. Put a real time on it. "By 8" or "after dinner," never just "later" or "soon."
  3. One thing at a time. If there are three things, write them down.
  4. Lead with the headline. Put the ask or the reassurance in the first sentence.
  5. Say the feeling in words. Don't make anyone decode it from your face or punctuation.
  6. Leave the sarcasm out of anything serious. It's the signal most likely to get lost or amplified.
  7. Choose the channel on purpose. Writing gives processing time. Voice carries warmth. Face to face helps with auditory processing. Ask which works best.
  8. Allow silence. A pause is usually processing, not refusal.
  9. Check what arrived. "What did you take from that?" catches static before it becomes a fight.
  10. Describe the behavior, never the person. "The bill was late" is fixable. "You're irresponsible" isn't, for any wiring.

Make a wiring card

The most useful thing two people can do with this page is tell each other how they're built, before a hard conversation, not during one. The Wiring Card turns the lines below into a card you can fill in, print, or copy. Nothing you type is saved. Many autistic adults describe wanting exactly this kind of explicit information about plans and tasks (Sutherland et al., 2025).

How I receive things best: [in writing / face to face / a short call] I take words: [literally / with a lot of tone-reading] Give me time to answer: [a few seconds / a few minutes / until tomorrow] If I go quiet, it means: [I'm thinking / I'm overloaded / I need space] Short texts from me mean: [nothing, I'm just brief / something's wrong] When I'm upset, I need: [space first / company / to talk it through] Please avoid: [sarcasm / "calm down" / loud voices / long texts] A request lands best as: ["Could you ___ by ___?"]

A wiring card isn't a contract, and it changes with your state. When you're depleted, every line gets stricter. Check your state first with the Nervous System Ladder or WP-02.
