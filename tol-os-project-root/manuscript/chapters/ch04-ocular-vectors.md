# Chapter IV — Deontological Parity & Sensory Gating

## Deontological parity

Most chore arguments are actually fairness-philosophy arguments wearing a dish towel. There are two common ways to judge whether a division of labor is "fair":

- **Outcome-based (consequentialist):** did the right things get done, by whoever had time or energy, however it worked out this week?
- **Duty-based (deontological):** did each person do the specific things they agreed to own, regardless of how the week went?

Both are reasonable. The friction shows up when one person is judging the week by outcomes ("it all got done, who cares who did it") and the other is judging it by duties ("you said you'd handle trash, and you didn't, again"). Neither framework is wrong — but a household needs to agree on which one it's using, the same way a contract needs governing law.

**Deontological parity** is this book's term for a simple agreement: once a task has a named owner in the RACI table (Workpaper 03), it's evaluated on whether the owner did their part — not on whether the outcome happened to work out anyway because someone else quietly covered it. This protects the person who keeps covering gaps from having that coverage used as evidence that the gap doesn't matter.

## Sensory gating

Borrowed loosely from neuroscience, where it describes the brain's ability to filter out repetitive or low-priority stimuli so attention isn't consumed by everything at once. Used here as a practical rule rather than a clinical claim: **not every comment needs to be let in for full processing the moment it's said.**

When you're mobilized or shutting down (Chapter II's ladder), a neutral comment can arrive at full volume and demand an immediate reaction. Sensory gating, practiced deliberately, looks like:

1. Noticing the comment landed harder than its content probably warranted.
2. Naming that out loud — *"I heard that as bigger than it probably was, give me a second"* — instead of reacting from the spike.
3. Deciding, once your state has settled, whether it actually needs a response now, later, or not at all.

This isn't suppression — the goal isn't to gate everything out permanently. It's timing: letting the nervous system finish its spike before deciding how much weight a comment actually deserves. Combined with deontological parity, it gives both people a way to separate "this task wasn't done" (a fact, evaluated against an agreed duty) from "this landed as an attack" (a reaction, evaluated against your current state) — instead of collapsing both into the same fight.
