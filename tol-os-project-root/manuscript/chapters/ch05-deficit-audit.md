# Chapter V — The Deficit Audit

*Like the tools in earlier chapters, the audit described here evaluates a shared arrangement over time — not either person's character, and not a settlement of past hours owed.*

## Why a monthly pass, on top of the weekly ones

By this point in the book, two rhythms are already running: the daily 90-second loop (Workpaper 13) and the weekly closing, where the Lemonade Stand gets tallied and the RACI treaty gets a quick glance. Both are useful precisely because they're small and frequent — but frequent, small checks have a blind spot. A task that goes unclaimed for one week looks like a fluke. The same task going unclaimed for four weeks in a row is a pattern, and patterns are invisible at the weekly scale; they only show up once you step back and look at a month at a time.

The Deficit Audit is that step back. Once a month, instead of asking "how did this week go," the question becomes: **which gaps kept reopening, no matter how many times we closed them?**

## What a deficit actually looks like at this scale

A single missed task is an event. A deficit is a task that keeps generating the same event, month after month, without ever landing on a stable owner. It's the difference between "the trash didn't go out Tuesday" and "the trash has quietly become no one's job for three months running." The first is noise. The second is a structural gap in the RACI treaty that weekly check-ins keep patching over instead of fixing.

This matters because unresolved structural gaps are exactly where the Anchor Tax described elsewhere in this framework compounds fastest — not from any single missed task, but from the same task being renegotiated from scratch every time it resurfaces, because it never got a name attached to it that stuck.

## What the audit is for, and what it isn't

The Deficit Audit, detailed as a working template in Workpaper 04, exists to do three things: surface which tasks are genuinely recurring gaps rather than one-off misses, decide whether each gap needs a new RACI owner or a capacity conversation, and then close the month cleanly so next month starts from a fixed structure instead of the same unresolved argument.

It is explicitly not a monthly performance review of either person, and it does not retroactively tally what was "owed" in hours already passed. Those hours are gone; billing for them backward doesn't fix anything and just turns an audit into a grievance. The only question the Deficit Audit asks about the past month is where the *structure* failed to hold — so that structure, not effort or affection, is what gets adjusted going into the next one.
