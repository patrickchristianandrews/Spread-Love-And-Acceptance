Foster, adoptive and kinship families: caring for a child who has been through a lot

📌 In short

  • A child who has moved a lot may push you away to see if you’ll stay. Stay calm, and stay.
  • Two carers, one approach: agree a few rules in private, and say why each one matters.
  • Meetings, logs and the feelings work are real jobs. Write them down and share them.
  • Raising a grandchild or a relative’s child? You set the rules now, and you deserve support too.

This page is for foster carers, adoptive parents, and grandparents, aunts, uncles and other relatives who are raising a child. Nobody here is to blame: not you, not the child, and not the parent who can’t be there right now. You are doing a big job, often with little thanks.

When a child pushes you away

A child who has moved homes before has often learned that adults leave. So they may test you: break rules, say hurtful things, or act as if they don’t care. Pushing you away is often a test of whether you’ll stay. It is not a sign that you are failing.

  • Stay calm and stay steady. The same rules, the same routines, the same warm voice, day after day.
  • Separate the child from the behaviour. You can say no to the behaviour and still say yes to them.
  • Expect setbacks after big days. A review meeting, a birthday, a visit or a school change can stir up old feelings.
  • Trust takes months, sometimes years. Small, kept promises build it faster than big speeches.

Words you could use

“You can be angry with me. I’m still here, and I’m not going anywhere because of this.”

“That’s not okay in our house. And you’re still welcome at the dinner table tonight.”

Two carers, one approach

It is very common for one carer to be firmer and the other warmer. Both are care: one wants safety and predictability, the other wants the child to feel heard. The trouble starts when the child hears two different rulebooks. Try this on a calm evening, without the child:

  1. Each of you writes your top three non-negotiable rules. Only three.
  2. Compare. Keep the ones you share. Talk about the rest, and agree which ones the child will hear.
  3. Agree who holds each rule. One of you leads on bedtime, the other on homework. The other backs them up.
  4. Disagree in private. In front of the child, you are on the same side.
  5. Look again in two weeks. What is working? What needs to change?

Words you could use

“I think you’re being too soft” → “I worry she’ll feel unsafe without clear limits. Can we pick three we both hold?”

“I’ll back you up on the phone rule. Can you take the lead on it?”

For a calm way to have that talk, see Check-ins. For the bigger picture, the Library has a short piece on parenting styles.

House rules, with the why written in

Children who have lived with many sets of rules need to know why a rule exists. A rule with a reason feels like care. A rule without one can feel like control. A few examples:

  • Phones downstairs at 10 on school nights, because sleep makes everything easier.
  • Homework before games, because then the evening is yours.
  • Tell us where you are going and when you’ll be back, because we worry, not because we don’t trust you.
  • Everyone does one job after dinner, because this is your home too, not a hotel.
  • No hitting, no breaking things, because everyone here gets to feel safe, you included.

A “House rules for us” list. Write the rules down together: phone time, homework, chores and bedtime, with a line for why each one matters to each of you. Let the young person suggest changes (“11 on weekends, 10 on school nights?”). Sharing the meetings, paperwork and feelings work

Caring for a child in care comes with a second job most people never see: social worker visits, reviews and meetings, the daily log, school meetings, contact arrangements, and being there after a hard day. If you’re fostering, the meetings, logs and emotional work count as real jobs too.

Often one carer ends up with most of it, without anyone deciding that. Write every job down, then give each one an owner. A weekly meeting is heavier than replying to a school email, so look at the weight, not just the number of jobs.

Words you could use

“Could you take the evening calls and one meeting a month? I’ll keep the daily log.”

Contact visits and the evening after

Seeing a parent or family member can bring up love, hope, anger and disappointment all at once. The evening after is often the hardest part.

  • Keep that evening light. A favourite meal, a film, no big talks and no new rules.
  • Don’t ask for a full report. Let them tell you if and when they want to.
  • Never criticise their family. Even when you are upset about what happened. They may hear it as criticism of them.
  • If a visit is cancelled, say it plainly and kindly, and don’t promise the next one will happen.

Words you could use

“Visits can bring up a lot. You don’t have to talk about it. I’m around if you want to.”

Raising a grandchild or a relative’s child

If you are raising your grandchild, niece, nephew or a younger brother or sister full-time, you are the parent figure now. You set the house rules. Advice written for babysitting grandparents (“their house, their rules”) does not fit you.

It is a lot to hold. You may be a grandparent and a parent at once. You may be grieving the retirement you planned, and worrying about your own child at the same time. You can love your child and still be angry with them. All of that can be true together.

Talking about a parent who can’t be there

If the child’s parent is ill, in prison, or in treatment for drinking or drugs, children often wonder if it was their fault. Tell them the truth in words that fit their age. Say again and again that it was not because of them.

  • It’s not their fault, and they can’t fix it.
  • It’s okay to miss their parent and be angry with them, sometimes in the same hour.
  • Speak kindly about the parent, even when you are hurt. “Mum is ill and getting help” is honest and kind.
  • Only promise what you can keep. “I don’t know when Mum will be home. I do know I’ll be here.”

Words you could use

“Mum’s getting help for an illness that makes it hard to stop drinking. It’s not because of anything you did.”

“You can miss her and be cross with her. Both are allowed here.”

“I’m your nan, and right now I’m also the one who sets the rules. I’ll always tell you why.”

If a teen says something like “You’re not my mum,” try not to take the bait. It is usually grief talking. “No, I’m not. I’m the one who’s here, and I love you” is enough.

Money and practical help

Many relatives start caring in a hurry, with no plan and no money set aside. Ask early about financial help, school support and legal arrangements. The rules differ by country and area, so ask a kinship advice service or your local children’s services. You don’t have to work it all out alone.

Looking after the carer

You can’t pour from an empty cup. Being steady for a child who tests you is tiring, and so is being the one who does the feelings work.

  • Take turns being “on”. One evening off each a week, even just an hour.
  • Tell each other what you did today, including the parts nobody saw.
  • Find people who get it: a carers’ group, a support line, or another family like yours.
  • Check your own tank before a hard day with Today’s Weather.

Where to get support

  • Your social worker or agency is the first place to ask about training, respite, support groups and money.
  • US: ask your local child welfare agency about kinship navigator programs, or dial 211 for local help. AARP has free guides for grandfamilies. If a parent is struggling with drink or drugs, Al-Anon and Nar-Anon support families, Alateen is for teens, and the SAMHSA National Helpline is free and open any time: 1-800-662-4357.
  • UK: Kinship (kinship.org.uk) for relatives raising children; The Fostering Network (thefosteringnetwork.org.uk) for foster carers; Adoption UK (adoptionuk.org); Adfam (adfam.org.uk) for families affected by drugs or alcohol. Young people can call Childline on 0800 1111.
  • Anywhere: findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country.
