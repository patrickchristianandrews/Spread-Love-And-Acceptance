Alone is not the same as lonely

“Alone” describes your situation. “Lonely” describes a feeling: that your connections with people are fewer or thinner than you need. The two often get mixed up, by other people and by ourselves. Someone living alone can feel well connected. Someone in a busy family home can feel very lonely.

What research suggests

Loneliness researchers Cacioppo and Patrick (2008) describe loneliness as a biological signal, a bit like hunger or thirst. It tells us we need connection. They also describe how, when it lasts, it can make people more wary of others, which can keep them stuck. That’s worth knowing: when you feel lonely, people may seem less friendly than they are.

Solitude is different. Nguyen, Ryan and Deci (2018) asked people to spend short spells alone. Time alone tended to calm strong feelings, both excited and upset ones, leaving people more relaxed. When people chose to be alone, rather than being told to, it went with feeling more relaxed and less stressed. So time on your own isn’t a lack of something. Chosen well, it can be restful.

In everyday life

Treat solitude and loneliness as two different needs. Protect the alone time you enjoy. Notice when the lonely signal appears, and answer it with something small: a message, a call, an errand where you’ll chat to someone. It may help to know your own patterns. For many people it’s Sunday afternoons, evenings after work, or after a poor night’s sleep.

For example (made up): Femi, 34, is a nurse who works long shifts and lives alone. After a busy week she loves a silent Saturday morning, phone off, coffee, a book. By Saturday evening she sometimes feels low and cut off. She used to scroll until midnight, which made it worse. Now she keeps one low-effort Saturday plan: a call with her cousin in Lagos, or the late swim at her local pool where she knows a few faces. Her Saturday mornings are still hers.

Words you could use

To yourself: “This is a lonely moment, not a lonely life. What small thing would help right now?”

To a friend: “I love my own space, and I also miss you. Could we make a standing date?”

To a relative who worries: “I’m genuinely happy living on my own. What helps is when you call, not when you worry.”

Try this: For a week, note two things each day: one moment you enjoyed being alone, and any moment you felt lonely. My weather today can keep a one-minute reading on this device, and its almanac shows patterns over time.

A home that runs itself (with nobody to remind you)

A home takes two kinds of work: the doing, and the noticing, planning and remembering around it. In shared homes, the second kind is often uneven and unseen. That is the “mental load” (see Invisible labor and the mental load). When you live alone, all of it is yours, with no one to notice when you forget, and no one to take over when you’re ill.

What research suggests

Unfinished tasks tend to nag at us. Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) found that unfinished goals kept popping into people’s minds and got in the way of other tasks. But when people wrote a specific plan for the goal (what, when, where), the nagging stopped, even before the task was done. For someone running a home alone, this is useful. You don’t have to do every job today. Writing down when you’ll do it is enough to get it out of your head.

In everyday life

Make one master list of the jobs that keep your life running. For each: how often, when it’s next due, how you’ll be reminded, and who your backup is if you can’t do it.

  • Yearly: insurance renewals, tax returns, the heating service, car checks and registration, alarm tests, health and dental checks, renewing a passport or license.
  • Monthly: bills and statements, subscriptions, meter readings, clearing out the fridge, paying a friend back.
  • Weekly: food, trash and recycling, laundry, plants, a tidy.
  • The forgettable ones: birthdays, backing up your phone, updating passwords, the “later” pile of post.

Then reduce the remembering. Set bills to come out on the same date. Put yearly jobs in your calendar with an early reminder. Keep one tray for post. Keep a “house book” or phone note with the boiler model, meter locations, the plumber’s number and where the stopcock is.

For example (made up): George, 78, lost his wife, June, last year. June had always done the bills and the birthdays. For months, letters piled up and he felt ashamed to ask. His granddaughter Amira sat with him one Sunday and they wrote every job down together: the gas bill, the car tax, June’s sister’s birthday. They put the dates in a big paper calendar on the kitchen wall. Now Amira calls on the first Sunday of each month, and they go through the month’s jobs together. George says it feels like company, not admin.

Words you could use

“I’ve never done the tax return. Would you sit with me while I do it the first time?”

“Want to do a boring-jobs afternoon? We both bring our admin, and we get cake after.”

To a neighbor: “If I’m ever away or ill, would you mind if I gave you my number? I’ll do the same for you.”

Routines and rhythms for one

Shared homes come with built-in timing: someone gets up, someone cooks, someone says goodnight. On your own, especially if you work from home, are between jobs or have retired, days can lose their shape. A few loose anchors give them shape again.

What research suggests

The basics affect how connected we feel. In a study by Ben Simon and Walker (2018), young adults kept others at more of a distance and felt lonelier after a night without sleep. Strangers who watched short videos of them saw them as lonelier and less appealing to talk to. The lab part was small, but it fits what many people notice. After a bad night, the world feels less friendly. So sleep, food and daylight aren’t separate from loneliness. They are part of it.

In everyday life

  • Food for one: cook a full recipe and freeze portions, keep a “lazy dinner” list for tired nights, and share a meal with someone once a week if you can.
  • Mornings and evenings: a short routine to start (curtains, a walk, getting dressed) and one to end (lights down, phone away, door locked).
  • Working from home: a fake commute around the block, lunch away from the desk, and one in-person contact on most days, even a chat at the store.
  • Retired, or between jobs: a few fixed points each week: a class, volunteering, a standing coffee.
  • Mess and tidying: your standards are yours. A ten-minute tidy before bed beats a weekend of catching up.

Routines are a help, not a test. No streaks, no guilt. If a week falls apart, start again from wherever you are.

Words you could use

“Thursday is my soup-and-a-film night. Want to make it a regular thing?”

“I work from home, so I’m trying to see one real face a day. Coffee Friday?”

“I made too much curry again. Want some? Bring a box.”

Making and keeping friends as an adult

For most people, school, college and early jobs did the hard part of friendship for them. They put the same people in the same place, again and again, with time to spare. Adult life removes much of that. People move, work long hours, care for others, and couple up. Making friends then takes a little more intention. It isn’t a skill you’ve lost.

What research suggests

It takes time. Hall (2019) studied adults who had recently moved to a new city and were looking for friends, and students in their first weeks in college. More hours together, especially relaxed leisure time rather than only working side by side, went with moving from acquaintance to friend. His rough estimates: about 50 hours together to become casual friends, about 90 hours to become friends, and more than 200 hours to become close friends. These are averages from surveys, not rules. But they explain why one great evening rarely makes a friendship, and why a weekly class often does.

Small contact counts. Sandstrom and Dunn (2014) found that people felt happier and more like they belonged on days they talked with more “weak ties”: classmates, neighbors, regulars. Not only close friends. In another study, people who chatted briefly and warmly with a barista felt more connected than people who kept it efficient.

We expect it to go worse than it does. Epley and Schroeder (2014) asked commuters to talk to a stranger, or to sit in silence. People predicted the silence would be nicer. In fact, those who talked had the better journey. And Boothby and colleagues (2018) found that after a first conversation, people tended to underestimate how much the other person liked them. They called it the “liking gap.” In a study of new university roommates, it lasted for months.

In everyday life

  • Choose things that repeat: a weekly class, team, choir, club, volunteering shift, faith group or games night. Repetition does the planning for you.
  • Give it three or four visits before deciding. The first time is the least friendly it will ever feel.
  • Be a regular: same café, same gym time, same dog park. Familiar faces become easy hellos.
  • Make low-stakes invitations: short, specific, easy to say no to. “Coffee after next week?” beats “We should hang out sometime.”
  • Follow up. If someone says yes once, ask again within a couple of weeks. Friendships are built from the second and third invitations.
  • Host small: soup, a film, a board game. It turns your home into somewhere people come.
  • Keep old friends warm: a voice note, a photo, a standing monthly call.

For example (made up): Ines, 29, moved cities for work. For two months she went straight home every evening and told herself she was too tired to meet people. Then she signed up for a weekly beginners’ climbing session. Week one, nobody spoke to her. Week three, she and Sam, a bi woman about her age, laughed at both falling off the same easy route. Week five, Ines asked, “A few of us could get noodles after?” Most said no. Sam and one other said yes. Six months on, the three of them have a group chat called “Falling Off Things.”

Words you could use

“I’m new here and trying to meet people. What do you do for fun round here?”

“I really enjoyed chatting last week. Fancy a coffee after the session?”

“I’m doing a small board-game night on Friday. No pressure, but you’d be very welcome.”

“That was fun. Same again in a couple of weeks?”

Being the friend who always asks first

People who live alone often become the planners. Friends with partners or children may have less time, less energy, or just more people asking for them. Over months it can feel as if you care more than they do.

What research suggests

Liu and colleagues (2023) ran a series of studies where people reached out to someone they hadn’t been in touch with for a while: a short message, a note, a small gift. Senders consistently underestimated how much the person receiving it appreciated it. The gap was bigger when the contact was more of a surprise. In other words, your “just thinking of you” message probably lands better than you think. That’s a good reason to keep sending them.

In everyday life

  • Don’t keep a ledger. Who texted first often reflects their week, not their care.
  • Make it easy for them to reply: “No need to reply properly” takes the pressure off.
  • Ask for something back, once and kindly. Make it small and specific: “Could you pick the next date?”
  • Match your effort to the friendship, without cutting anyone off. Put more energy where it is returned, and stay warm with the rest.
  • Suggest a standing plan. A first-Tuesday dinner means nobody has to initiate.

Words you could use

“I love seeing you. I’ve noticed I’m usually the one who plans. Could you pick the next one?”

“Shall we make it the first Tuesday of every month? Then neither of us has to remember to ask.”

“Saw this and thought of you. No need to reply properly.”

“I know evenings are hard with the kids. Could I come to you, and we just chat while you do bath time?”

Friendships in your 20s

For many people, their twenties are the first time friendship stops being automatic. School or college ends, and people scatter to jobs, cities, relationships, or back to their parents’ house. Group chats keep everyone in touch, sort of. Many people quietly feel they have fewer friends than everyone else. They are rarely alone in that.

After school or college

The friends you saw every day won’t all stay close, and that’s not a failure. Pick a few you most want to keep, and turn the friendship into a rhythm: a monthly call, a yearly trip, a game you play online on Sunday nights. Friendships that last past this stage usually have something regular holding them up.

Group chats

Group chats are great for keeping a group alive. They can also make you feel left out: plans you weren’t in, jokes you missed, messages left on read. Before deciding what it means, ask. Most of the time it’s chaos, not a plot. And if a group chat is the only contact, suggest something real now and then.

Moving to a new city

The first few months are the best time to build a new circle, before habits set. Say yes more than feels natural. Join one weekly thing early. Tell coworkers and neighbors you’re new. People are often happy to recommend things or bring you along.

Friends at work

Many adult friendships start at work. Go slowly: lunch, then a coffee outside work, then maybe something on the weekend. Keep in mind that a coworker may want to keep work and life apart, and that’s okay.

Friend breakups

Some friendships end with a falling out, and many just fade. Either can hurt as much as a romantic breakup, with less permission to say so. You can grieve a friendship. If you want to try to repair it, Talking about it: when a friendship hurt can help. If you don’t, you can still end it kindly.

Different paths

By the mid-twenties, friends’ lives start to look very different: one engaged, one traveling, one in a new job, one still figuring it out. You don’t need to be at the same stage to stay close. Ask about their life, and share yours.

For example (made up): Kai, 24, non-binary, finished university and moved home to a small town while their friends went to big cities. They felt left behind in the group chat. Rather than going quiet, they wrote: “I miss you lot. I’m stuck at my parents’ till spring. Can we do a monthly video call and a visit in summer?” Two friends said yes immediately. Kai also joined the town’s Thursday trivia night at the bar and, to their surprise, found a team of people their age.

Words you could use

“Saw the photos from Saturday, looked fun! Count me in next time?”

“I miss our group. Shall we fix one call a month, same day, so it doesn’t depend on anyone organizing?”

“I’ve just moved here and don’t know many people. Anything you’d recommend?”

After a friendship ends: “I’m sad about how things went. I’m grateful for the good years, and I wish you well.”

Living alone when you’re neurodivergent

Many autistic and ADHD adults find living alone a real relief. You can control the noise, the light, the routine and the food, with no one to mask for. It can also mean nobody shares the jobs that executive function makes harder: planning, starting, switching tasks, remembering. Some people do both and are AuDHD. Some have other differences: dyspraxia, dyslexia, a brain injury. The ideas below are for all of you.

What research suggests

Differences in executive function are common in ADHD: the psychologist Russell Barkley (1997) put them at the heart of how he understood it. They are also common, on average, in autistic people: Demetriou and colleagues (2018) pooled many studies and found lower scores across executive function tasks, with a lot of variation from person to person. Not everyone is affected the same way. But it explains why “just do it” rarely works, and why changing the setup works better than trying harder.

In everyday life

  • Make jobs visible: a whiteboard by the door, open shelves, clear boxes, a checklist on the back of the front door.
  • Name your alarms: “Trash out tonight,” not just a beep.
  • Shrink the first step: “Put the letter on the table” is a step. “Do the admin” is a project.
  • Body doubling: working on a task with someone else present, in person or on a video call. Many people with ADHD or autistic people find it helps them start and keep going. There are also online body-doubling sessions.
  • Make your home suit your senses: dimmable lights, blackout curtains, ear defenders, soft fabrics, a calm corner. Eating the same meals is fine if it makes life easier.
  • Plan social contact so it doesn’t depend on remembering: fixed weekly calls, a friend who checks in, groups that meet at a set time. Choose settings you enjoy, like a quiet walk or a shared hobby.
  • Plan recovery time after social events, appointments and busy days.

For example (made up): Jonah, 38, is autistic and has ADHD. He loves living alone, but the post used to pile up until it was scary. Now he and his friend Leah, who has ADHD too, do a video call every Sunday at 11. Cameras on, mics mostly off. He opens his post, she does her washing. It takes forty minutes. Afterward they chat about anything except post.

Words you could use

“Could we do a body-double call on Sunday? You do your thing, I’ll do my admin.”

“I’d love to come, but I need a quiet evening after. I might leave early, and it’s not you.”

“Please remind me. Reminders help me, and you’re not nagging.”

“I prefer messages to calls. A quick text is the easiest way to reach me.”

Try this: Make a How to talk to me card, a one-page “how I work” card, and share it with a friend. More: Bonding: autistic and sensory-friendly, and Neurodivergent relationships.

Asking for help without a partner

People in couples often have one obvious person to ask: lift to the hospital, help with a heavy box, a second opinion on a contract. Living alone, you need a small circle instead. That turns out to be a strength. A few people, each asked for a bit, is sturdier than one person asked for everything.

Why asking helps

Many people put off asking because they don’t want to be a burden. But being asked for something manageable often feels good to the person asked. It says they are trusted. And small favors going back and forth are one of the ways friendships grow. Ask clearly, make it easy to say no, and offer something back.

Your “people I can call” list

Write it on paper and in your phone. Share it with your check-in buddy.

People I can call (an example to copy)

  • In an emergency: name and number, and a second person.
  • If I’m ill for a few days: someone nearby for food, medicine, the trash.
  • Lift to or from a hospital: name.
  • Holds my spare key: name.
  • Looks after my pet or plants: name.
  • Practical help (a car, a ladder, heavy lifting): name.
  • Money, forms or legal questions: a person, or a free advice service.
  • Someone to talk to on a bad night: a friend.
  • My doctor’s surgery or clinic: number.

For example (made up): Bea, 52, needed a minor operation and a ride home. Her first thought was a taxi, and lying that someone would collect her. Instead she asked three friends in a group message: “I need a ride home on the 14th, about 4pm. Anyone free? Totally fine if not.” Two said yes within an hour. One brought soup.

Words you could use

“I’ve got a small operation next month and need a ride home. Could you do it? Say no if it’s tricky.”

“I’m making a list of people I can call in an emergency. Could I put you on it? Happy to be on yours.”

“I’m bad at asking for help, so I’m practicing. Could you help me move a dresser on Saturday?”

“Thank you for yesterday. I owe you dinner, and I mean it.”

Health and safety basics

These are the practical things most people who live alone set up once. They aren’t about expecting the worst. They mean you can relax.

  1. A check-in buddy. Agree with one person (or a few) on a daily or near-daily message: a thumbs up, a photo of your breakfast, anything. Agree what happens if one of you goes quiet: call, then message someone else, then check.
  2. A spare key with someone nearby, or a key safe, so someone can get in if needed.
  3. An emergency contact card: in your wallet, and on your phone’s emergency information (it can be shown without unlocking). Include who to call, health conditions, allergies and medicines.
  4. Working alarms: smoke and carbon monoxide. Test them twice a year.
  5. Phone by the bed, charged. A flashlight you can find in the dark.
  6. The house book: where the stopcock, fuse box and meters are, and who to call.
  7. A small stock of easy food, painkillers and basics for a few days of illness.
  8. Tell someone when you travel, have a procedure, or meet someone new from online.
  9. Older, or with a health condition? Ask your doctor or local services about a personal alarm you can wear, or a falls sensor.

Words you could use

“Could we be each other’s check-in buddy? A quick morning message. If one of us goes quiet all day, the other calls.”

“Would you hold a spare key for me? I’ll hold one of yours.”

“I’m meeting someone from an app on Friday at the Crown. I’ll text you by ten.”

Pets

For many people living alone, a pet is a big part of home: company, routine, a reason to get up and out, and a way to meet neighbors. It also brings costs, vet bills, and the question of care when you’re away or ill.

What research suggests

The evidence is more mixed than the warm stories suggest. A systematic review by Kretzler, König and Hajek (2022) looked at studies on pets, loneliness and social isolation. Only some studies found pet owners less lonely, with more positive results during the COVID-19 pandemic. So a pet can be wonderful company, but it doesn’t reliably fix loneliness on its own. The social side, such as chats on the dog walk, may matter as much as the animal.

In everyday life

  • Before you get one: think about cost, time, your lease, your energy, and who would care for them if you were away or ill.
  • Add a “pet backup” to your list, with written care notes.
  • Can’t have one? Pet-sit, walk a neighbor’s dog, foster for a shelter, or volunteer.

Words you could use

“If I ever end up in the hospital, could you look after Biscuit? I’ve written everything down.”

Weekends and holidays

When friends disappear into family time, the quiet can feel louder. That’s especially true on big holidays, when the whole world seems to be with someone. Planning a little ahead makes it easier.

  • Weekends: plan one thing each weekend, even small, and protect some time that’s yours.
  • Big holidays: decide a few weeks ahead what you want: company, solitude on purpose, or a bit of each. Then make it happen.
  • Host: an “orphans’ dinner” for friends who are also on their own often becomes the best meal of the year.
  • Join: say yes to invitations, even for part of the day.
  • Give: volunteering on the day can bring meaning and company.
  • Make it yours: traditions don’t need a family. A walk, a film you watch every year, your own menu.

Words you could use

“I’m around on the 25th. If anyone else is, I’m doing a big lunch. Bring nothing but yourselves.”

“Could I join you for part of the day? I’ll bring dessert and wash up.”

“I’m spending it on my own this year, on purpose, and I’m looking forward to it.”

Dating, or not dating: both are fine

Living alone often draws questions about dating, as if it’s a phase before “real life.” It isn’t. Some people want a partner, some don’t, and some are happy either way. Some people are asexual or aromantic, and build full lives around friendship, family and community. A life with close friends, a home you like and things you care about is a full life.

  • If you’d like to date, keep your own routines and friendships going alongside it. See Early days: dating and new relationships, including how to tell an ordinary difference from a real concern.
  • If you don’t, you don’t owe anyone an explanation.
  • If you find someone, living apart is a real option. Many couples, especially later in life, choose to keep their own homes.

Words you could use

When asked “Seeing anyone?”: “Not right now, and I’m happy. What’s your news?”

“I’m not looking for a relationship. My life’s pretty full.”

To someone you’re dating: “I love my own place. Can we talk about what living apart could look like?”

When loneliness feels heavy

Ordinary loneliness comes and goes. Sometimes it settles in: weeks of feeling cut off, low, not wanting to see anyone, or feeling that nobody would notice if you weren’t there. That is a time to get support, not to try harder on your own.

What research suggests

Loneliness that lasts matters for health. Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2015) pooled 70 studies following more than three million people over time. Loneliness, social isolation and living alone were each linked to a higher risk of dying earlier, on average. These are averages across large groups, and the studies show links, not causes. Living alone is not the risk in itself for someone who is well connected. What matters is having people. The U.S. Surgeon General’s 2023 advisory on loneliness made a similar case.

The good news is that loneliness can change. In a review of ways to reduce loneliness, Masi and colleagues (2011) found that approaches that work on how we think about other people, such as noticing the assumption that “nobody wants to see me,” tended to help more than simply giving people more chances to meet. That fits the liking gap above: when we’re lonely, we often misread how welcome we are.

In everyday life

  • Tell one person, even by message.
  • Talk to your doctor. Low mood and loneliness often feed each other. Talking therapies and other help work.
  • Look for befriending services and support groups, many of them free.
  • Notice the thoughts that keep you home: “They won’t want me there.” Test them gently with one small invitation.

Words you could use

“I’m having a hard time and I’ve been really lonely. Could we talk this week?”

To a doctor: “I live alone and I’ve been feeling very low and cut off for a while.”

Your situation

Your first place, in your twenties

Your first place alone is exciting and, often, quietly overwhelming. Nobody hands you the manual for bills, landlords, cooking for one, or what to do when the boiler stops. Start the master list early, learn a handful of easy meals, and ask older friends or family to show you one adult job each. Coming from a shared house or halls? Missing the noise is normal. See Friendships in your 20s.

On your own by choice, in midlife

You may know exactly how you like things, and still get asked “Don’t you get lonely?” Keep friendships on a rhythm, as friends’ lives fill with partners, children or caring. Think ahead about who you’d call if you were ill, and keep your list current. Planning for older age, from wills to who would speak for you if you couldn’t, is worth doing early.

After a breakup or divorce

Living alone feels very different when you didn’t choose it. Go gently, and lean on people. On my own after a breakup or divorce is written for this, including part-time parenting.

Later in life, after a loss

After a partner dies, you may be running a home alone for the first time in decades, while grieving. The jobs they did can feel the hardest: the car, the bills, the cooking, the birthdays. Write them down one at a time. Ask family, friends or a local service to show you the ones you’ve never done. Many older people also find a weekly group, a lunch club or volunteering brings back some shape to the week. See Grief and later life.

To a grown-up child: “I’m managing, and I’d love help with the car insurance. Your dad always did it.”

“I’d like to do it myself. Could you sit with me the first time?”

Living alone with a disability or long-term illness

You may need more practical backup, and you deserve it. Plan for bad days: food in the freezer, a list of who can help with what, and the safety basics above. When one of you is ill or disabled is written for couples, but its ideas about sharing by energy work with friends and family too. Caring for someone else while living alone? See Help for caregivers.

Every kind of person

Everything here applies whatever your gender, sexuality, culture or faith. Many LGBTQ+ people build a chosen family that matters as much as any other. Bring them into your plans and your list. In some cultures, living alone is less usual, and family may worry or disapprove. You can reassure them about your safety and your connections while keeping your choice.

Exercises

1. Alone time and lonely time

For a week, jot one moment you enjoyed being alone and one moment you felt lonely each day, with the time. At the end, look for a pattern you could plan around.

2. The master list

Write every repeating job you can think of, with how often and the month it’s next due. Add a reminder for the next three. Notice how it feels to have them out of your head.

3. Your weekly anchors

Choose three: a meal, a plan with people, and one thing just for you. Write them on a card on the fridge. Loose, not strict.

4. Five people

Write five people you’d like to be closer to, and one small next step for each. Do one step a week.

5. People I can call

Fill in your list from the Asking for help section. Ask one new person if they’d be on it, and offer to be on theirs.

6. The safety setup

Questions people often ask

Is it bad for my health to live alone?

Not in itself. Large studies link loneliness and isolation with poorer health on average, and living alone shows up in those studies too. But many people live alone and are well connected. What matters most is regular contact with people you like, and help when you need it.

How long does it take to make a friend as an adult?

One study estimated about 50 hours together for a casual friendship, around 90 for a friendship, and over 200 for a close friendship. Relaxed time together adds up fastest, so choose things that repeat.

Why do I feel awkward after meeting new people?

Many people do. Research on the “liking gap” found that people tend to underestimate how much the other person liked them after a conversation. They probably enjoyed it more than you think.

I’m always the one reaching out. Should I stop?

Not necessarily. People appreciate being contacted more than senders expect. Keep reaching out, ask once for a little back, suggest a standing plan, and put more energy where it’s returned.

What is body doubling?

Doing a task with someone else present, in person or on a video call, while they do their own thing. Many people with ADHD and many autistic people find it helps them start and keep going.

Will getting a pet stop me feeling lonely?

Maybe, but not reliably. Studies are mixed. A pet can bring routine, company and chats with other owners, and it works best alongside people, not instead of them.

The limits of this page

This page gives general, everyday guidance. It can’t know your situation, and it isn’t medical, legal or financial advice. Much of the research it mentions comes from a few countries, often with students or adults in cities, so treat it as a guide to patterns, not rules for your life. If you feel very low or hopeless, talk to your doctor.
