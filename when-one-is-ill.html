When one of you is ill or disabled

📌 In short

  • Share the load by energy, not hours. Plan for good days and bad days.
  • Jobs done from bed count: planning, booking, ordering, bills. Rest and pacing are part of the load too.
  • Being ill isn’t being a burden, and feeling worn out as the partner isn’t a failure of love.

This page is for both of you: the partner who lives with a long-term illness or disability, and the partner who does more of the house and some of the care. It is for illness that goes up and down, like ME/CFS, long COVID, MS, chronic pain or depression, as well as disability that stays the same. Neither of you is to blame. The illness changed the setup, so the setup needs to change too.

Share the load by energy, not hours

Much of this site counts hours: who did what, for how long. When one of you is ill, hours don’t tell the whole story. Ten minutes of cooking can cost one of you a whole afternoon. Paid hours don’t show energy, health or care either.

So set the split together, by what each of you can do. In the Lemonade Stand, tick “Compare with a split you choose” and pick the split that is fair for you two right now, like 70/30. Change it when things change.

Jobs that can be done from bed count

A lot of running a home is thinking work, and much of it can be done lying down. If you are the one who is ill, these can be yours. If you are the partner, please count them as real work, because they are.

  • Planning meals and placing the online grocery order
  • Booking appointments and keeping the family calendar
  • Paying bills, budgeting, and dealing with insurance or benefits forms
  • Remembering birthdays, ordering gifts, writing cards
  • Answering school, landlord or family messages
  • Ordering prescriptions and household supplies

Managing an illness is work too: pacing, resting, tracking symptoms, going to appointments. Rest that keeps a crash away is part of the load, not idleness.

Good-day and bad-day owners

Give each job an owner for good days, and a backup for bad days. Then a bad day doesn’t need a conversation. Everyone already knows the plan.

For example, Sam lives with ME/CFS and Alex works full time:

JobGood daysBad days
Meal plan and grocery orderSamSam, from bed, or a simple standby list
CookingAlex, or Sam for easy mealsAlex, or the freezer
Appointments and billsSamSam when they can, otherwise it waits a day
LaundryAlexAlex, or it waits

Make yours with the one owner per job list. Also agree what can simply be dropped on a bad day. Some things are allowed to wait.

If you are the one who is ill

Being ill isn’t being a burden. You’re a partner with less energy. What you do on good days counts. What you plan and remember from bed counts. Looking after your health so you don’t crash counts.

It is very common to feel guilty, or to push too hard on a good day to make up for it, and then crash. Saying what you can do, honestly, helps your partner more than overdoing it.

Words you could use

“Today is a low day. I can do the grocery order from bed, but not cooking. Can we have something easy?”

“I hate that you’re carrying more right now. Thank you. Can I take the bills and the calendar so they’re off your mind?”

If you do more of the load

Working, running the house and caring for someone you love is a lot. Feeling tired, sad for the life you planned, or even resentful sometimes is normal. Resentment is a signal, not a verdict. It doesn’t mean you love your partner less. It usually means you are carrying too much, and some of it needs to go to someone else.

  • The fair split isn’t only between the two of you. Ask family, friends or paid help to own one specific thing each.
  • Take real breaks, even an hour a week that is just yours.
  • Say it to someone safe, like a friend, a support group or a counselor.

Words you could use

“I’m running low too. That isn’t your fault. Can we ask your sister to take Thursday’s cooking?”

“I don’t think you’re a burden. I think we need more help than just us two.”

There is more on this, and on asking family for help, in Caring for someone you love.

A gentle thing to do together on a bad day

On a bad day, being close matters more than getting things done. Here are a few things that need almost no energy.

  • Tonight only: a 3-minute wind-downSilent, and it moves on by itself. It ends with: “Whatever didn’t get done tonight is allowed to wait.”
  • The Night GardenBreathe slowly to make flowers bloom. No timers, and nothing to win.
  • The 90-second daily check-inThree short sentences each, with no debating. Easy to do lying down.

Bonding tips for low-energy days

When one of you is ill, time together can shrink to appointments and care jobs. You can stay close in ways that cost almost no energy, and that keep you partners, not only patient and carer.

  • Be close without talking. Lie side by side with an audiobook, a podcast or rain sounds. Being near each other counts.
  • Agree a quick energy signal. A number from 1 to 5, or a thumbs up or down, so the other knows whether to offer chat, quiet company or space, without anyone having to explain.
  • Bring the world to the bed or the sofa. Photos from the walk, the funny thing at work, a coffee from your favourite place.
  • Talk about something other than the illness for ten minutes a day: a show, a memory, a plan for a good day.
  • Save a small treat for a good day, like a short drive or a film, and keep it gentle so it doesn’t cost the next day.

Words you could use

“I’m at a 2 today. No talking, but I’d love you near. Lie with me for a bit?”

Closeness is offered, never demanded, on good days and bad. Either of you can say “not today” to touch or company.

Where to get support

  • The doctor or nurse who knows the illness can often refer you to home help, an occupational therapist, or pacing support.
  • The charity or patient group for the condition usually has guides for partners and online groups for both of you.
  • US: your local Aging and Disability Resource Center can point you to home help and respite. The Eldercare Locator, 1-800-677-1116, can find yours.
  • UK: Carers UK helpline, 0808 808 7777. You can also ask your council for a carer’s assessment.
  • Australia: Carer Gateway, 1800 422 737.
  • If it gets too heavy, or either of you has thoughts of not wanting to be here, call or text 988 (US), any time. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country.

If either of you is afraid of the other, or care is being used to control someone, that is not a sharing problem. See Not safe at home?

Questions couples often ask

Is it fair if I can’t do half?

Fair doesn’t have to mean half. A split you agree together, based on what each of you can do right now, can be fairer than an even one. What you do from bed, and the work of managing your health, both count.

What if my partner’s good and bad days change all the time?

Give each job a good-day owner and a bad-day backup, and agree what can wait. Then nobody has to negotiate on a hard morning.

I feel guilty for resenting it. Am I a bad partner?

No. Resentment is a signal that you are carrying too much, not a verdict on your love. Look for one more helper, and take a real break.
