Guide · Staying calm together

Helping it settle

When someone reacts far more strongly than the moment seems to call for, it helps to ask where the reaction might be coming from, and then to do the part you can actually do. You cannot calm another person. You can change your own tone, pace, timing, distance, body and words, and those changes make it easier for them to settle. This page walks through both: where a big reaction can come from, and the levers that are yours.

The short version:

  • A big reaction usually has more than one source: the body (tired, hungry, overloaded), the week, the meaning heard, and sometimes an older hurt.
  • You cannot control their reaction. You can control your voice, your speed, your timing, the space between you, your breathing and your words.
  • Say less, slower and lower. Name what you see in a few words. Offer a choice. Do not argue the facts yet.
  • If it keeps rising, pause with a time to come back, and then come back.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar III, Read your state first, and Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. It is for everyday heated moments. Nothing here is a way to win an argument, and none of it works if it is used to control someone else.

Read the moment before you answer it

Before you respond to the words, spend two seconds on these questions. They are for you, not for the other person.

  1. What state are they in? Wired (fast, loud, pacing), shut down (blank, flat, silent) or steady? The ladder on Where am I right now? gives names for these.
  2. What state am I in? If your heart is racing, wait. Your reaction will be part of theirs.
  3. What kind of day has it been? Sleep, hunger, noise and stress all make a person react faster and harder.
  4. What did they hear? The meaning they took may be different from the one you meant.
  5. Does this feel older than the topic? A reaction that is far bigger than the moment sometimes carries something from before. See Old hurts and new reactions.

Where an overreaction can come from

A big reaction is almost never about one thing. Here are common contributors, most of which are in the room right now.

  • The body. In one study, people reported more anger and irritability and less pleasure when they felt hungrier (Swami and colleagues, 2022, early evidence). After a night without sleep, people felt lonelier and kept others farther away (Ben Simon and Walker, 2018, early evidence).
  • The week. Stress from outside the relationship made everyday annoyances land harder (Neff and Karney, 2009, moderate evidence). The kitchen argument is often about the week.
  • Being flooded. When the body is in a high state of alert, thinking and listening get harder. Stress chemicals weaken the part of the brain that plans and weighs things (Arnsten, 2009, strong evidence). That is why the same person who is reasonable at noon cannot hear you at eight.
  • Feeling unheard or dismissed. In a lab task, people who got dismissive responses showed more negative emotion and a faster heartbeat than people who got validating ones (Shenk and Fruzzetti, 2011, moderate evidence).
  • Shame. Shame is about the self (“I am bad”) and tends to lead to hiding, blame and anger. Guilt is about the act (“I did a bad thing”) and tends to lead to repair (Tangney and Dearing, 2002, moderate evidence). Someone who looks angry may be ashamed.
  • Expecting rejection. People who anxiously expect rejection read it into unclear moments (Downey and Feldman, 1996, strong evidence).
  • Sensory overload. Noise, light, crowds and touch use up the same energy as emotions. Overload can look like irritability or shutting down.
  • An older hurt. Sometimes a tone, a silence or a closed door brings back something from long ago. It is never the person’s fault, and it does not need to be explained to be respected.

This is not a list of excuses. It is a list of things you can look for, so that you answer what is happening and not only what was said.

What is in your control

You cannot make someone calm down. The seven things below are yours, and they are what the person in front of you picks up on first.

1. Your tone

Lower and warmer beats louder and sharper. A steady, quiet voice tells a nervous system that nothing is being thrown at it. Validating responses, such as “That makes sense,” led to less emotional reactivity than dismissive ones in a lab task (Shenk and Fruzzetti, 2011, moderate evidence).

Try

  • Drop your voice a notch instead of raising it.
  • Soften your face and unclench your jaw before you speak.
  • Start with something true you can agree with: “I can see this matters a lot.”

Words

“I want to understand this. Tell me what it was like.”

2. Your pace

Slow down your speech, leave gaps, and say one thing at a time. A fast stream of words, questions or explanations is hard to take in when someone is flooded.

Try

  • Short sentences. One idea each.
  • Leave a few seconds of quiet after you speak. Do not fill the silence.
  • Ask one question, not three.

Words

“Let’s go slowly. I’m listening.”

3. Your timing

Some moments are not the moment. When a person is flooded, they often need time before the topic can be discussed at all. Gottman’s work on couples measured arousal along with behavior and linked heated, negative conflict with relationships getting worse (Gottman and Levenson, 1992, moderate evidence). A break of about twenty minutes or more is a common rule of thumb from his clinical work, and not a tested cutoff. What matters more is saying when you will return.

Try

  • Pick a calmer time for the topic: after food, after sleep, not at the door.
  • If it is rising now, name a pause and a time.
  • Do not hold the big talk at bedtime or when either of you is running on empty.

Words

“This matters to me and I want to do it well. Can we talk at 7 after dinner?”

4. The space between you

Distance and angle matter. Standing over someone, blocking a door or leaning in can feel like pressure. Side by side tends to feel less like a face-off (this is common practice advice, not a tested finding). A calm, trusted presence can ease the body’s response to threat. In a small study, holding a spouse’s hand lowered threat-related brain activity, most in happier marriages (Coan and colleagues, 2006, early evidence), and partners can help keep each other steady (Butler and Randall, 2013, moderate evidence).

Try

  • Sit down, or step back a little.
  • Stay out of the doorway, and leave them a way out of the room.
  • Turn the TV off, lower the noise, dim the light.
  • Offer a hand or a hug only if they want one. Ask first.

Words

“Do you want me nearby, or some room?”

5. Your own body

Your state spreads. If your chest is tight and your breath is short, they will pick it up. You can slow your own body in the moment. Breathing exercises with long out-breaths improved mood and eased physiological arousal in a month-long trial (Balban and colleagues, 2023, early evidence).

Try

  • Breathe in for 4 and out for 6, quietly, a few times.
  • Drop your shoulders and put your feet flat on the floor.
  • Hold something cool or press your hands together for a moment.

Words

Silently: “Slow. I don’t have to fix this in the next ten seconds.”

6. Your words

Few words, plain words. Naming what you see can lower the intensity of a feeling, even though it does not feel like it at the time (Torre and Lieberman, 2018, moderate evidence), so reflect in a few words. Avoid the phrases that sound like orders or verdicts.

Say

  • “That sounds really hard.”
  • “You’re upset, and it makes sense.”
  • “I’m here. No rush.”
  • “What would help right now?” or an either-or: “Do you want to talk, or sit for a bit?”

Skip

  • “Calm down.” It almost never works.
  • “You’re overreacting.” or “You’re being dramatic.”
  • “Always” and “never.”
  • Explaining why they should not feel it.
  • Guessing their history or naming a condition.

One more caution: encouraging someone to “let it all out” by hitting or yelling does not tend to help. In one experiment, angry people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had upset them felt angrier afterward (Bushman, 2002, moderate evidence). Quiet, slower outlets such as a walk work better.

7. Stepping away, and coming back

A pause is not a way of leaving. It becomes one when there is no return. Walking out without a word can feel like abandonment to the other person. A short sentence turns a wall into a door: what you are doing, how long, and when you will be back. Repair after a rupture matters more than never having one. In the still-face studies, babies who were upset by an unresponsive parent recovered when the parent responded again (Tronick and colleagues, 1978, strong evidence).

Step away

“I’m too wound up to do this well, and I don’t want to say something I’ll regret. I’m going to take 20 minutes. I’ll be back at 8:15.”

While you are apart

  • Settle your body: slow breathing, a walk, cold water on your face.
  • Do not rehearse the argument. Do something calming.
  • Note what you want to say, in one sentence.

Come back

Be on time. Start with warmth, not the case. “Thank you for waiting. I’m calmer now. What would you like to say first?” If the other person needs longer, agree a new time. Coming back late teaches the pause is a way out.

A short sequence you can use

  1. Steady yourself. One long breath out. Soften your face and shoulders.
  2. Go lower and slower. Fewer words. Leave gaps.
  3. Reflect in a few words. “This has been a lot.” Do not argue the facts yet.
  4. Offer a choice and some space. “Talk now or after dinner?”
  5. If it keeps rising, pause with a time. “Let’s stop for twenty minutes. I’ll come find you at 8.”
  6. Come back and repair first. Warmth first, topic later, and only when both of you are settled.

A made-up example: the late bill

Ravi finds out the electric bill is late and his voice goes sharp. Mina feels the old pull to defend herself and notices her chest tightening. Instead of answering the words, she breathes out slowly and says quietly, “You sound really stressed.” Ravi says, “I just can’t take one more thing.” Mina says, “Okay. Let’s eat first and look at it after, together.” Nothing was solved in that minute, and the bill got handled an hour later without a fight.

With children and teens

  • Get lower. Crouch or sit so you are not towering. Use fewer words than you think you need.
  • Feeling first, lesson later. “You are really upset. I’m here.” The teaching can wait until everyone has settled.
  • Give a choice. “Shoes now or in two minutes?”
  • For teens, give room. “I’m not going anywhere. Come find me when you want to talk.”
  • Repair is the lesson. If you lose your cool, say so: “I yelled, and that wasn’t fair. I’m sorry.”

At work

  • Move the talk out of public view, and sit at an angle instead of across a desk.
  • Keep your voice lower than theirs and your sentences short.
  • “Let’s take ten minutes and come back to this at 2.”
  • Return to the facts and the request once the temperature has dropped.

If the big reaction is yours

  • Learn your first sign, such as a hot face, a held breath or a fast voice.
  • Use your pause line early, before you are at the top: “I need twenty minutes and I’ll be back at 8.”
  • On a calm day, tell people what helps: “If I go quiet, give me a little room and then check in.”
  • Afterward, repair without attacking yourself. “I reacted harder than the moment deserved. I’m sorry. Here is what I’m working on.”
  • If the same reaction keeps coming up, look at Old hurts and new reactions. It is not your fault, and it is something you can get steadier with.

What this page is not

These steps are for everyday conflicts between people who mean well. They are not a way to manage another person, to win, or to keep someone quiet. If a pause is being used to punish, avoid or end every difficult talk, see the silent treatment, or needing space? for how to tell the two apart.
