Tool

What am I feeling?

Sometimes a feeling has a clear cause, and sometimes it doesn’t. This tool asks a few gentle questions, one at a time, and helps you notice what you might not have considered yet: small things adding up, a tired body, a need that has gone without, a story your mind added. Then it shows you a map of what it heard, a picture made from your own answers, and one or two small first steps.

How it works, honestly. This runs on your device with simple written-out rules. It is not AI. Nothing you type or tap is sent anywhere, and nothing is kept unless you choose to save a session.

Every guess is only a guess. You can say “I don’t know,” skip any question, go back, or stop and see what we have at any point. It takes about 3 minutes for the quick version and up to 8 for the thorough one.

The tool

What you get at the end

  • A short summary you can confirm, change or reject, in four groups: confirmed by you, likely contributors, possible connections, and still unknown.
  • A picture made from your answers, with a plain-words list that says what every shape and color stands for. You can change a color, change what a part means, or remove it.
  • A plain ending: what is happening, what might be contributing and how sure we are, what is in your hands and what isn’t, one or two first steps with the reason each one fits, and a way to check later whether it helped.
  • Optional history on this device, so you can set two days side by side. You can delete it anytime.

Five patterns people often miss

After the broad questions, the tool may offer a guess about one or two of these. Each comes with a question to test it and a question that checks the other way. If your answers don’t support a guess, it gets set aside.

  1. Small things adding up. Noise, interruptions, clutter, waiting and rushing can pile up until the last small thing feels like the whole problem.
  2. Stress that spills over. A draining day in one part of life can show up as short patience in another.
  3. Low reserves. When sleep, food or recovery time run short, ordinary things weigh more and feelings run hotter.
  4. A need that has gone without. Rest, time alone, connection, appreciation or fairness missing for a while can look like irritation or sadness with no clear cause.
  5. The story on top of what happened. Minds fill gaps fast. Sometimes the story is a guess, and sometimes it is exactly right.

The tool also looks outside you. Workload, a noisy space, money, caring for someone, unfair treatment and a body that needs rest count as much as thoughts do. Not every hard feeling is a thinking problem.

What this tool will not do

  • It won’t diagnose anything, predict anything, or say anything about your personality, your childhood, another person, or your mental health.
  • It won’t tell you to confront someone or end a relationship. A short session isn’t enough to decide anything that big.
  • It won’t treat the picture as a measurement. The picture shows what you told it, using colors and shapes that mean what you say they mean.
  • It can’t see what you didn’t tell it. When something isn’t covered, the map says so under “Still unknown.”

Where the ideas come from

The five patterns are this site’s own plain names for ideas with research behind them. The questions themselves have not been tested as a measure, so treat the results as a starting point for noticing, never as a score. Here is what each idea leans on and how strong it is.

IdeaWhat the research saysEvidence
Small things adding upEveryday hassles predicted psychological symptoms better than major life events did. Kanner and colleagues, 1981.Moderate, a classic study with many later ones pointing the same way
Stress spilling overAfter high-workload days, air traffic controllers were more withdrawn with their spouses that evening. Repetti, 1989.Moderate, a small daily-diary study
Sleep and feelingsAfter a night without sleep, the brain’s alarm center reacted more strongly to negative pictures. Yoo and colleagues, 2007. In a two-week daily study, people reported more conflict with their partner after poor nights. Gordon and Chen, 2014.Moderate, small lab and diary studies
Hunger and feelingsHunger made people judge things more negatively, mainly when they weren’t paying attention to their own feelings. The authors say hunger alone isn’t enough to cause it. MacCormack and Lindquist, 2018.Early, a few experiments

Questions

Is this AI?

No. It uses a fixed set of questions and written-out rules, like a careful checklist. It never looks anything up and never writes anything new. Nothing leaves your device.

Is anything saved?

Only if you tap “Save this session on this device.” Then it sits in your browser, and only you can see it. You can delete one session or all of them, and clearing your browser data removes them too. If you never save, everything is gone when you close the page.

Why does the map say “likely” and “possible” instead of just telling me?

Because a few taps can’t know your life. The map shows how strongly your own answers point to each idea and what each one rests on. You decide what fits.

Can I use the picture somewhere else?

Yes. You can save it as an image or print it. The saved image includes the list of what each part means and a line saying it is a picture of what you told it, not a scientific emotional scan.
