Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice everything you carry, including the invisible, mental and emotional load. Between you and others: put it on one shared, fair page and give each job a clear owner, instead of blaming anyone.

  1. Start with yourself.

    Every relationship runs through your own nervous system first. A quick check can show that a snap at 4 p.m. was leftover stress, not about them.

    Dig deeper: knowing yourself

  2. With a partner, the unseen work is often heaviest.

    One of you may track every bill and birthday while the other never sees that work. Start with the Field Audit (WP-01, “Who did what”): a week’s record of who did what, written down by each of you.

    Dig deeper: where partners start

  3. With family, write down who owns what.

    Families often clash on tone and urgency more than on the task itself. Agree who owns what, so nobody has to guess. Never ask a child to keep track of what a parent does.

    Dig deeper: where families start

  4. Co-parents need calm handoffs and one owner for each task.

    A court order or parenting plan always comes first. Inside it, four small habits keep two homes steadier:

    1. Keep handoffs short. Use the same few lines on exchange day: times, bags, school things. Kids are never the messengers.
    2. One owner per task on a shared calendar: school forms, dentist visits, permission slips.
    3. Check a message before you send it with the Tone Filter (WP-09) or the Signal Translator.
    4. A short weekly check-in across both homes, about next week only.

    In you: check your own battery before a handoff. Between you: clear owners and short handoffs instead of blame (Pillars II and III).

    Dig deeper: the exchange-day script and more

  5. With friends, use a light touch.

    Often one friend plans and the other just shows up. The point isn't a ledger of favors. It's naming what went unseen, once, kindly.

    Dig deeper: where friends start

  6. Roommates need the same picture of the chores and the bills.

    List the week's tasks so everyone sees the same thing, then give trash, bills and cleaning a named owner. With three or more of you, keep one shared-expenses note: each bill, how it is split, who pays it and by when.

    Talk about money at a house meeting, one topic at a time, starting with the numbers rather than with who is late. The Lemonade Stand works with more than two people, and the Signal Translator helps you test the opening line first.

    Dig deeper: rent, bills and three or more people

  7. At work, the same unseen work shows up.

    Follow-ups and reminders keep a team moving but rarely get noticed. Use these tools among peers who agree to them, never as a way to rate anyone’s performance.

    Dig deeper: where teams start

  8. Caregiving can get heavy quietly.

    It might be an aging parent shared with siblings, or a husband, wife or partner who needs you after surgery or an illness. One person often ends up with most of the care. Give each part of it one owner. Running near empty is a sign to get more support, not a personal failing.

    One small thing today: check your battery, take a one-minute break with Breathe (the button in the bottom corner of every page), or ask someone for one specific help, like “Could you do Thursday’s drive?”

    Dig deeper: where caregivers start

  9. One map shows it all at once.

    A table lists every chapter, workpaper and tool against each kind of relationship. It's a guide, not a rule: you know your relationships best.

    Dig deeper: the full map
