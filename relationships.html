Every kind of relationship

How it fits your relationships

The Objective Ledger starts with you. It helps you see your own stress, rhythms and reactions clearly and without shame. Then it gives any two people who share time, space, work or care a fair way to see what each of you carries, so hard conversations can start from shared facts instead of competing impressions.

The ideas stay the same across every relationship. What changes is what's being shared: a home, a child, a project, a parent's care, or a friendship's give and take. This page shows how each part of the program connects to each kind of relationship, with an example of what it can look like in real life.

Yourself

Every relationship runs through your own nervous system first. This is the self-discovery half of the program: learning your own load, rhythms and reactions well enough to explain them, to yourself and then to others.

You snap at someone at 4 p.m. and can't work out why. A quick WP-02 check shows you've been running near empty since morning. The snap was leftover load, not about them, and now you can say so.

Start with

  • Today’s Weather: a minute on today’s conditions, with a forecast, a talk window and one next step.
  • WP-02, the Battery & Stress Meter: separate what you're already carrying from what just happened.
  • WP-11, the Calm-Down Kit: decide in advance what settles you, so it's ready when you need it.

How the rest connects

Chapter III explains why a reaction can be bigger than its cause. Frequency Calibration shows your natural pace for money, decisions and check-ins, which is useful to know before comparing it with anyone else's. Every other tool works better once you can name your own state.

Wired Differently explains how your neurotype shapes the way words reach you, and how they reach people wired differently. The Signal Translator lets you test a sentence against both people's wiring before you say it. Check-ins is the order for the conversation itself, once the room is safe enough to have it.

Self-discovery isn't self-criticism. The aim is to understand yourself as objectively and kindly as you'd want to be understood.

Partners

Two people sharing a life, whether married, dating, living together or not. This is where the invisible work of running a shared life is usually heaviest, and where it's least likely to be said out loud.

One of you tracks every birthday, bill, appointment and running-low item. The other genuinely doesn't know that work exists, and hears "you never help" as unfair, because from where they stand, it is.

Start with

  • WP-01, the Daily Balance Sheet: a week of who actually did what, written down by each of you.
  • WP-03, the Domestic RACI Treaty: one owner for every recurring task, so it stops being renegotiated.
  • WP-13, the Daily Check-In: 90 seconds a day that keeps small things small.

How the rest connects

The whole program applies. The Preface and Chapter II name the imbalance. CALC-01 gives a plain read on whether it's sustainable. PROG-01 walks you through it over six weeks, and REPORT-01 keeps the record so progress builds. When a topic is too big for the 90-second loop, Check-ins is the order, and the Signal Translator is where you test the opening sentence against both of you before you sit down. Do not turn that meeting into a pile, a blame, or a criticism of the person. One topic. Say that you are not against them. The ground rules cover the rest, including how to stop and come back.

The tools describe the arrangement, never the person. If either of you starts using the numbers to win, stop and go back to WP-11.

Family

Parents and adult children, siblings, in-laws, and grandparents: the relationships you didn't choose but often share a roof, a group chat or a holiday table with.

Your adult son moves back home. You think "helping out" means cooking twice a week; he thinks it means keeping his room tidy. Every dinner turns into the same argument about what was agreed, when nothing actually was.

Start with

  • Chapter I, the Radio Frequency Paradigm: families often clash on urgency and tone more than on substance.
  • WP-03, the Domestic RACI Treaty: write down who owns what, so nobody has to guess.
  • WP-09, the Tone Transducer: old family patterns make tone land hard; this slows it down.

How the rest connects

Chapter III helps when a reaction carries years of history. Chapter IV helps a family agree on what "fair" means before arguing about whether something was. The Carrier Wave Decoder works for any two family members after a conversation goes sideways. For the talk itself, Check-ins keeps acknowledgement ahead of the old rebuttal. Test the opening line in the Signal Translator first, especially if a holiday table or a group chat is the room. Those are poor rooms for a hard topic.

The worksheets are written for adults. With children and younger teens, use the ideas, like naming your state and owning tasks clearly, rather than the forms. Never ask a child to audit a parent.

Co-parents

Two people raising a child together, whether in one home or two, together or separated. The work is constant, the stakes are high, and most of it is invisible to the other house.

Two homes, one nine-year-old, and a standing argument about who handles school forms, dentist visits, and the permission slip that nobody saw until the morning it was due.

Start with

  • WP-03, the Domestic RACI Treaty: every recurring kid task gets exactly one owner.
  • WP-09, the Tone Transducer: turn a charged message into fact, feeling and ask before you hit send.
  • WP-04, the Deficit Audit: once a month, sort what keeps slipping into a real gap or a one-off.

How the rest connects

The Lemonade Stand and CALC-01 show whether the load is lopsided. The WP-13 check-in works well weekly instead of daily for parents in two homes. REPORT-01 keeps a calm, factual record that both of you can see. A charged message should go through the Signal Translator before it is sent, with the relationship set to co-parents. If you do meet, Check-ins is the order, and it is never a case file for later.

If there's a court order or parenting plan, it comes first. These tools are for the day-to-day, not a substitute for legal advice or mediation, and never for building a case against the other parent.

Friends

Friendships run on unspoken give and take, which makes them easy to strain without anyone noticing until it's already hurt.

One friend always plans, books and reminds; the other always shows up. After a few years the planner feels taken for granted, the other has no idea, and neither has said a word.

Start with

  • The Carrier Wave Decoder: for the conversation that went sideways and still stings.
  • WP-09, the Tone Transducer: say the unseen thing as fact, feeling and a clear ask.
  • WP-01's Neutral Refusals: kind, ready-made ways to say no without it becoming a rift.

How the rest connects

The Preface explains why the planner's work is invisible. Chapter I explains why "whenever works" means different things to different people. Most of the tracking tools are more than a friendship needs. If you do name it, Check-ins is enough structure, and the Signal Translator is worth ten minutes on the sentence so it arrives as a fact, not a verdict.

Use a light touch. The point isn't a ledger of favors; it's naming what's gone unseen, once, kindly.

Roommates

People sharing a home without sharing a life: housemates, flatmates, or a friend in the spare room. The chores are real, and the goodwill runs out fast when they aren't shared.

Four people, one kitchen, and a chore chart everyone agreed to in September and nobody has looked at since October.

Start with

  • The Lemonade Stand: list the week's tasks and hours so everyone sees the same picture.
  • WP-03, the Domestic RACI Treaty: a named owner for trash, bills, supplies and cleaning.
  • WP-13, the Daily Check-In: a quick weekly version at a house meeting works well.

How the rest connects

Chapter IV helps a house agree on how fairness will be judged: by time, by task, or by outcome. WP-04 catches the problem that comes back every month. CALC-01 shows whether the current setup is sustainable. A house meeting is a check-in. Use the order on Check-ins, one topic, and run the opening sentence through the Signal Translator so it does not land as a call-out in front of the others.

With more than two people, fill in the two-column sheets in pairs, or add columns on paper.

Coworkers and teams

You spend more waking hours with some coworkers than with family. The same invisible work shows up at work: the follow-ups, the notes, and the reminders that keep things moving.

One teammate quietly sends the follow-ups, keeps the notes and remembers the deadlines. None of it appears in anyone's review, and she's starting to resent the whole team.

Start with

  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: RACI started in project management, so it fits a team naturally.
  • Chapter I, the Radio Frequency Paradigm: urgency and tone mismatches cause most workplace friction.
  • WP-09, the Tone Transducer: check a message before it goes out on chat or email.

How the rest connects

The WP-13 check-in works like a short stand-up meeting. WP-04 is a monthly retrospective. WP-02 and WP-11 are for you, privately, on a hard day.

Use these among peers who agree to them. They aren't a way for managers to evaluate people, and nothing here should become a performance record or HR documentation about anyone.

Caregivers

Caring for an aging parent, a relative who's ill, or someone with a disability, often while sharing that care with siblings or other family members.

Three siblings share care for their mother. The one who lives closest ends up with the appointments, the medications and the 2 a.m. calls, while the others help "whenever they can."

Start with

  • WP-02, the Battery & Stress Meter: caregiver strain builds quietly; this makes it visible.
  • WP-03, the Domestic RACI Treaty: one owner for each part of the care, so "whenever" becomes a plan.
  • WP-11, the Calm-Down Kit: for the moments you're too depleted to talk well.

How the rest connects

The Preface describes caregiving exactly: unbilled work only one person can see. The WP-13 check-in helps with handoffs between the people sharing care. CALC-01 and REPORT-01 show, calmly and in numbers, when the arrangement can't hold. Sibling logistics belong in a check-in, not in the hallway after an appointment. Test the ask in the Signal Translator first. The person you care for is not the audience for that meeting.

If WP-02 keeps showing you near empty, that's a signal to get more support, not a personal failing.

When a relationship isn't safe

These tools assume two people who both want things to be fairer. They can be misused by someone who controls, threatens or frightens another person, in any kind of relationship.

If someone you live with, depend on or care for controls your money, phone, friends or movements, threatens you, or leaves you afraid, please put your safety first and talk to people trained for exactly this.

In the US: the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788, or visit thehotline.org. For a child at risk, call the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline at 1-800-422-4453. For an older or disabled adult, the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 can connect you with Adult Protective Services. If you're thinking about suicide or in emotional crisis, call or text 988. If you're in immediate danger, call 911.
