Friday, 6 p.m.

Three friends, ten years in. One says “Classic Dev” and the other two groan and grin, because it means “you were late and it was somehow charming.” A new colleague who has joined them for drinks smiles politely and has no idea what it means.

What a touchstone is

A touchstone is a small, shared piece of meaning: a word, a joke, a ritual, a story, a song, a place or an object that quickly tells you both “we are us.” Like a stone worn smooth by hands, it gets its meaning from being used. It lets two people say a lot with very little, and feel at home without explaining.

They are not only for couples. Families, friends, roommates, teams and long-distance relationships all grow them, usually without noticing.

An honest word about the evidence

There is real, modest research here. In a study of married couples, those who used more private idioms and pet names tended to report higher satisfaction (Bruess and Pearson, 1993). Studies of close relationships also point to shared humor, rituals and doing new things together as linked with closeness. These are links, usually from self-report, and they don’t say that adding a pet name will fix a hard year. Use this as a prompt to notice what is already yours, not as a guarantee.

Words: names, phrases and greetings

The word you use for each other, the way you say hello, the shorthand for a long story. Good ones are chosen and mutual.

  • Check that both people like it. A name that began as teasing can quietly become a wound. Ask once, lightly: “Do you still like it when I call you that?”
  • Retire gently. It is fine to outgrow a word. “I think we’ve grown out of that one. Want to find a new one?”
  • Say it on purpose. A phrase said with a look carries more than one said while scrolling.
  • Mind the audience. A word that is sweet at home may embarrass in front of others. Ask.

Jokes

An inside joke is a shared secret door. It also takes care. The warmest ones are about the two of you or about the world, and the easiest to keep are ones you could explain to a friend without embarrassment.

  • Tell the story of the joke. “Remember how that started?” revives the feeling more than the punchline does.
  • Watch the target. If the joke is at one person’s expense and they are laughing a little less each time, it is time to stop.
  • Don’t hide in it. A joke used to dodge a real conversation is a closed door, not an open one.
  • Make a new one. Jokes go stale. A silly name for the new kitchen gadget is a fresh start.

Rituals

Rituals are touchstones you do. Researchers who study couples (John Gottman, for example) write about small rituals of connection, like a goodbye kiss, a regular catch-up or a weekly meal. They work because they are small, regular and mean something.

  • Keep them small enough to survive a bad week. A two-minute hello beats a monthly ceremony you dread.
  • Let it bend. A ritual you can skip without a fight is a gift. One you can’t skip is a rule.
  • Share the organising. If one person always remembers, that is invisible work too. Take turns.
  • Make room for the odd ones. Some families have a goodbye wave from the window, a handshake, a secret signal. Silly counts.

Stories

How you met. The disastrous trip. The first apartment. Retelling these reminds you who you are together, and it gives newer friends a way in.

  • Tell it together. Let each person add their part. Different versions are fine: you each remember your own piece.
  • Add chapters. Last year’s story can sit next to this week’s.
  • Keep hard stories out of fights. Using a painful shared story as ammunition turns a touchstone into a weapon.

Songs, places and things

A song, a bench, a recipe, a mug. They hold a feeling in one place. A few cautions: not everyone attaches to objects, and some places and songs are tied to hard memories. Ask before you put one on the list.

How they go stale, and how to freshen them

Touchstones fade the same way any good thing does: through repetition without attention. This is the quiet side of complacency. The word is there, but nobody is.

  • Signs: nobody laughs, you say it without looking up, you can’t remember where it came from, one person stopped using it.
  • Freshen: retell where it came from; say it on purpose with eye contact; change it slightly; or make a new one beside it.
  • Let go kindly: some words have done their job. Thank them and move on.

When it goes wrong

  • Private words that shut others out. In a family, group or team, a joke only two people get can make everyone else feel outside. Explain it, or share it.
  • A name someone dislikes. If a person flinches, believe them, even if you meant it kindly.
  • Teasing as control. If a shared joke or nickname is used to put someone down, wear them out or make them doubt themselves, it is not a touchstone any more.
  • Different tastes. One person loves rituals and the other finds them stiff. Find the size that suits you both.
  • After a loss or a break. A touchstone can hurt when the relationship changes. It is fine to put it away.

Different families, cultures and wiring

What counts as a warm word differs a lot. Some families have many nicknames; others none. Some people feel close through shared silence, music or a certain kind of food. Some are more comfortable with plain talk and find pet names cloying. Wired Differently and your Wiring Card can help you say which kinds of words land well for you.

At work and with friends

Teams have touchstones too: a Friday ritual, a team in-joke, a way of saying “nice catch.” They help people feel like a group. Keep them light, include new people (explain the joke) and never use them to single someone out. See Appreciation at work.

A four-week plan

  1. Week 1, notice. Each of you privately lists three words, jokes or habits that feel like “us.” No sharing yet.
  2. Week 2, share and check. Take turns. For each: “Do we still like this? Keep, freshen or retire?”
  3. Week 3, freshen one. Choose one that has gone flat. Retell where it came from, or give it a new twist.
  4. Week 4, make one. Invent one new touchstone together. It can be silly. Say it three times this week.

Words you can use: “I love that we have a word for that.” • “Do you still like it when I…?” • “Can we make up a new one?” • “That one has run its course, I think. Thank you, little train.”

Questions people often ask

We don’t have any. Is that bad?

No. Many good relationships have few. Start with one small thing and see whether it feels natural.

Is this the same as love languages?

No. Love languages are about how you show and receive care. Touchstones are the specific shared words and habits you build together. They often overlap: a nickname can be words of affirmation, and a Sunday ritual can be quality time.

What if my partner finds it cheesy?

Then keep it light and small, or start with something you both find funny. The point is shared meaning, not a particular style.

Can I make touchstones with a new friend or a coworker?

Yes, slowly. Let them come from things you both noticed and laughed at. Don’t push a nickname on someone.

Limits and cautions

  • This guide is general education. It isn’t therapy and it doesn’t diagnose anyone.
  • The research on private idioms is modest and mostly correlational.
  • Nothing here is required, and a relationship with few touchstones can be a good one.

If any of this is being used against you. If someone uses shared words, teasing or “sorry” to control you, to wear you down, to isolate you or to make you feel afraid, better wording will not fix that.
