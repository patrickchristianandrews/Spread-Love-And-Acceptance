On my own after a breakup or divorce

Maybe the split was your choice, maybe it wasn’t, maybe it was both. Either way, you are running a life on your own now, and perhaps seeing your kids only some of the time. This page is for the first weeks, the rebuilding after, and the days when the kids are with you.

In short

  • The first weeks are about getting through: sleep, food, one person to talk to.
  • Loneliness is a signal, a bit like hunger. Answer it with one small step.
  • Get a clear picture of your money early.
  • Build a weekly routine with one regular thing that’s just yours.
  • Part-time with your kids? You are still their parent full time.

The first weeks

  • Expect all sorts of feelings: relief, grief, anger, guilt, sometimes all in one afternoon. A breakup is a real loss, even when you wanted it.
  • Keep the basics going: sleep, a proper meal, some daylight every day.
  • Tell one or two people what has happened, and what would help.
  • Hold off on big decisions you don’t have to make yet.
  • Go easy on alcohol, late-night messages to your ex, and their social media.

Words you could use

“It’s been a hard few weeks. I don’t need advice. I just don’t want to be on my own on Saturday. Are you around?”

Building a life again

  • Give the week a simple shape: the same wake-up time, a few set meals, one regular activity.
  • Have one thing that’s just yours: a class, a team, a walking group, volunteering, a hobby you dropped.
  • Make the place yours. Move the furniture, put up your own pictures, buy a plant. Small changes help a home feel like yours, not half of the old one.
  • Check in with yourself once a week. Today’s Weather takes a minute, and the self path goes further when you’re ready.

When you’re ready, think about what you want next time, not only what went wrong. A new chapter on your own has more.

Loneliness and friends

Loneliness after a split is very common, especially in the evenings and on weekends without the kids. It is a signal, a bit like hunger, telling you that you need people. It isn’t a sign that something is wrong with you.

  • Send one easy message today. It doesn’t need to be a big catch-up.
  • Some couple friends may drift, and some will stay. It’s okay to make new ones.
  • Regular beats big. A weekly thing with the same faces builds friendships faster than one big night out.

“Saw this and thought of you. Fancy a coffee next week?”

If loneliness turns into hopelessness, or you think about ending your life, call or text 988 (US), or find your local line at findahelpline.com.

Money on your own

  • Write down what comes in and goes out each month, now in your own name.
  • Change your passwords, and check shared accounts, cards, bills and subscriptions.
  • Sort out child support through the proper channel, in writing.
  • If it’s tangled, get free advice from a legal aid service, a debt advice charity or your bank.
  • If your ex controls the money or uses it to frighten you, see After a separation: when it’s more than conflict.

Part-time with your kids

Seeing your kids every other weekend, or a few nights a week, can feel like being a visitor in their lives. You are still their parent, full time. Lots of parents in this spot live alone, in a small place, on a tighter budget than before. That’s fine. Kids need you, not a big house.

Make your place theirs too

  • A space that stays theirs, even if it’s a drawer, a shelf or a box under the bed: a toothbrush, pyjamas, a charger.
  • Their things on the wall: a drawing, a photo, a calendar with the next visit marked.
  • A small job each, so they belong rather than visit: feeding the fish, choosing Friday’s dinner.

“This is your room, even when you’re at your other home.”

Weekend ideas that don’t cost much

  • Cook together: pizza from scratch, Saturday pancakes.
  • Do ordinary errands together: the shop, the library, the launderette. Kids like being useful.
  • Go outside: a park, a bike ride, a walk with snacks, a ball to kick.
  • Keep a board game, a pack of cards or a jigsaw that stays out between visits.
  • Let each child pick one thing this weekend, and take turns.

“The kids are bored at my place”

Some boredom is fine, even good. You don’t have to fill every hour or be the fun parent. Older kids and teens often want ordinary time: their phone, food, a lie-in, and you nearby. Side-by-side time, like a drive to get snacks, often brings the best talks.

“No need to talk. I’m going for a drive to get snacks. Want to come? You pick the music.”

Every-other-weekend routines

  • Start the same way each time, like Friday pizza or a Saturday morning walk. Routines make it home.
  • Keep the big rules close to the other home where you can, such as bedtime and screens.
  • Do homework early on Saturday, not on Sunday night.
  • Make room for their friends and activities, even on your weekend. It shows you want their life, not just your time.

The Sunday goodbye

Sunday evenings are hard for many kids and parents. Some kids go quiet or snappy before a handoff. It is usually about the change, not about you.

  • Keep the last hour calm and simple: a meal, a show, packing the bag together. The handoff-bag checklist helps.
  • Say when you’ll next see them, and when you’ll talk before then.
  • Plan something for yourself afterwards, like a call with a friend or a walk. The first hour in a quiet home is often the hardest.

“I’ll miss you. I’ll call you on Wednesday, and I’ll see you Friday at six.”

Staying close between visits

  • A short call or video at the same time each week. Short is fine.
  • Send a photo, a joke, or a question about the thing they care about.
  • Ask the other parent for school and sports dates, so you can turn up.
  • Never ask the kids to carry messages or report on the other home.

Talking with your ex

Keep it short, kind and about the kids. Separated co-parents has scripts, “who decides what,” and what to do if things may end up in court. Before you send a hard message, check it in the Signal Translator.
