Two faiths or cultures, one family

Maybe one of you is Muslim and the other Christian. Maybe one of you grew up Hindu, Jewish, Sikh or Buddhist, and the other with a different faith or none. Maybe it is two cultures, two languages or two sets of family traditions. Both of yours matter equally here. This page helps you decide things together, like names, rituals, holidays and how to raise the kids, and then tell both families.

Start with what matters most to each of you

Before you talk about any one decision, each of you answers three questions on your own. Then swap.

  1. What matters most to me, the thing I would find really hard to give up?
  2. What would I like, but could flex on?
  3. What doesn’t matter much to me at all?

The true must-haves are usually fewer than you feared, and they often fit together. One person’s must-have may be the other’s “doesn’t matter much.”

Words you could use

“I don’t need everything my way. Two things really matter to me. Can I tell you what they are, and then hear yours?”

Decide the big things together

These are the decisions that come up most for two-faith and two-culture families:

  • Names: a name from each tradition, a first and a middle name, or a name that works in both languages.
  • Ceremonies: a naming day, a baptism, an aqiqah, a blessing, a bris, or none. Which ones, when, and who comes.
  • Upbringing: which faith or faiths the kids learn about, places of worship, classes, and food at home.
  • Everyday life: prayer times, alcohol at home, what the kids call each grandparent.

You don’t have to settle everything now. Decide what is due soon, and agree when you’ll talk about the rest. Take one decision at a time, at a calm, agreed check-in.

“Let’s decide the name first, just the two of us. Then we’ll each tell our own parents.”

Talking about it: faith, family and feelings

Faith and culture talks touch identity, parents and your hopes for your children, all at once. A small question about a name can feel like a question about who you are. That’s why these talks heat up fast, even between two people who love each other.

  • Get curious before you decide. Ask what a tradition means to your partner, and listen to the whole answer before you say what you think.
  • Say it back. “So the christening matters because your grandmother did it for you?” Feeling understood calms a talk faster than winning a point.
  • One decision at a time, at an agreed time, not at a family dinner.
  • Never put down your partner’s faith or family, even when you’re hurt. Talk about what you need instead.
  • Pause when it gets hot, and set a time to come back. Coming back is the part that counts.

Words you could use

“I want to understand why this matters so much to you. Will you tell me? I’ll just listen first.”

“I’m getting upset, and I don’t want to say something unfair about your family. Can we stop and pick this up on Saturday morning?”

Each of you tells your own family

Once you’ve decided, each of you tells your own family, and says you decided together. That way nobody hears “your wife made you” or “he talked you into it.” You protect your partner with your family, and your partner does the same for you.

“Mum, we’ve decided together, and I know how much this means to you. I’d love you to be part of the day.”

“We’ve talked about this a lot, and we’ve made our decision together. I’d still really like to hear how you feel about it.”

What we’ll tell both families

Write one message together, then each of you sends it to your own family, in your own words:

“We’ve thought about this a lot, and we’ve decided together: [your decision]. Both of our families and both of our traditions matter to us, and we want the children to know and love both. We’d love your support, and we want you close.”

Elders advise, the couple decides

In many families, parents and elders have a real say, and their wisdom matters. Listening with respect is not the same as handing over the decision. Decide together which things you’ll ask them about, and which the two of you will decide.

“I’d really value your advice on this. After that, the two of us will make the decision together.”

Holidays and festivals on one calendar

Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, Christmas, Easter, Diwali, Hanukkah, Passover, Lunar New Year, Nowruz, Vaisakhi. Two traditions can mean a full calendar, and some dates move every year.

  1. Put every festival for the year on one shared calendar, from both traditions, with this year’s dates.
  2. For each one, decide: do we host, visit, or keep it small at home?
  3. Share the work: cooking, presents, travel, cards. Give each job one owner in the Lemonade Stand.
  4. Tell both families the plan early, in one go.
  5. Keep a few traditions that are just yours as a family.

“This year we’ll host Eid at ours and go to your parents for Christmas. Next year we’ll swap who travels. Does that feel fair to you?”

Kids can belong to both

Children can love two traditions, two languages and two sets of grandparents. What helps most is parents who speak kindly about each other’s faith and culture, and who never ask a child to choose.

  • Tell the stories of both. Cook the food of both.
  • Let grandparents share their traditions, within what you’ve agreed.
  • Answer questions honestly, even “Mum believes this, and I believe that.”
  • Older kids and teens may want to choose for themselves. That can be part of growing up.

“You belong to both our families. You get Nani’s stories and Granny’s songs, and both our festivals.”

Bonding tips across two traditions

Your differences can bring you closer, not only give you decisions to make. Couples who stay curious about each other’s traditions often find they have more to share, not less.

  • Ask about your partner’s favourite festival memory from childhood. The food, the smells, who was there. Then bring one piece of it into your home.
  • Go to each other’s celebrations as a guest who wants to learn. Ask what things mean, and join in where you feel comfortable.
  • Learn a few words or a dish from the other family. A greeting in your in-laws’ language, or your mother-in-law’s biryani, can mean more than a gift.
  • Make one new tradition that belongs to both of you, like a winter meal with a dish from each family.
  • Share quiet moments, if you both want to. Sitting beside your partner while they pray, or a moment of thanks before dinner that fits you both. Nobody should be pushed to practise a faith that isn’t theirs.

Words you could use

“What did Diwali feel like when you were little? I’d love to make one part of it ours this year.”

When it gets hard

Faith often matters more at certain moments: a birth, a death, a wedding, a child starting school. If you keep having the same argument, slow down, take one topic at a time, and remember you are on the same side. Talking about it has ways to listen and to pause. Many faith communities have leaders or counsellors who work with mixed families. If anyone pressures or threatens you, or you feel controlled, see Not safe at home?

Questions people often ask

How do we decide what faith to raise our child in?

Each of you first says what matters most and what you can flex on. Decide together, in private, starting with what is due soon. Many families raise children to know and love both traditions.

Who should tell the grandparents?

Each of you tells your own family, and says “we decided together.” It protects your partner, and it keeps the decision with the two of you.

How do we share Eid and Christmas, or other festivals, between two families?

Put every festival on one calendar, decide which you host and which you visit, and swap who travels each year. Tell both families the plan early, in one go.
