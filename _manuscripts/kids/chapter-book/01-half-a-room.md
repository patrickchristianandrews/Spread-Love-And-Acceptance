# Half a Room
ill: door

Wren Alvarez had exactly one rule about her bedroom, and she had written it on the door in glitter pen: KNOCK FIRST.

Her brother Theo never needed the sign. Theo knocked three times, waited, and said "It's Theo" every single time, as if there were a chance it might be somebody else. The dogs could not read, so they ignored the sign and came in anyway. Tidbit zoomed. Sugarfoot strolled. Between the two of them, Wren's rule was mostly a suggestion.

On the last Sunday in September, her dad knocked. Once. Then he opened the door without waiting, which was Clue Number One.

"Family meeting," he said. "Kitchen. Mom made pancakes."

Clue Number Two. Wren put down her sketchbook. In the Alvarez house, pancakes on a Sunday meant one of two things: either something wonderful had happened, or something big was about to.

"Is it a puppy?" she asked.

"We have two dogs."

"Is it a vacation?"

"Kitchen," said Dad.

The kitchen smelled like butter and maple syrup. Mom stood at the stove in her work badge, even though it was Sunday, which was Clue Number Three. Theo was already at the table with his headphones around his neck, lining up his fork, his knife and his napkin so that all three pointed the same way. Tidbit sat under his chair like a small black cannon waiting for a pancake to fall. Sugarfoot lay across the doorway, because that was where she always lay, so that everybody had to step over her and say "Excuse me, Sugarfoot."

"Okay," Mom said, sliding a plate in front of Wren. "Here's the news. Grandpa Lou is coming to live with us."

Wren's fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

"For how long?" asked Theo. He asked it plainly, the way he asked everything. Theo wanted the real answer, and he wanted it in a number.

"For a while," Dad said. "His building is getting new pipes and new floors, and then the landlord is selling it. Three flights of stairs are a lot for his knee anyway. So he'll stay here until he finds a new place."

"How long is a while?" said Theo.

"We don't know yet. A few months, maybe more."

Theo thought about this. "That is not a number," he said, and he took his headphones off his neck and put them back on, which was how Theo said *I need a minute.*

Wren loved Grandpa Lou. She really did. He told terrible jokes and bad weather forecasts. He could whistle through his fingers. When she was little, he had taught her to skip stones on the lake and to eat watermelon with salt.

So she was surprised by the heat that started in her face and spread to her ears.

"Where is he going to sleep?" she asked.

Mom and Dad looked at each other. It was a quick look, the kind that has a whole conversation inside it.

"Well," Dad said. "He can't do the stairs. And the only bedroom on the first floor is..."

"Mine," said Wren.

"Yours," Mom said gently. "It's just for a little while. And we thought, since Theo's room has plenty of space..."

"No," said Wren.

"Wren."

"I'm not sharing with Theo. Nothing against Theo." She glanced at him. He was studying the ceiling tiles. "But I'm eleven. I need a door. I have a sign."

"I know you do," Mom said. "I know it's a big change. Nobody's decided anything about the details yet. We wanted to tell you first and talk it through together."

That was a nice thing to say. It was a perfectly reasonable thing to say. But Wren's hands had gone prickly, and her heart was knocking around in her chest like a dog who wants out, and her brain had stopped producing words that were useful.

"Fine," she said.

It came out flat and fast. She pushed back her chair, and it scraped, and Theo flinched. She stepped over Sugarfoot without saying "Excuse me." She went upstairs and shut her door, and she did not knock first on the way out, because it was her own door.

She sat on her bed with her back against the wall and looked at the room that was about to stop being hers.

There was a poster of a rainy city. There was a shelf of sketchbooks. There was the glow-in-the-dark star she had stuck on the ceiling when she was six, right over the pillow, a little crooked. Someone else was going to sleep under that star.

A whine at the door. Then a bump. Tidbit pushed through the gap with her nose and jumped on the bed, circled three times and flopped across her feet.

"Don't," Wren told her. "I'm mad."

Tidbit licked her ankle. A minute later, Sugarfoot arrived at her own speed, put her chin on the edge of the mattress and gazed at Wren with her big brown eyes and her long drooping ears.

"I'm not mad at you," Wren said. "I'm not even mad at Grandpa."

She wasn't sure who she was mad at. That was part of the problem. Her mad had no address.

There was a knock. Three knocks, a pause.

"It's Theo."

Wren let out a breath. "Come in."

He opened the door and stayed in the doorway, one foot on the hall carpet and one on her floor. He had his headphones around his neck again. In his hand was a piece of paper.

"I made you something," he said. "You looked like Grumble."

"Like who?"

"Grumble. From the Static." He held out the paper. It was a drawing, done in careful gray pencil: a cloud with a frown and two angry eyebrows and little zigzags around it. Underneath, in his small square handwriting, he had written: GRUMBLE. WHAT HE WANTS: TO BE HEARD. WHAT HELPS: A MINUTE.

Wren looked at it for a long time. Theo's whole shelf was full of the Frequency Buddies, the cartoon he watched every day at exactly four-thirty. Tidbit and Sugarfoot were named after the two pups in it, and so were half the stuffed animals in his bed. The rest were Ollie the owl, Dot the snail, Hopper the frog, Nutmeg the squirrel, Lumi the red panda and Beep the robot, lined up on his shelf by height, with three yellow ducklings at the end because they were the shortest. In the cartoon, the clouds of Static were what floated in when feelings got too loud. There was Grumble, who was gray and grumpy, and Fuzz, who was purple and prickly, and Mumble, who was shy and peach-colored and hid in corners, and Drift, who was green and wandered off whenever things got hard.

"I don't look like that," Wren said.

Theo tilted his head. "Your eyebrows are in the same position."

She felt her mouth twitch. It was impossible to stay completely angry at someone who delivered news like a weather reporter.

"Is this what you do when you get loud inside?" she asked. "Draw it?"

"Sometimes. Or count the ceiling tiles." He considered. "Mom says it is okay to need a minute. She says I just have to tell people I'm taking it."

"That's a good rule."

"I know," Theo said. "I wrote it on my wall."

Wren laughed, a short surprised laugh, and some of the heat went out of her ears. She patted the bed. Theo came in, step by careful step, and sat at the very edge, as far from Tidbit's tail as possible.

"I don't want to share a room," Wren said.

"I know. Your face said so." He nodded. "I don't know if I want to either. I like my room the way it is. But Grandpa knows all the names of the clouds."

"The real ones?"

"Cumulus. Stratus. Nimbus." Theo smiled, and it was the smile he gave only to facts he was really fond of. "He told me on the phone. He says there are ten kinds."

Downstairs, a pan clattered. Somebody turned off the radio. Through the floor came the low sound of their parents talking, and Wren thought: *they are probably worried about me.* The knot in her chest stayed the same size. It felt a little less lonely, though.

She tacked Theo's drawing to her corkboard. Grumble frowned down at her, gray and ridiculous.

"Okay," she said to the drawing. "You can stay."

She did not say it to her parents. Not yet. She was still too revved up for that, and she knew it, a little. She just did not yet have a word for what her body was doing, or the first idea what to do about it.

That part would come later. The first thing that came, on the very next day, was a text.

::: try
Notice your body before you say anything. Are your hands prickly, your face hot, your heart knocking? That is your body telling you it is revved up.
If you can, say what you are doing: "I need a minute." Theo's rule works for anyone.
Next time you are mad and don't know at whom, try drawing the mad. What does it look like? What does it want? What helps?
