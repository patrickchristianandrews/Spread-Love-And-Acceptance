# The Stairs
ill: stairs

Wren woke up at 11:40 with her heart already running.

For a few seconds she didn't know why. The room was dark. The rain sound pattered. Across from her, Theo was a small shape under the blanket, turned to the wall. Then everything came back at once, in the order it had happened, and her stomach dropped through the mattress.

*You are SO annoying.*

She put both hands over her face.

She tried lying still. She tried counting, which didn't work. She tried the breath, in four, out six, and managed three rounds before her thoughts got loud again. After a while she gave up, slid out of bed, and padded into the hall in her socks.

There was a strip of yellow light under the stairs.

Grandpa Lou was sitting on the third step from the bottom in his bread-loaf slippers and a bathrobe that had once been blue, with a mug of something steaming, and Sugarfoot stretched out across the step below like a furry draft stopper. He looked up as she came down. He didn't seem surprised.

"Weather's fine for a walk," he said. "A little breezy, with a chance of regret."

"You heard," said Wren.

"The whole house heard." He patted the step beside him. "Sit. It's a good step. It's got a view of the front door."

She sat. Sugarfoot lifted her chin off the stair, looked at Wren with serious eyes, and put it back down, close enough that Wren could feel her warmth against her ankle. Neither of them said anything for a bit. It was the kind of quiet that doesn't ask for anything. Wren found she could breathe in it.

"I said something terrible," she said at last.

"I know."

"I said nobody gets him. I said the dogs are the only ones who want to be around him." Her voice wobbled. "That isn't true. It isn't even a little true. And I knew it was the worst thing I could say. I said it anyway."

Grandpa Lou nodded slowly. He didn't tell her it wasn't so bad. She was grateful for that.

"Did it come out of nowhere?" he asked.

"No. I was so tired. And everything's been loud. And I'm mad about my room, and I'm mad at Mateo, and I'm mad at myself, and the phone kept buzzing and he kept saying the rule and I just..." She made a small explosion with her hands. "I wasn't even mad at him. He was the one who was there."

"That happens," said Grandpa Lou. "The mad goes looking for an address, and it doesn't always find the right one."

Wren stared at him. It was nearly word for word what she'd thought on the first day.

"That's a very good explanation, Grandpa."

"I stole it from your brother. He said it at dinner." He smiled a little. "Listen, Wren. What happened up there isn't something you can make fine. You can't make it disappear. But it's also not the end of the road. Do you know what Theo's doing right now?"

"Hating me?"

"I'd be surprised. Let's guess it's something else. Something about his body. When a person has gotten too loud inside, or heard something that hurt a lot, some people get louder. And some people go quiet. Their body puts on a brake. They aren't punishing anyone. Think of a circuit breaker. If it didn't trip, the house might burn down."

"So he's not ignoring me on purpose?"

"I can't say for certain. I wasn't in his head. But I know your brother. When Theo goes quiet, he's usually not trying to hurt you. He's trying to hold on to himself."

Wren thought about this. "But what if it is on purpose? What if he doesn't want to talk to me ever again?"

"Then you would live with that, and you would still have apologized. But I don't think so." He took a sip. "Here's how you can tell the difference between a kid taking a break and a person freezing you out. A break has a way back. Somebody says what's happening ('I need some time,' or just a face that closes up) and comes back, sooner or later, when they're ready. Freezing someone out is different. It goes on and on and nobody says why or when it's going to end, and the point of it is to make you feel bad for as long as possible."

"Theo doesn't do that."

"No. He told you he was taking a minute under a table, remember? He tells people. That's the whole reason his rule works." Grandpa Lou looked into his mug. "Most of the time the person who needs a break isn't the problem. The trouble starts when nobody knows when they're coming back."

Wren plucked at her sleeve. "You said that like you know."

"I do." He was quiet for a moment. "I had a brother named Ray. We were ten and twelve, and we shared a room, believe it or not. A lot like this one. He was always whistling, and I was trying to read, and one night I said something awful. I said, 'You're a baby and no one likes you.' It was a lie, and the instant it left my mouth I wanted to catch it and put it back."

"What did Ray do?"

"Went quiet. For a week." He shook his head. "I didn't say sorry for four days. Do you know why? I was embarrassed. And I thought if I said sorry too soon, he'd laugh. And I thought if I said it too late, it wouldn't count. So I waited, and the waiting got harder, and I started telling myself he was being silly to be mad. A great trick, by the way. I recommend nobody try it."

"What happened?"

"On the fifth day I walked into the kitchen and said the three things." He held up fingers. "One: *I'm sorry.* Two: *I told you something cruel, and it wasn't true, and it was my fault.* Three: *What can I do to make it right?* And Ray looked at me for a very long time, and he said, 'You can stop calling me a baby.' And I said, 'Done.'"

"That's it?"

"That's it. It was the best-tasting sandwich I ever ate, because we ate it together." He smiled. "Ray's eighty-one now. He still whistles."

Wren laughed. It was a small, wet laugh, and then the tears came. She put her face on her knees. Grandpa Lou did not say anything. He moved a little closer, and put one big, warm hand on her back, very lightly, and left it there. Sugarfoot let out a long sigh and pressed her whole side against Wren's feet.

It felt like the first kind thing that had happened all day.

"Did I make him hate me?" she said, into her knees.

"I don't know," said Grandpa Lou honestly. "That's up to him. What I do know is this. You can't force a person to forgive you on your schedule. You don't get to say sorry and then demand that it's all better by breakfast. What you can do is make an honest apology and then give it room to land. That is what makes an apology an apology, and keeps it from turning into an order."

Wren wiped her nose on her sleeve. "What if he says no? What if he needs a long time?"

"Then you wait, and you keep being the kind of person someone could forgive. You'd be surprised how much of the repair is just that."

"But how do I even start?"

"A few things." He counted again. "First, don't do it right now. It's midnight, he's asleep, and the rule is eight-thirty. Tomorrow, ask him when would be a good time. Let him pick. Second, say what you actually did. Not 'I'm sorry if you felt bad.' That one's useless. Say 'I said you were annoying and that nobody gets you, and it wasn't true, and it hurt you.' Third, don't explain it away. I know you were tired. You can tell him that if he asks, but save it for after."

"An excuse explains," said Wren slowly. "An apology fixes."

"Where did you get that?"

"I just thought of it."

"That's good. Write it down." He raised his mug at her. "And fourth, ask what would help. Don't guess. He's got a better handle on his needs than most adults."

She nodded. A weight she hadn't known she was carrying shifted a little. She was still sorry. She still felt sick. But there was a path now, small and narrow, and it ran from where she was to where she needed to be.

They sat there for a while longer. Grandpa Lou finished his tea. Somewhere in the house, the barometer clicked softly. Out the front window, a car went by, and its headlights slid across the ceiling and were gone.

"Grandpa?"

"Mm."

"Thank you for not coming in."

"I thought about it." He chuckled quietly. "Then I thought, she's old enough to get here on her own. And look. Here you are."

She leaned her head against his shoulder. He smelled like peppermint and sawdust.

"Up to bed," he said, a few minutes later. "Tomorrow's got a forecast to it."

"What kind?"

"I'd say partly sorry, with a good chance of sandwiches."
