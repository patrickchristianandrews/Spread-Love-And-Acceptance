# Boxes
ill: boxes

Grandpa Lou arrived on Saturday morning in the back of a rented truck, sitting on a kitchen chair.

"He's not supposed to ride like that," Dad muttered, as the truck pulled into the driveway. But Grandpa Lou climbed down with one hand on the tailgate and one raised in the air, like a king arriving at a very small kingdom, and called out, "Forecast for today! Bright, with a ninety percent chance of boxes!"

Wren stood on the front steps with her arms folded and Theo beside her, headphones around his neck. She had planned to look neutral.

"Boxes are not weather," said Theo.

"Fair point, son," said Grandpa Lou. "That was a metaphor."

Theo considered this. "Okay. Please tell me when you are doing a metaphor. If I am not sure, I will ask."

"Deal," said Grandpa Lou, and shook his hand, which Theo allowed for exactly two seconds.

Then Grandpa Lou spotted her, and his whole face went up like a window shade, and she felt her arms unfold on their own.

"There she is. The weather girl."

"I'm not the weather girl, Grandpa."

"You drew the rain on my fridge calendar for a whole year. I'd call that credentials." He hugged her. He smelled like peppermint and sawdust, which was how he always smelled. "Listen. I know I'm taking your room. If I could have picked any other place on earth to put a bad knee, believe me, I would have."

That was so exactly what Wren had been thinking that she didn't know what to say. "It's okay," she said, which was only a little bit true.

"It's not," said Grandpa Lou, "but it will be."

Then the unloading began, and the house filled up with noise.

There were a lot of boxes. There was a record player in a case and a crate of records. There were three plants that had to ride in the front seat because they got carsick. There was a coffee can of mismatched screws that Grandpa insisted on carrying himself, and a toolbox, and a lamp shaped like a lighthouse, and a wooden barometer that he hung in the hall at once, tapping it with one finger and saying "Hm."

Dad and two neighbors carried the heavy things. Mom stood in the hall with a clipboard. Tidbit ran in circles around everybody's legs, thrilled by the sheer amount of new smells, and Sugarfoot sat on the porch and supervised. Somebody turned on the radio. Somebody else started the drill, because Grandpa's bed had to be put together first, and the drill went *rrrrRRRRR* through the walls of the whole house.

It was a lot. Wren felt it in her teeth.

And then she noticed that she hadn't seen Theo in a while.

---

She found him under the dining room table.

He was sitting with his knees pulled up and his headphones clamped on over his ears, with the big noise-blocking kind that he wore on the bus. His eyes were shut. His lips were moving. Tidbit, who had slipped under there to find him, was lying across his feet and panting in a worried way.

Wren crouched down and lifted the edge of the tablecloth.

"Theo."

Nothing. His lips kept moving. She leaned closer. *Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three.* He was counting the slats on the underside of the table.

Her first feeling, honestly, was annoyance. It rose before she could stop it. It was Saturday. Grandpa Lou was here. Everyone was working. And her brother was hiding under a table.

"Of course," she muttered. "Of course it has to be about him."

She did not say it loudly. But Theo had heard something. His eyes opened. He pushed one headphone off his ear.

"I'm taking a minute," he said. His voice was very small and very exact. "It's too loud. There's drilling and a radio and four people talking at once. I can't hear the one thing I'm supposed to be hearing. I told Mom. She said that was okay."

"I didn't say it wasn't okay."

"You said it was about me."

Wren opened her mouth and closed it.

It was true. She had said it. And she had been about to say a second thing, which was *You always do this,* and she was suddenly glad she had not.

"Sorry," she said. "That wasn't fair."

"Okay," said Theo. He put the headphone back.

She stayed crouched there, with the tablecloth hanging over her shoulder and her knee going numb, and she didn't know what to do. If she went away, he would be alone, but she'd guessed that was what he wanted. If she stayed, she might be too much. If she said something, it might be the wrong thing. She felt clumsy, like a person trying to carry a full bowl of soup across a room.

"Do you want me to go?" she asked.

Theo opened his eyes. "I don't know. I'm still counting."

"Okay. I'll stay, but I won't talk."

"That would be good," he said, and closed his eyes again.

So she sat down on the floor, next to the table leg, and did not talk. Tidbit moved over and put her head in Wren's lap. A minute went by. Another. The drill stopped. The radio got turned down. Somebody laughed in the hallway, and it was a normal-sized laugh, and Theo's shoulders came down from around his ears, a little at a time.

A new pair of feet appeared at the edge of the tablecloth. They were wearing slippers shaped like loaves of bread.

"Mind if I join the meeting?" said Grandpa Lou.

"The meeting is under the table," Theo said, without opening his eyes.

"So I see." Grandpa Lou considered the problem. Then, to Wren's surprise, he lowered himself carefully to the floor, with a sound like a slow tire going flat, and crawled in under the tablecloth with them, bad knee and all. He wedged himself between a chair leg and a box of records, and he did not say a single word.

The three of them sat there, breathing.

After a while, Theo pushed both headphones down around his neck.

"Is it over?" he asked.

Wren looked at Grandpa, ready for a joke. But he answered the question exactly as it was asked.

"The drilling is over," he said. "The radio is off. There are still about thirty boxes, and I'm going to be here for a few months. So part of it is over, and part of it isn't."

Theo thought about this. "Thank you," he said. "Most people say yes."

"Most people say yes to make you feel better," said Grandpa Lou. "I prefer telling you the weather as it is. It's more useful."

Something shifted in Theo's face. It was a small shift, like a curtain moving in a window. "That's what I like about clouds," he said.

"Me too."

Wren looked from one of them to the other, and a funny thing happened inside her. She had thought she would feel left out. Instead she felt a little bit like she was watching two people discover they spoke the same language, which, she realized, they probably did. It was a language where you said exactly what you meant and you told the truth about how much was left to go.

*Theo isn't wired wrong,* she thought. *He and I are wired differently, and we keep missing each other.*

She wasn't sure that was an original thought. It had a slightly Mom-ish ring. But it felt like hers.

---

They carried the boxes in all afternoon. Wren's things went upstairs in a pile of her own boxes, labeled in marker: BOOKS, SKETCHBOOKS, CLOTHES, STUFF. She was going to be sleeping on the spare bed in Theo's room, the one the cousins used when they visited, under a ceiling with no star on it.

By dinner, Grandpa Lou was settled in her old room. Wren walked past the open door. He'd hung his barometer on her wall right above the glowing star, which was still stuck to the ceiling, since nobody could reach it.

"It's a good star," said Grandpa Lou from the bed, where he was resting his knee on two pillows. "I'll keep an eye on it for you."

"Thanks."

"Wren." He waited until she looked at him. "When I get quiet, it doesn't mean anything is wrong. I just do my thinking in the dark. And when I talk too much, it usually means I'm nervous. I'm going to be nervous for a bit. New house and all."

"You're nervous?"

"Terrified," said Grandpa Lou happily. "Tell your mother I said I'm fine."

"That isn't fine, Grandpa," Wren said. "Fine isn't a rung."

"A what?"

"Never mind. I'll show you the fridge."

::: try
Some people need quiet to settle, and some people need company. Neither is a mistake. You can ask: "Do you want room, or do you want me close?"
If someone is taking a minute, you can sit nearby without talking. Your calm can keep them company.
When you answer a question, you can say how much is true. "The drilling is over, and thirty boxes are left" is a better answer than a plain yes.
:::
