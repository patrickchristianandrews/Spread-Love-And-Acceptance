# One Owner Per Job
ill: storm

On Monday, the four of them met at lunch with a roll of tape, a stack of paper, and Mateo's notebook, which he handed to Wren before anyone had said a word.

"I thought you should hold it," he said. "That way you can see if I start assigning stuff."

"You are going to be unbearable about this," said Josie.

"I'm trying to be good!"

"You're being good," Wren said, trying not to laugh. "Okay. Let's do this right. Everybody tell us what they'd like to do. Then we'll see where there's overlap."

"What's overlap?" asked Josie.

"When two people want the same thing."

It turned out there was very little. Mateo, who loved building, wanted the model. He said so, and he said it as a request: "Can I be the model person? I have the clay." Imani, who drew everything, wanted the poster. Josie wanted to write the talk, and she wanted a backup, because she got nervous when she had to give the talk alone. Wren, to her own surprise, wanted none of the above.

"I want the part about why thunderstorms happen," she said. "The science part. Like, how it works. And I want to show how it's like static."

"Like the clouds on Theo's show?" said Imani.

"Kind of. Thunderclouds build up electric charge. It's called static. And lightning is how the cloud lets it go." Wren had read that in a library book, in a beanbag, on Friday, and it had stuck in her mind like a burr. "When the charge builds, you get a storm. When it discharges, it's over. The sky clears. It doesn't last."

"That's actually kind of beautiful," said Mateo.

"Don't tell my brother. He'll want it on a card."

They wrote it all down. Each job had an owner and a backup, in two columns, just like at Wren's kitchen table. Imani wrote the column headings: *Who does it. Who steps in.* Mateo was the model, with Josie as backup. Imani was the poster, with Wren as backup. Josie was the talk, with Imani as backup, and Wren was the science, with Mateo behind her. They taped the sheet inside Mateo's notebook, and Mateo sat back with a look of enormous satisfaction.

"Look at that," he said. "Everybody has a job. Nobody has a little job."

"There are no little jobs," said Josie solemnly. "Only little clouds."

---

It was not a perfect week.

On Wednesday, Mateo's model collapsed, clay and all, in the middle of the cafeteria, and for a second Wren saw his face start to go the way it had on Friday: fast words, bouncing knee, a plan forming. She could see him reaching for his notebook, to reassign everything.

"Mateo," she said.

He looked up.

"Fine or need a minute?" she asked.

He stared at her. Then, slowly, he sat back down. "Minute," he said. "I need a minute. I'm nervous."

"Okay. Take it. We'll pick up the clay."

They picked up the clay. It took four minutes, and Mateo hummed under his breath the whole time, and when he was done, his shoulders had come down from his ears. "Okay," he said. "I'm back. The model is fine. I think I can make it a better volcano. Which is not what we're doing. A better thunderstorm."

"Go for it," said Josie.

On Thursday, Josie froze.

They were practicing the talk in the library, and when she got to the part about lightning, her voice went thin. She stopped. Her face was pink and her eyes were wide.

Wren knew that look. She had seen it in the mirror.

"Josie," she said quietly. That was all. She said it the way they'd agreed, gently, and then she waited. She did not say "You're doing great!" or "Just breathe!" or any of the other things people said that hit the ears like gravel.

Josie swallowed. "I know it," she whispered. "I know every word. It just all went away."

"That's allowed. It's your body hitting the brakes. It'll come back."

"Okay." She breathed. "Okay. Can somebody say the first line?"

"I'll do the first line," Imani said, as backup, and she did. And then Josie said the second, and the third, and the whole thing came back, line after line, like a train rolling out of a tunnel.

They had done it. It wasn't dramatic. Nobody cheered. But Wren felt it settle in her, like a dropped stitch found and picked up: that was what a team was.

---

On Friday, they stood in front of the class.

Mrs. Okafor had seated herself at the back with her clipboard and her reading glasses, looking terrifyingly interested. The model sat on the desk: a handsome clay thundercloud, painted in two shades of gray, with a little flash of yellow lightning in the middle and a tiny paper house underneath, as if daring it.

Josie gave the talk. Her hands shook, and her voice only wavered once. Imani held up the poster at the right moments, a beautiful diagram with arrows and a cross-section of a cloud. Mateo stood behind the model and kept his hands to himself, which Wren knew was costing him a lot.

Then it was Wren's turn.

"So, how does a thunderstorm work?" she said. "Imagine a cloud getting more and more full of static. It builds and builds. It's loud, and it's tense, and it feels like it'll never end."

She paused. She had not planned the next part. It came on its own.

"But then the charge finds a way to let go. That's lightning. It's very loud and very bright, and it's over in a flash. And then the air is clear. It rains. A storm is the sky doing what it has to do so it can feel better."

She saw Imani's face go soft. She saw Josie smile. She saw Mateo look at his model with a new expression, as if someone had finally said what he'd been trying to build.

"Thank you," she finished. "That's our project."

There was a short silence. Then Mrs. Okafor stood up and took off her glasses.

"Do you know what impressed me most?" she said. "The science was good. The model was good. But what I noticed was that each of you knew exactly what your job was, and each of you knew who was behind you. That's rarer than good clay." She nodded. "Well done. All four."

At their desks afterward, Mateo whispered, "Did she say we were rarer than good clay?"

"I think so."

"That's going on the poster."

"What poster?"

"Our other poster. The rules poster." He grinned. "We're making it a thing."

---

That afternoon, when Wren got home, Grandpa Lou was at the kitchen table with his reading glasses on and a pencil behind each ear, working on the fix-it list. Tidbit was asleep on his feet.

"Well?" he said.

"We got an A."

"I knew it. I could tell by the forecast."

"There wasn't a forecast."

"There's always a forecast. It's just written in the way people walk in." He put down his pencil. "Come here. Look at this."

On the whiteboard, under *Who does it* and *Who steps in,* there was a new row at the bottom, in Mom's handwriting. It said: *Noticing, remembering, planning.* In the first column, it said *Wren.* In the second, *Everybody.* And under that, in Theo's careful capitals: *WITH A CARD.*

"Your mother added it this morning," said Grandpa Lou. "Said she wanted to say thank you for the pile."

Wren stared at the board. Then she stuffed her hands in her pockets and looked at the floor, because her eyes were suddenly doing something undignified.

"Mom's going to cry again," she said.

"I think she already has," said Grandpa Lou. "It's been a week of weather."
