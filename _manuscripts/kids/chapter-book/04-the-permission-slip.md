# The Permission Slip
ill: slip

On Thursday morning, Wren's science museum permission slip was missing.

It had been in her backpack on Monday. She was almost sure. Then Grandpa Lou's boxes had happened, and Theo's schedule had happened, and the slip had gone wherever things go when you aren't looking at them.

"It's due today," Wren said, tipping her backpack upside down on the kitchen floor. Out fell three pencils, a granola bar with a bite out of it, and a sock that was not hers. "Mrs. Okafor said no slip, no museum."

"Where did you last see it?" said Dad, who was pouring cereal with the dazed look of a man who had been awake since five.

"If I knew that, it wouldn't be lost!"

Dad flinched. Wren felt a prickle of guilt, but she was also panicking, and when she panicked, her volume got ahead of her.

"I'll check the pile," said Dad, and he went to look in the pile.

In the Alvarez house, there was a pile. It lived on the end of the counter and consisted of mail, school notices, a library book that was three weeks late, and a sticky note that said *CALL ABOUT THE THING.* Mom was the person who knew what was in the pile. Mom was the one who sorted it every Sunday night with a cup of tea and a stack of colored paper clips.

But Mom, this week, was not here. She had started a new shift at work, with early mornings and two late nights, and she had left at 6:10 with a travel mug and a kiss on the top of Wren's head.

"I don't see it," Dad called.

"It has a green border! And you have to sign it!"

"Wren." Dad came back, holding the library book. "I'm looking. Take a breath."

She did not take a breath. She felt hot all over. "Mom would know where it is."

"Mom's at work."

"Mom always knows."

It came out sharper than she meant. Dad stopped with his hand on the library book. There was a short, strange silence, the kind where everyone is thinking something and nobody wants to be the first.

"You're right," Dad said slowly. "She does."

He didn't say it angrily. He said it like a man finding something under the couch he had not known was there.

At that moment, Tidbit, who had been investigating the open pantry, backed out with a bag of dog food in her teeth and dragged it across the kitchen. Something fluttered out from beneath it. A square of paper with a green border.

"Oh," said Wren.

"Oh," said Dad.

Theo, who had been sitting very still at the table with his toast cut into four equal triangles, said calmly: "The dog gets fed at 5:30. Mom feeds her at 5:30. This week Mom wasn't here at 5:30. The bag moved."

They both stared at him.

"I'm just saying how the slip got there," said Theo.

Dad sat down at the table and put his head in his hands. For a moment, Wren thought he was upset. Then she realized his shoulders were shaking. He was laughing.

"It was under the dog food," he said. "The whole time. The dog food was on it."

"I'm still late," said Wren. But the thing in her chest let go a little, and she started laughing too, and then Theo, who did not always laugh when other people did but did sometimes laugh at surprising things, said "The bag moved," once more, solemnly, and that was it. All three of them were gone.

Dad signed the slip. Wren made the bus by forty seconds.

---

That evening, Grandpa Lou called a meeting.

"I've been in this house for five days," he announced, standing at the head of the kitchen table with his reading glasses on his forehead and a yellow legal pad in his hand. "And I've noticed something. Your mother runs this household like an air traffic controller. And I've never seen her sit down."

"That's not true," said Dad. "She sits sometimes."

"Name one."

Dad opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at the ceiling.

"Exactly," said Grandpa Lou. He clicked a pen. "Let's make a list. Everything that has to happen in a week, in this house, to keep everybody alive and fed and in clean socks. I'll write. You shout."

It started slowly. "Dishes," said Wren. "Trash." "Dog food," said Theo. "Laundry." "Groceries." "Lunches."

Grandpa Lou wrote it all down. Then he looked up. "That's the doing. What about the other part?"

"What other part?"

"The part before the doing. Somebody has to notice that the milk is almost out. Somebody has to remember that Theo's bus changes on Fridays. Somebody has to know that the dentist appointment is on the fourteenth and the permission slip is due on Thursday and that Theo's shoes are too small."

Nobody said anything.

"Who knows all that?" said Grandpa Lou, gently.

"Mom," said Wren.

"Mom," said Dad, a little quietly.

"Mom," Theo agreed. "And she writes it on the pile."

"That," said Grandpa Lou, writing at the top of a new page in big letters, *NOTICING, REMEMBERING, PLANNING,* "is also work. It's the invisible kind. You can't see it, but when it isn't done, everything falls over, and then a slip of paper is lost under the dog food."

Wren looked at the list. She had never once thought of *remembering* as a job. It had always just happened, as if it were weather.

"It's a lot," she said.

"It's a lot," Grandpa Lou agreed. "And that's no complaint about anybody. Your mother's way of doing it works fine. She has been carrying the whole bag because nobody mentioned it was heavy. So let's look at the whole bag together and decide who carries what."

"A job should have one owner," said Theo.

They all turned.

"The Frequency Buddies had this problem," Theo said. He sat up straighter. "In the episode with the basket. Everybody thought somebody else was bringing the apples, so nobody did. Then Professor Puddles said, 'A job that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.' So they gave each friend one job."

"That's very wise," said Grandpa Lou. "Who's Professor Puddles?"

"He's a raindrop with glasses."

"Then he's my kind of man."

So they did it. It took an hour. Every job on the list got one owner and one backup, written in two columns: *Who does it* and *Who steps in.* Wren took lunches (hers and Theo's) and the dishes on weeknights. Dad took the laundry and the Tuesday dinner. Grandpa Lou took the plants, the fix-it list, and Wednesday dinner, which he announced would be "mostly soup." Theo, who had gone very quiet and then very focused, said he wanted to feed the dogs.

"At 5:30," said Wren, smiling.

"At exactly 5:30," said Theo. "Every day. And I will put a card on the bag so it doesn't move."

That was the first time Wren had thought: *fair doesn't mean everybody does the same thing.* Theo's job had clear steps and a fixed time, which he liked. Dad's job was big and boring, which Dad claimed to like. Wren's job needed a little planning, and she found she didn't mind. A fair share was one that actually fit the person who had to carry it.

And Mom's jobs? Grandpa Lou drew a thick line across the bottom of the page. "Your mother's list," he said, "is now whatever's left after we do all this. And we are going to check every Sunday that it is shorter than it was."

"What about the pile?" said Dad.

"I'll take the pile," said Wren, surprising herself. "Just the sorting. And the dates. I'll put them on the whiteboard."

Dad looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached across the table and squeezed her shoulder. "Thank you," he said, and she could tell he meant it, and he also meant *I'm sorry I didn't see it either.*

When Mom came home at ten that night, tired and carrying a bag of takeout noodles, she found a whiteboard on the fridge with every job on it and a pickle-shaped magnet holding up a note. The note was in Theo's handwriting. It said:

*DEAR MOM. YOU ARE NOT THE ONLY ONE WHO REMEMBERS NOW. LOVE, THEO. P.S. THE DOG FOOD IS FINE.*

Mom read it twice. Then she sat down at the kitchen table, put her face in her hands, and stayed there.

Wren's stomach did a flip, and she took a step forward. But Dad was already pulling out the chair next to her. He sat close, one hand on her back, and said nothing. Mom's shoulders went up and down. After a minute she looked up, and she was laughing and crying at the same time.

"I did not know," she said, "how tired I was."

"We know," said Grandpa Lou, from the doorway, with a pot of soup. "That's why there's soup."

::: try
Jobs have two parts: the doing, and the noticing, remembering and planning. Write both on your list. The second part is easy to miss because nobody sees it.
Give each job one owner and one backup. "Everybody's job" often means nobody's.
A fair share fits the person who has to carry it. If one person likes a clear time and steps, and another likes a big job to chew on, they can both be fair.
:::
