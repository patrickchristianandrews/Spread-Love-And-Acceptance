# Fine
ill: phone

The text came in at 7:12 on Monday morning, while Wren was standing in the hallway holding a toothbrush.

::: chat
Imani: sleepover friday?? my place. nana is making peach cobbler
Josie: YES
Mateo: obviously. i will bring the good controller
:::

She looked at it for a while. A sleepover at Imani's was one of the best things a person could be invited to. Imani's nana made cobbler with a crust like a cloud, and Imani's dad let them stay up until the second movie ended. Wren would normally have typed three exclamation points before the toothbrush was even in her mouth.

But her chest still felt like a sock drawer somebody had dumped out on the floor. She typed.

::: chat
Wren: maybe. things are weird at home
Imani: weird how??
Wren: fine. my grandpa is moving in and i lose my room
Mateo: lol you get to live with Theo. free alarm clock
Josie: haha
:::

Wren stared at the screen.

*Free alarm clock.*

She knew what Mateo meant. Theo did wake up at 6:00 every morning, exactly, because 6:00 was when he woke up. Everybody in the family knew that. It was kind of a joke, even a fond one.

But it didn't feel like a joke right now. It felt like Mateo was saying that her whole big problem was something to laugh about, and that Theo was something to laugh about, and that was not a thing anyone else got to do. Wren was allowed to be annoyed with Theo. Nobody else was.

Her thumbs went fast.

::: chat
Wren: fine.
Imani: k
:::

*K.*

One letter. It sat there by itself, small and gray and cold. Imani had typed "weird how??" with two question marks, and then, when Wren finally said something, all she wrote back was *k*.

Wren turned the phone face down on the sink. Her heart was banging. She brushed her teeth so hard her gums stung.

---

By lunch she had a headache.

The thing about the group chat was that it kept going even when you stopped. All morning, her phone buzzed against her leg in her backpack. Buzz, buzz. She did not look at it. Not looking took more energy than looking would have.

In math, Josie leaned over and whispered, "Are you coming Friday or not?"

"I said maybe."

"Okay. You don't have to bite."

"I didn't bite," said Wren, who had bitten.

Josie turned back to her worksheet with her ears a little pink. Wren felt a flash of guilt, then a flash of anger at the guilt, and then, because it was the only thing left to do, she looked out the window and counted the bricks on the building across the parking lot. She didn't know she was doing it until she reached forty-one. It was the thing Theo did with ceiling tiles.

At lunch, Imani sat down across from her with a tray and a careful face.

"Hi," Imani said.

"Hi."

"Are you mad at me?"

Wren's first thought was *Yes.* Her second thought was *Why?* because she could not have said exactly why. Her third thought was a lot of words, all of them sharp.

"I'm fine," she said.

Imani's face did something small and closed up. "Okay," she said. She picked up her sandwich. She put it down again. "You said fine like four times this morning."

"Because I'm fine."

"Okay."

They ate without talking. Mateo came, said "What did I miss?" and got no answer from either of them, and went to sit with the soccer kids. The table felt as wide as a parking lot.

Wren's phone buzzed again. This time she looked.

::: chat
Mateo: did i say something wrong??
Josie: idk
Imani: i think we should leave her alone
:::

*Leave her alone.*

That was it. That was what she'd wanted, right? To be left alone? But reading it hurt like pressing on a bruise. They were talking about her, in a place she could see but not talk back to, as if she were a weather system that had come in over the school.

Without really deciding to, she typed, deleted it, typed again.

::: chat
Wren: whatever
:::

Then she muted the chat, pushed back her chair and walked out of the cafeteria, past the kid with the juice box and the teacher with the whistle, down the hall to the bathroom, where she locked herself in a stall and put her forehead against the cool metal door.

Her ears were hot. Her stomach was tight. Her hands were shaking a little. Her thoughts were going so fast they were tripping over each other: *They think I'm a problem. They're laughing at Theo. I lost my room and nobody cares. Imani hates me. Nobody gets it.*

She did not know that every one of those thoughts was a guess. They didn't feel like guesses. They felt like facts. Facts, she'd find out, usually have a bit less shouting in them.

After a while, the bell rang, and the hot feeling drained away and left something worse: a flat, tired, gray feeling, like a battery at one percent. In science she forgot her notebook. In the hallway she walked into the corner of a locker and didn't even say ow.

By the time she got home, she wasn't revved up anymore. She was running on empty.

---

The kitchen smelled like onions. Grandpa Lou's boxes would arrive on Saturday, and until then, the house was in the strange quiet of a place that knows something is coming. Mom was home early, standing at the counter with her laptop open and her badge still on.

"Hi, honey. How was school?"

"Fine."

Mom looked up. She did not say anything. She just looked, the way she did when she was reading a label.

On the fridge, held up by a magnet shaped like a pickle, was Theo's chart. He had made it two years ago, and the family had been using it ever since, with only a little grumbling. It was a ladder, drawn with a ruler. At the top rung, in green marker: CALM AND CONNECTED. In the middle, in orange: REVVED UP. At the bottom, in gray: RUNNING ON EMPTY. Next to each rung was a list of what it felt like, in Theo's neat capitals. REVVED UP: HOT EARS. FAST HEART. TOO MANY WORDS. RUNNING ON EMPTY: HEAVY. FLAT. WANT TO HIDE.

There were four magnets at the side, one for each of them, and a pickle-shaped extra one for company. Mom's was on the green rung. Dad's, which he'd put there that morning before work, was on the green rung too, with a tiny sticky note: *Big news day, nervous.* Theo's was on orange. His note said: *Too many unknowns.*

Wren's magnet was lying on the counter. She picked it up.

"Where are you?" Mom asked, softly.

"I told you. Fine."

"Fine isn't a rung," Mom said, and for a second Wren almost smiled, because it was true, and because Mom said it so seriously.

She put her magnet on the gray one. Then she didn't wait to be asked anything else. She went upstairs and lay down on the bed that was almost not hers anymore, and Sugarfoot came in at her own pace, climbed up and lay with her warm side against Wren's legs.

Nobody said anything. That was exactly right.

::: try
Reading a message, you only get the words. You don't get the face or the voice. Before you answer, read it again in a different voice. What if they were tired? Busy? Wearing gloves and typing with one thumb?
"Fine" can mean fine, or it can mean "I'm not ready to say." If you hear it, you can ask, "Does fine mean fine, or do you need a minute?"
When you answer a hard message while your heart is racing, you may send a different message than you meant. Wait until your ears cool down.
:::
