# The Project
ill: cloud

Mrs. Okafor loved weather almost as much as Grandpa Lou did, which was unfortunate for Wren, because the next project was about it.

"Groups of four," Mrs. Okafor said on Friday morning, tapping the board. "You will build a model of a weather system, make a poster that explains it, and give a five-minute talk. Due next Friday. Choose your groups. Quickly."

Wren's stomach dropped. She hadn't really talked to the Four Corners since Monday. Imani had waved on Tuesday, a small unsure wave. Josie had saved her a seat on Wednesday and Wren had pretended not to see. Mateo had sent three memes, which she'd left on read. The group chat sat muted on her phone like a sleeping animal.

Now it came across the room at her, in the form of three people with their chairs already moving.

"Hi," said Imani, sliding her desk next to Wren's. "We saved you a place."

"Okay," said Wren.

"Cool," said Mateo, a little too loudly. "So. Weather."

He took out a notebook. Mateo always had a notebook. He talked fast when he was nervous, and he was visibly nervous now, with his knee bouncing under the desk.

"So here's my idea," he said, flipping pages. "We do a thunderstorm. I'll build the model, because I have the clay and my dad has a hot glue gun. Imani, you're good at drawing, so you do the poster. Josie, you write the talk, because you're good at words. And Wren, you can, um..."

He looked at his notebook.

"You can do the labels."

"The labels," said Wren.

"The little cloud labels. Cumulus, nimbus, that stuff. It's easy." He flipped the page. "Then we practice Wednesday, and I'll put it all on one slide. Okay? Good. Everybody got their job?"

The ordinary part of Wren understood. Mateo was trying to be organized. He wanted to get it done. He was probably worried they'd run out of time.

But there was another part, and that part was already past the ordinary part and out the door, because it had heard only this: *You get the little cloud labels. Everyone else gets a real job. You don't get a vote.*

"You didn't ask me," she said.

"What?"

"You didn't ask me what I wanted to do."

"Well, I'm asking now. Do you want a different one?"

"You always do this."

It slipped out. It had a hard shape, like a stone.

"I always do what?" said Mateo, bewildered.

"You always take over! You always decide for everybody. You did it with the sleepover and you did it with the game last month and you did it with the group chat and now you're doing it with this, and then you say it's a joke if anybody gets mad!"

A silence landed on their four desks. Across the room, a couple of kids looked over.

"I'm not taking over," Mateo said. His voice cracked. "I'm trying to make a plan. Somebody has to."

"Can you both stop," said Josie quickly. "Everyone is looking."

"I don't care who's looking!" said Wren, and her voice was higher than she'd ever heard it. "Forget it. Do it without me. You were going to anyway."

She stood up so fast her chair tipped over. It hit the floor with a bang.

Mrs. Okafor said "Wren?" in the careful voice teachers use when they don't want to be a part of the problem but know they are.

"I need a minute," said Wren, which was the only sentence she could still find, and she walked out the door with her ears on fire.

---

The library was empty except for Ms. Park, the librarian, who was putting stickers on the spines of new books. She glanced up as Wren came in, took one look at her face, and said, "The reading corner is free. Take as long as you need," and went back to her stickers. She did not ask a single question.

Wren sank into a beanbag in the corner, her face hot and her hands trembling. Her heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears. *Mateo is a bully,* said her brain. *He thinks I'm nothing. He didn't even ask. Everyone's looking at me. I embarrassed myself.* Then, a half-second later: *I'm a horrible person. I yelled at him. Everyone hates me.*

It was so fast and so loud. It was like standing in a room with all the radios on at once.

*Static,* she thought, suddenly. *This is what Theo means.*

She looked at her hands. She could almost see Grumble floating over her head, gray and fuming, and Fuzz prickling on her shoulder, and Mumble in the shadows, wanting to run away. Not a real thing. But a good name for a real feeling.

*What does it want?* she asked herself, as if Theo were asking. Slowly, her breathing came up from the floor of her chest. She pressed her palms against her knees. *To be heard.*

Okay. Heard about what?

She made herself stop and breathe the way Mom had taught them, in for four, out for six, with the out-breath longer than the in. She did it five times. The pounding in her ears went from a drum to a tap. Her thoughts, which had been tumbling, slowed enough to be looked at one at a time.

She looked at the first thought. *Mateo is a bully.*

Was he? A bully, she knew, is someone who hurts people on purpose, over and over, and keeps going when you ask them to stop. She thought about Mateo. Mateo, who had once handed her his own umbrella in the rain and walked home soaked. Mateo, who made a plan for everything because plans made him feel safe. Mateo, whose knee bounced when he was scared. When he took over, did he stop if someone said stop? She actually did not know, because she had never said it. She had yelled it, after months of letting it pile up, with a "you always" on the front.

Was it true he *always* did it? She tried to remember an example besides the sleepover. She found two. That was not always. That was twice, and a feeling.

She looked at the second thought. *He thinks I'm nothing.* Nobody had said that. Mateo had given her a small job because it was the last one left. He had probably not thought about it. Which was its own kind of thing, but a different kind of thing.

What she could actually say about it was this: *When you gave out the jobs without asking, I felt like I didn't count.* That was true. It was also about as different from "You always take over!" as a sentence could be. One of them was a complaint, and the other one was something a person could answer.

She sat there for another minute.

"Wren?"

She looked up. It was Josie, standing awkwardly at the end of the shelf. Josie, who had once cried at a commercial for a bank.

"Mrs. Okafor said I could check on you," Josie said. "Mateo is in the hall. He feels terrible. He said to tell you he's sorry and he's not going to talk unless you want him to. And Imani said, um, to say she's here if you want." She shifted. "I don't know why I said all that in one breath."

Wren laughed, a wobbly laugh. "Thanks."

"Are you okay?"

*Fine,* Wren almost said. She heard it coming and stopped it with her teeth.

"No," she said. "I'm kind of a mess. I need a few more minutes, and then I think I owe Mateo a real sentence."

"Okay," said Josie. She stood there, not sure whether to stay or go. Then she sat down in the other beanbag, a little way off, and took a book off the shelf and opened it upside down, which Wren did not mention.

Ms. Park went on putting stickers on the spines.

After a while the bell rang. Wren went to wash her face. The hall was full of kids and noise, and it was just a hall. She was not fixed, not by a long shot. But the radios had been turned down, and she could finally hear what she was going to have to do.

::: try
When the static comes, name the feeling and ask what it wants. "Grumble wants to be heard" is a start.
Your thoughts when you're upset are guesses, even when they feel like facts. Pick one and ask: Did they say that, or did I?
Try swapping "You always..." for "When you did this, I felt that, and I'd like this." The first is a verdict. The second is something a person can answer.
