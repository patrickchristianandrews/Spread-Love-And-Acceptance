# Lights Out
ill: moon

Theo's room had rules, and Wren had agreed to every one of them. She had even helped write them.

*One. Lights out at 8:30. Two. The rain sound stays on all night. Three. Screens go in the hall drawer at 8:00. Four. If you need to talk after lights out, you whisper, and you only whisper about something that can't wait.*

They had written these on a poster over her new bed, in two colors of marker, on the Sunday before. It had seemed so reasonable then. Sharing a room had seemed like a thing you could manage with a poster.

That was before the Friday of the project.

By eight o'clock that night, Wren was empty. She'd had a few crackers for dinner. Her stomach was too knotted for food, and her body was too tired for anything else. She'd managed one short sentence to Mateo in the hallway: *I'm sorry I yelled. Can we talk Monday?* He'd nodded fast and said "Yeah. Totally. Yes," and looked at his shoes. It was something, but it wasn't enough. It sat in her chest like a stone she hadn't finished swallowing.

At 8:00, she was supposed to put her phone in the hall drawer.

She didn't.

She told herself it was just to check the time. She told herself she'd be quick. She got into bed, pulled the blanket up, tilted the screen so the light stayed in the cave of her knees, and opened the chat.

It had come back to life all afternoon.

::: chat
Mateo: i messed up today. i was trying to help and i made it worse
Mateo: wren if you're reading this im sorry. you can pick whatever job you want. you can pick ALL the jobs
Josie: nobody has to pick all the jobs lol
Imani: matty its ok
Imani: we all got loud. wren is it ok if we call you tomorrow
:::

Her eyes burned. There was a lump in her throat the size of a walnut. She had wanted so badly for them to say this. And now they had, and all she could think was that she'd been unfair, and everybody had seen it, and maybe they'd say it was okay only because they felt sorry for her.

*They're being nice because they pity me,* she thought, and her chest tightened.

"Wren," said Theo, from his bed.

"One minute," she whispered.

She started to type a reply. She deleted it. She started again. The screen flashed, and her phone buzzed against the mattress, and the buzz came through the whole bed like a small earthquake.

"Wren."

"One minute, Theo."

It was 8:19. In the dark, Theo's bed gave a small creak as he sat up. He had his headphones around his neck, and she could just make him out, sitting straight, with his hands folded in his lap.

"It's eight nineteen," he said. "The phone was supposed to go in the drawer at eight. The light is blue. It is shining on the ceiling. And it buzzes. The rain sound can't cover it."

"I know. I'm sorry. I just have to answer something important."

"Okay," said Theo.

He lay back down.

She typed. *hey. i'm sorry about today. i was having a really*

Buzz. Josie had sent a heart.

Buzz. Mateo, a sad face with a tear.

Buzz. Imani: *so can we call tmrw??*

Each one made a little pulse of blue on the ceiling. Wren watched her own hands tremble. Under the covers it was too hot, but she didn't want to move. *Just one more. I just have to say the right thing.* She did not know what the right thing was, and the harder she looked for it, the more it slid away.

From across the room, Theo spoke again. His voice was higher.

"It's eight thirty-four."

"Theo, please. I'm trying."

"Lights out was eight thirty. It's eight thirty-four. The phone is still on. The rule says eight." He was breathing fast now. "I can't go to sleep with it on. I have to go to sleep at eight thirty. If I don't, tomorrow is wrong. And it's still buzzing."

"I KNOW," said Wren, much louder than she meant to. "Just give me a second!"

Silence.

Then, in a very small voice: "It's been fifteen minutes of seconds."

And there it was. Something in her went off like a firecracker. The heat that had been building since Monday, since the kitchen, since the pancakes, all of it rose up in one huge, hot wave. She couldn't hold it in. She did not even try.

"You are SO annoying!" she said, sitting bolt upright. "Do you know that? Everything has to be your way! Your rules! Your times! Your headphones! You make everybody else's life harder, and then you act like you're the one who's suffering! No wonder nobody gets you! No wonder the dogs are the only ones who want to be around you!"

She heard what she had said when it was already out. It echoed in the room, like a plate that has been dropped and hasn't yet landed.

Nobody moved. The rain sound pattered on, soft and steady.

Then Theo did something Wren would never forget. He did not yell. He did not cry. He took his headphones from around his neck, put them over his ears, rolled over to face the wall, and lay perfectly still.

The silence of it was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

"Theo," she whispered.

Nothing.

"Theo, I didn't..."

He didn't move. His back was a small, stiff shape under the blanket, and Tidbit, who had been asleep on the rug, got up and padded over and lay down against his bed, pressed along the side of it, like a guard.

Wren's phone buzzed again. She turned it face down, then off, and shoved it into the drawer of the nightstand with shaking hands. The room went dark. The only light left was the little glow of the clock: 8:41.

She sat there for a long time with her arms around her knees.

*That was not fine,* she thought. *That was not even close to fine.*

She knew exactly what she had done. It was not a feeling or a guess. She had said something cruel, on purpose, to the person who was least able to fight back, and it had worked: his whole face had gone shut. Her stomach turned over.

There was an excuse, of course. She'd been tired, and hungry, and stretched thin. All of that was true. But she was already learning that being tired explains a thing without making it okay. An excuse could tell you why it happened. It couldn't take it back.

She wanted to say sorry. Right then. She had her mouth open to say it, and the words *I'm sorry* got stuck somewhere behind her teeth, heavy and flat as pennies.

*If I say it now, he'll have to listen to me, and he's got his headphones on,* she thought. *He doesn't want me talking.* She didn't know if that was a guess or a fact. She wanted to know, but she was afraid of the answer.

So she lay down in the dark, under a ceiling with no star, and listened to the rain. It sounded like a tiny sky, weeping politely to itself. After a long time, her eyes closed.

Down the hall, in the room that had once been hers, Grandpa Lou was awake. He heard the shouting. He heard the quiet that came after. He lay there, thinking, with his hands folded on his chest, and he did not go in.

Because he knew something Wren was about to find out. The worst moment of the night was over. The most important moment was still to come, and it was going to be on the stairs.

Phones and screens have a way of making the rest of the world seem small. If a shared room or a shared house has a screen rule, keep it. It's kind, and it's also a promise.
If you say something cruel when you are worn out, you still said it. Being tired explains it. Saying sorry is how you fix it.
When the words won't come, it usually means your body isn't ready yet. Give yourself a short break, and make a plan to return.
