# 8. Why owning it is hard for some people

*A Sunday afternoon, a semi in Swindon. Made up.*

Corinne has found out, from a letter, that Rafe moved four hundred pounds out of their joint savings in March to cover his car repair, without telling her. It isn't the money. They have enough. It's that this is the second time, and last time he'd promised to talk to her first.

She sits down opposite him at the kitchen table, with the letter, and says so. Calmly, she thinks.

Rafe looks at the letter. Then at the table. He doesn't say anything. His face goes still and slightly blank, and when she says, "Rafe? Can you at least say something?" he gets up and walks out to the garden and stands by the shed.

Corinne sits at the table thinking: *He won't even talk to me about it. He just walks away.*

Two hours later he comes back in. He's pale, and he says, slowly, "I'm sorry. I couldn't speak. You're right, I did it, and I said I wouldn't. I was embarrassed about the car and I didn't want to admit it. Can we talk now?"

## Refusal and overwhelm can look identical from the outside.

The layers. On the surface, a question and a man walking out. One layer down, the second broken promise about the same thing, which is what Corinne actually came to talk about. Below that, Rafe's body has gone into something close to a shutdown: his words have gone, and walking to the shed is the only move left. Below that, embarrassment about the car, which was the reason he hid the transfer in the first place, and which the letter dragged straight back into the light. And at the root, the plain fact that a body on high alert can't do a careful conversation, however much its owner might want to.

Taken slowly, what Corinne saw as refusal was something else. That doesn't make the transfer all right, and it doesn't mean she was wrong to raise it. It means that whether someone *can't* own something right now and whether they *won't* are very different situations, and they need very different responses.

This chapter is about the reasons people find ownership hard. It's written with compassion, on purpose, because almost all of us have at least one of these reasons in us somewhere. It is not a set of labels to put on someone. Every one of these reasons is something a person *does*, under certain conditions, and none of them is what a person *is*.

## Your state sets the volume.

Start with the body, because it comes first. The science of the nervous system has a simple idea at its centre: your state sets the volume. A body that's calm hears a comment at the volume it was said. A body on high alert hears every comment louder, and a body past its limit may stop hearing at all.

> **From the research.** In John Gottman's work with couples, "flooding" describes being so physically overwhelmed in a conflict (heart rate up, breath short, thoughts narrowing) that listening and problem-solving become close to impossible. He found that repeated flooding predicted later trouble, and that taking a real break to calm down, before returning, helped.
> *Gottman and Silver (1999) · Moderate: a popular book built on the observational research described in chapter 7; the advice about breaks is widely used and sensible, though the book itself isn't a controlled trial.*

When Corinne put the letter down, Rafe's body did what bodies do when they sense a threat. That's not an excuse for the transfer. It's an explanation of the next ten minutes. Nobody owns anything well while their body is shouting at them to get out of the room, and pushing harder ("Can you at least say something?") turns the volume up further.

> **Two ways to read this: going silent.**
>
> Some people's wiring, when overloaded, shuts down. Words go first, and sometimes movement and expression too. Their face can look blank or cold. They may leave the room, or sit very still. From the inside, it's often closer to a fuse tripping than a choice: the system protecting itself. Many people who experience it are distressed by how it looks to others, and want to explain, and can't, until it passes.
>
> Other people's wiring reads silence and walking away as a message. In most social settings, going quiet in the middle of a hard conversation means *I won't engage with this*, or *you don't matter enough to answer.* It's easy to take it as stonewalling, or the silent treatment.
>
> **If you shut down,** a short sentence agreed in advance can do a lot. Something like "I'm overloaded, I'm not leaving the conversation, I'll come back in an hour" can be said in a few words, or written on a card, or texted from the garden. Then come back, as Rafe did. The return is what tells the other person the silence wasn't a refusal.
>
> **If silence feels like a message,** try asking yourself whether this looks like *won't* or *can't*. A blank face, slow speech or a sudden need to leave can be signs of overload rather than contempt. Give it time and see whether they return. If they do, and they can talk then, it was probably overload. If they never return to it, that's a different pattern, and the rest of this chapter has more on it.

> **From the research.** In a study co-designed with autistic young people, participants described shutdown as an involuntary state of overload in which the ability to speak or respond can be lost, as distinct from deliberately ignoring someone, and explained that it is often misread by people around them.
> *Phung, Penner, Pirlot and Welch (2021) · Early: a small qualitative study with eight young people, which describes experiences well and can't say how common they are.*

## Five reasons people find ownership hard.

Once the body has settled, many people can own what they did, the way Rafe did. Some still find it hard. In practice, the reasons tend to fall into five groups. People usually have more than one, and the mix changes with the topic and the day.

### Shame

Chapter 3 covered this one: owning the act feels like accepting a verdict on the whole self. The person isn't defending the act so much as defending their right to be thought of as a decent human being. They may get angry, collapse into "I'm a terrible person", or quickly change the subject. Rafe's embarrassment about the car was shame at a small scale: he hid the transfer because owning it meant owning the car trouble too.

What helps: make the self safe before you ask about the act. Say what you're not saying. Leave room for them to own it without having to grovel.

### Defensiveness

Here, criticism is felt as an attack, and the response is to defend: counter-complaints, justifications, "that's not what happened". Joss in the Glasgow kitchen, buttering toast, is an example. Defensiveness often runs on the body's alarm system: the threat is felt before it's understood.

What helps: lower the temperature first. Raise things at a calm time, start softly, keep to one specific thing. Chapter 7 has the rest.

### Limited self-awareness

Some people genuinely don't see what they did, or don't see its effect. Pete in chapter 1 had no idea his mother had been ringing Lorraine. This isn't stupidity, and it isn't unique to anybody. Every one of us has blind spots about ourselves.

> **From the research.** A meta-analysis of 266 studies found that people tend to give themselves credit for good outcomes and put bad outcomes down to circumstances, and that this tendency was large on average, though smaller in some cultures (notably East Asian samples) than others. Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson's book on self-justification describes how, once we've done something, our minds work quietly to make it look reasonable to ourselves, which makes it hard to see clearly afterwards.
> *Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde and Hankin (2004) · Strong: a large meta-analysis. Tavris and Aronson (2007) · Theory: a readable book built on decades of research on cognitive dissonance.*

What helps: specifics, and questions (chapter 6). The concrete, camera-level detail is often what lets someone see. "She rings me when you don't ring her back. Four times this month."

### Learned behaviour

Some people grew up somewhere apologies weren't made, or were made only under pressure, or were used against the person who made them. A boy whose father never once said sorry, and who watched his mother's apologies get thrown back at her for years, can grow into a man for whom "I was wrong" feels genuinely dangerous, or simply foreign: a phrase in a language he never learned. He may explain, minimise or shift blame without even noticing, because that's what owning things sounded like at home.

What helps: say the unwritten rule out loud, without contempt. "In this house, saying sorry doesn't mean you lose." Model it yourself, often, for small things. Notice and thank the first awkward attempts.

### Unwillingness

And sometimes a person understands perfectly well what they did and how it landed, and doesn't want to own it, because owning it would cost something: discomfort, a change in behaviour, a concession, a loss of position. It's the hardest of the five to sit across from, and it's real. It isn't the same as the other four, and it doesn't respond to the same things. Patience, questions and reassurance about the self won't change a decision.

What helps: clarity about what you will and won't keep doing. Chapter 11 is about that.

## You can't tell which it is from one apology.

The five can look very alike in the moment. A person who is overwhelmed and a person who is unwilling may both go quiet. A person who is ashamed and a person who is defensive may both turn it back on you. One evening tells you very little.

Patterns tell you a great deal. Over weeks and months, some questions are worth asking quietly, of yourself:

- When things are calm, do they come back to it? Do they become more reflective later, even if they couldn't in the moment?
- When they understand something, does their behaviour change, at least a bit, at least sometimes?
- Do they own small things easily and only struggle with certain topics? (That usually points to shame or a learned fear around those topics.)
- When they see the effect of something on you, do they look troubled, even if they can't say so?
- Or, over a long run, do they understand clearly, never come back to it, and carry on in the same way?

The first four point towards someone who finds ownership hard but is, in their own way, trying. The last points towards unwillingness. Most people are a mixture, and most people improve when the conditions do.

There's a sentence in the notes behind this book that's worth keeping in your pocket: the best approach for someone who is ashamed and defensive is different from the approach for someone who repeatedly avoids responsibility while understanding exactly what they're doing. The first needs safety, time and specifics. The second needs a limit.

## You can't do someone else's inner work.

This is the hardest truth in the chapter, and it applies to every one of the five. You can make ownership easier. You can make it safer, more specific, better timed, less shaming. You can make it harder to deflect. You can explain clearly what accountability would look like. And you can decide what you'll do if it doesn't come.

What you can't do is the looking for them. The moment where a person sees what they did and lets it be true is something that happens inside them, on their timetable, or not at all. You can open the door, light the room and put the chair in the right place. You can't walk them through it.

That's not a failure on your part. It's simply where your part ends.

**Said:** "Rafe? Can you at least say something?"
**Heard:** *You're failing at this conversation, too.*
**Meant:** *I'm frightened you don't care.*
**A better way:** "I can see this has hit you hard. I'm not going anywhere. Take the time you need, and come and find me when you can talk."

> **Say it**
>
> When someone has shut down: "I can see you can't talk right now. That's OK. Come back when you can. I'll be here."
>
> When you're the one overloaded: "I'm not walking out on this. I can't speak yet. Give me an hour."
>
> When a pattern needs naming, on a calm day: "I've noticed that when I bring up money, we never get back to it. I don't think that's on purpose. Can we work out a way that makes it easier for you to come back to?"

The website's *Calm-Down Kit* helps you make a plan for heated moments in advance, including what you'll say and where you'll go (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-11). If you're in the middle of one right now, *Upset right now?* has a fast version for two people (spreadloveandacceptance.com/upset-right-now). *The logic behind it* has a whole section on shutdowns and on why a reaction can make sense once you can see the steps (spreadloveandacceptance.com/the-logic-behind-it).

## One small thing to try

On a calm day, agree a short phrase with someone close to you that means *I'm overloaded, not leaving, back soon*. Make it something either of you can say in three or four words, or text. Then, the next time either of you uses it, the other person's only job is to believe it, and the first person's only job is to come back.

## What to remember

- A body on high alert can't do careful ownership. Calm first, conversation second.
- Shutdown and refusal can look the same from outside. The difference shows in whether the person comes back.
- Shame, defensiveness, limited self-awareness, learned behaviour and unwillingness are five different reasons, and they need different responses. Most people have a mix.
- Judge by patterns over time, not one apology. You can open the door, but the looking is theirs to do.
