# 2. Sorry for the feeling, or sorry for the thing

*Saturday night, the drive home from a family dinner in Cardiff. Made up.*

Fran's sister got engaged, and the whole family went for a curry to celebrate. Twice during the evening Fran started telling a story, and twice Dele, her partner of four years, stopped her to fix a detail. The first time it was the day of the week. The second time it was the price of a taxi, which she'd said was twenty pounds and he was fairly sure was fourteen. Her dad laughed. Her sister said, "Ooh, fact-checked," and everyone laughed again, and Fran laughed too, and didn't finish the story.

In the car she says, "That really got to me, the correcting. In front of my family."

There's a long pause while Dele thinks about it properly. Then he says, carefully and with complete sincerity, "I'm sorry it made you feel that way."

Fran looks out of the window for the rest of the M4.

## One sentence, two completely different messages.

Lay it out quickly. On the surface, an apology with the right word in it. One layer down, the apology is about her reaction, and the corrections themselves have dropped out of the sentence. Below that, Dele doesn't yet see what was wrong with the corrections: the details were inaccurate and he fixed them, which in his book is helping. Below that again, Fran grew up as the younger sister whose stories got finished and fixed by other people at that same table, and tonight was the old evening come round again. And at the root, a cost has been moved from one person to another without either of them quite noticing.

Now slowly.

"I'm sorry it made you feel that way" is a sentence with an odd grammar. Its subject is *it*, and *it* made *you* feel. The speaker's action has gone missing. What's left is regret about the other person's emotional state, which is a real thing to regret, offered in place of something else. That something else is responsibility, which is a different thing entirely.

**Regret** is a feeling about an outcome. You can regret the rain. You can regret that a friend is having a hard week. You can regret, quite genuinely, that someone you love is upset.

**Responsibility** is a statement about your part in causing the outcome. It names what you did. "I corrected you twice in front of your family." It needs a verb, and the verb needs to be yours.

An apology with regret and no responsibility leaves the other person holding the thing they came to talk about. They raised an action. They got back a comment about their feelings. And because the comment was technically kind, they now look unreasonable if they push.

## The sentence can be completely sincere.

It's tempting to treat "I'm sorry you feel that way" as a trick. Sometimes it is one: a way of closing a conversation without conceding anything. But in the car on the M4, Dele isn't being slippery. He's being as exact as he knows how to be.

He is, honestly, sorry that Fran is upset. He doesn't yet understand what he did wrong. The day of the week *was* Thursday and the taxi *was* fourteen pounds. To say "I'm sorry I corrected you" would, from inside his head, mean apologising for being accurate, and that would be saying something he doesn't believe. So he says the true thing he does believe. It's the most honest sentence available to him, and it lands as the least honest thing he could have said.

That's the gap this book keeps returning to. The same nine words can be a dodge from one person and a careful, truthful statement from another, and the listener usually can't tell which from the words alone.

> **Two ways to read this: "I'm sorry it made you feel that way."**
>
> Some people's wiring is very literal about apologies. Saying sorry for something you don't think was wrong feels like lying, and lying feels worse than the argument. If you aren't sure what you did, the only honest sorry is a sorry about the feeling. To a brain that works like this, "I'm sorry it made you feel that way" is a sincere and careful answer.
>
> Other people's wiring hears what's missing from a sentence as loudly as what's in it. To them, the missing verb is the message. "Sorry you feel that way" is a well-known way of not apologising, and they've heard it used that way often enough to flinch.
>
> **If you'd say it and mean it,** say the true thing more fully so it can't be mistaken for the dodge: "I can see this really hurt, and I'm sorry about that. I don't understand yet what I did. Will you tell me, and give me a minute to think about it?" That's still completely honest, and it opens the door instead of closing it.
>
> **If you hear it and flinch,** check before you decide what it was. "Are you sorry I'm upset, or sorry about the correcting? They're different and I'd like to know which." Someone who meant it sincerely will usually be relieved to be asked. Someone who meant it as a full stop will tell you that too, by what they do next.

## An economist would call it a cost moved onto someone else's books.

Economists have a word for a cost that one party creates and another party pays: an externality. The classic example is a factory upstream whose smoke is breathed by the town downstream. The factory's books look fine. The cost is real; it has just landed on someone who didn't choose it.

A non-apology works the same way. Something was done, and it cost something: Fran's evening, a little of her standing at her own family's table, the end of her story. When the apology is about her feelings, the cost stays on her books. Worse, it picks up a second charge, because now the problem under discussion is her reaction, which is something she apparently needs to manage. The person who did the thing walks away with the matter closed and nothing owed.

Looked at this way, the non-apology has a quiet incentive behind it. In the short run, it's cheaper for the person giving it. It ends the conversation without having to say anything uncomfortable. Nobody has to choose that on purpose for it to become a habit; things drift towards whatever is cheapest in the moment. That's why "sorry you feel that way" is so common, including from people who are otherwise kind.

> **From the research.** In one set of experiments, people who were asked to write a message refusing to apologise afterwards reported higher self-esteem and a greater sense of power than people who apologised or simply didn't, at least in the short term. The authors pointed out that this helps explain why refusing can feel good, and why it makes repair harder.
> *Okimoto, Wenzel and Hedrick (2013) · Moderate: two studies with self-report measures, short-term feelings only; it says nothing about what refusing does to a relationship over time.*

The long run is another matter. The cost doesn't disappear because one person stopped looking at it. It sits on the other person's books and accrues, and some months later it gets paid all at once, usually in a row about something unrelated.

## What the hurt person usually wants is the verb.

When someone raises a hurt, they almost never want you to take responsibility for their feelings. Nobody can do that, and most people know it. What they want is acknowledgement of the specific thing you did, a sign that you can see how it landed, and some sense that you'd do it differently.

> **From the research.** Roy Lewicki and colleagues asked several hundred people to rate apologies built from six parts: expressing regret, explaining what went wrong, acknowledging responsibility, declaring repentance, offering repair and asking for forgiveness. Acknowledging responsibility came out as the most important part, with an offer of repair second. Asking for forgiveness mattered least. Apologies with more of the parts were rated as better.
> *Lewicki, Polin and Lount (2016) · Moderate: two studies, using written apologies people read rather than ones they received, so real spoken apologies may differ.*

Regret, on its own, was the part that does least of the heavy lifting. It isn't useless. It's the opening note. But a whole apology made only of regret is a song that's all introduction.

So when Fran tries again, two days later, she doesn't argue about whether she should have been upset. That argument is unwinnable, and it's also beside the point. She goes back to the specific thing.

**Said:** "I'm sorry it made you feel that way."
**Heard:** *Your feelings are the problem here, and I'm sorry you have them.*
**Meant:** *I'm sorry you're hurt. I don't yet see what I did.*
**A better way (from Dele, once he understood):** "I corrected you twice in front of your family, and the second time your sister made a joke of it. The details didn't matter to the story. I can see it made you look like you didn't know your own life. I'm sorry I did that. If something's wrong in a story you're telling, I'll leave it, and if it matters I'll tell you afterwards."

The better version is still accurate, which matters to Dele, and it no longer asks him to apologise for being accurate. It apologises for the timing and the setting, which were his choices.

## Redirect to the thing, every time.

When someone keeps apologising for your feelings, the instinct is to defend the feelings: to prove you were right to be upset. Resist it. Once you're arguing about whether your hurt was justified, the conversation is about you, and the action has vanished entirely.

Instead, go back to the behaviour. Name it, plainly, in words a camera would accept. Make it small and specific enough that nobody can mistake it for an attack on their character. "When you corrected the taxi price in front of everyone." Then say what you're asking for, and what you aren't.

It helps more than you'd think to say what you're *not* asking for. A lot of people dig in because they believe they're being asked to accept something enormous: that they're careless, controlling, a bad partner. When you tell them clearly that you aren't asking for that, they can often stop defending and start listening.

> **Say it: a message you could send**
>
> "Can I try to explain something that keeps happening when I bring up a hurt? When you say 'I'm sorry it made you feel that way', it sounds to me like you're sorry about my reaction, rather than about what happened. I'm not asking you to be responsible for my feelings. They're mine. And I'm not asking you to agree with every word of how I see it. I'm asking you to own your part: the words or the choices that were yours. An apology that would land with me sounds something like 'I did this, I can see how it affected you, and here's what I'd do differently.' I'd like us to be able to talk about hard things without either of us having to defend ourselves to the death. And I want the same standard to apply to me when I'm the one who got it wrong."
>
> Change every word that doesn't sound like you. Keep the last sentence.

You can say it, write it, or do some of each. Some things go better on paper, because the other person gets to read the whole thought before they answer and can take their time. ## If you're the one who says "sorry you feel that way".

You might recognise yourself in Dele, or in the less sincere version. Either way, there's a short route out.

Ask yourself what, exactly, you're sorry for. If the answer is "that they're upset", you've found regret, and that's a fine start. Then ask: is there anything I did, any word or choice that was mine, that contributed? Very often there is, even when it's smaller than the other person thinks and even when they've got some of the details wrong. You can own that part, fully and plainly, without agreeing to the rest. Chapter 4 goes into how.

And if you honestly can't see anything you did, say so in a way that keeps the door open: "I can't see it yet. Help me see it." That isn't an apology, and it doesn't pretend to be. It's an invitation, and it's honest.

The *Apology languages* page on the website treats the parts of a good sorry as a menu rather than a script, so you can ask the other person which part they need (spreadloveandacceptance.com/apology-languages).

## One small thing to try

Next time you're about to say "sorry", stop for one breath and finish the sentence in your head: *sorry I…* If the next word is a verb you did, say the whole sentence out loud. If the next word is *you*, as in "sorry you…", you've reached for regret instead of responsibility. That's allowed, and it's often true. Just say what it is, and ask what you're missing.

## What to remember

- Regret is a feeling about an outcome. Responsibility names your part in causing it. A good apology has both.
- "I'm sorry it made you feel that way" can be a sincere, careful sentence, and it still leaves the action out. Ask which one you're hearing before you decide.
- A non-apology moves a cost onto the other person's books. It's cheaper in the moment and dearer later.
- When someone keeps apologising for your feelings, go back to the specific behaviour, and say clearly what you're not asking for.
