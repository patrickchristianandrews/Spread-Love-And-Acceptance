# 3. Owning it is not a verdict on you

*A Sunday afternoon, a garden centre café outside Norwich (made up).*

Euan has been working up to this for a week. Over a pot of tea and two slightly dry scones, he tells his mum, Marion, that he knows she told his sister about him losing his job. He'd asked her not to. He wanted to tell Kirsty himself, when he had a new one lined up.

Marion puts her cup down. "Well. I suppose I'm a terrible mother now."

"I didn't say that."

"No, you don't need to. I can't do anything right, can I? I only told her because I was worried about you. But fine. I'm the villain."

Ten minutes later, Euan is reassuring his mother that she's a wonderful mother, she's always been there for him, he didn't mean to upset her. He drives home having apologised to her, for bringing it up. Nothing has been said about Kirsty.

## A small act turned into a whole person.

The layers, quickly. On the surface, a confidence passed on. One layer down, Euan asked her clearly not to, so it was a promise broken, not just a slip. Below that, Marion heard "you did a thing that hurt me" as "you are a bad mother", and from that moment the conversation stopped being about Kirsty and started being about her. Below that, Euan wanted one small acknowledgement and instead had to comfort her through a feeling he hadn't caused. And at the root, something that runs underneath a huge number of failed apologies: when owning an act feels like accepting a verdict on your whole self, people will do almost anything to avoid it.

Taken slowly, that last layer is the subject of this chapter.

## Guilt says "I did a bad thing". Shame says "I am bad".

Psychologists draw a careful line between two feelings that most of us lump together.

**Guilt** is about an action. *I did something wrong.* It's uncomfortable, and it points outward, towards the person you affected and what you could do about it. Guilt tends to make people want to put things right.

**Shame** is about the self. *I am something wrong.* It's not so much uncomfortable as unbearable, and it points inward. You can't repair a whole self over a pot of tea, so shame looks for the exits: hiding, denying, minimising, explaining, or turning the tables so the other person becomes the problem.

> **From the research.** June Tangney and colleagues spent years testing that distinction. Across their studies, people who tended towards guilt were more likely to feel for the person they'd hurt and to want to make amends. People who tended towards shame were more likely to hide, to deny, to blame others and to feel anger. In one early study, shame-proneness went with anger and with blaming other people; guilt-proneness didn't.
> *Tangney and Dearing (2002); Tangney, Wagner, Fletcher and Gramzow (1992) · Moderate: a large body of research, with ongoing debate about how cleanly questionnaires separate the two feelings, and about differences between cultures.*

Marion's "I suppose I'm a terrible mother" is shame talking. Nobody accused her of being a terrible mother. Her mind jumped from the act to the self in about half a second, and once she was standing in front of that verdict, the only bearable move was to defend herself against it. Euan, being kind, joined in. And so the thing that actually happened, a promise broken, got lost under a much bigger thing that nobody had said.

This is very common. It isn't a flaw in a few difficult people. It's a very ordinary human response, and almost everybody has a topic where it fires. For one person it's being a good parent; for another, being competent at work; for another, being a fair partner, or a loyal friend, or someone who "isn't like that". Touch that topic, even lightly, and the self comes rushing in to defend itself.

## Debating keeps the claim and the person apart.

Formal debating has a rule that turns out to be one of the most useful ideas anywhere near an apology. Separate the claim from the person. An argument can be weak, or a fact wrong, without the person who made it being stupid or bad. Good debaters concede a point all the time. They say, "My opponent is right about that," and carry on, and nobody thinks less of them. Conceding a point is a different thing from conceding the round.

Ownership works the same way. An action can be wrong without the person who did it being a bad person. Owning the action is conceding a point. It doesn't concede the round, and it certainly doesn't concede your whole character.

That sounds obvious written down. In the moment it's very hard to feel, because shame moves faster than reasoning. So it helps to have the sentence ready, already built, before you need it. Something like: *I did that. It was wrong. I'm not a bad person, and it was still wrong.* All three parts are true at once, and each one makes the others easier to say.

> **From the research.** Karina Schumann and Carol Dweck found that people who believed personality can change were more likely to accept responsibility and apologise for things they'd done, including in a diary study of couples over a week. In separate work, Schumann had people reflect on a value that mattered to them before writing to someone they'd hurt. Those people offered fuller apologies and fewer defensive excuses than people who hadn't.
> *Schumann and Dweck (2014); Schumann (2014) · Moderate: several studies, a mix of real and imagined offences, mostly with students and couples in North America.*

Both findings point the same way. When people feel that their self isn't on trial, because they can change, or because they've just remembered something good about who they are, they can afford to own the act. The more secure the self, the cheaper the apology.

## Kindness to yourself makes amends more likely, not less.

There's a fear that being kind to yourself about a mistake will let you off the hook. The research suggests the opposite.

> **From the research.** In a set of experiments, Juliana Breines and Serena Chen asked people to think about a recent moral lapse. Those who were encouraged to write about it with self-compassion (understanding, without excusing it) reported more motivation to make amends and to avoid repeating it than those who were encouraged to boost their self-esteem or weren't encouraged at all.
> *Breines and Chen (2012) · Moderate: lab experiments with students, measuring how motivated people felt, which isn't the same as what they did afterwards.*

Self-compassion here doesn't mean telling yourself it was fine. It means talking to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend who'd done the same thing: *that was a mistake, it hurt someone, you can put it right, and you're still worth something.* Self-esteem asks, *am I good?* and gets defensive when the answer wobbles. Self-compassion skips the question and gets on with the repair.

## Collapsing is shame too.

There's a version of shame that looks, at first glance, like the opposite of defensiveness. Instead of fighting the verdict, the person agrees with it, loudly and completely. "You're right. I'm useless. I'm the worst friend in the world. I don't know why you put up with me."

It sounds like ownership. It has all the vocabulary of ownership, and more. But listen to what it's about: the self, again. The specific act (the confidence broken, the evening missed) has disappeared just as completely as it does in a counterattack, and now the person who was hurt has a new job. They have to reassure. "You're not useless. Of course I put up with you." Euan, driving home from the garden centre having apologised to his mother, has been given exactly this job.

Collapsing isn't usually a tactic. It's what shame feels like from the inside, and it's sincere. It still leaves the hurt person comforting the one who hurt them, which is the wrong way round. And it's exhausting to be on the receiving end of it, because every complaint, however small, turns into a crisis that you have to talk someone down from.

What helps, if you recognise yourself here, is proportion. A small act needs a small, plain apology: what you did, that it was yours, what you'll do. You can feel terrible privately, and talk about the terrible feeling with someone else on another day. In the moment, keep it to the size of the act. The person you hurt will usually find a short, specific "I'm sorry I told her, it was your news" far more comforting than a long account of your failings.

And if you're on the receiving end, you're allowed to bring it back, kindly: "I don't need you to feel awful. I just need you to see the one thing."

## What helps, on each side.

**If you're the one raising it,** frame it as an act and keep your sentence away from their character. Use the camera's words ("you told Kirsty about my job") and leave the verdict ("you can't keep anything to yourself") unsaid. Avoid "always" and "never", which turn one event into a personality. And it helps more than you'd expect to say out loud what you are *not* saying, because shame is listening for it.

| Said | "You told Kirsty about my job. I asked you not to." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *You're a bad mother who can't be trusted.* |
| Meant | *That one thing hurt, and I'd like you to see it.* |
| A better way | "Mum, I'm not saying you're a bad mum. You're not. I'm saying one thing hurt: I asked you to keep my job news to yourself and you told Kirsty. I'd like you to see why that mattered to me." |

It isn't Euan's job to manage his mother's shame. But a sentence that leaves her self out of the dock makes it easier for her to look at the act, which is what he wants.

**If you're the one being asked,** notice the jump. When you hear yourself think *so I'm a terrible…*, that's the moment. You've just turned an act into a verdict, on your own, without help. Try saying the act back instead: "You're right. You asked me not to tell her, and I did." Then, if you need to, say the part that keeps your self intact: "I did it because I was worried, and I'm not proud of it. It was your news to tell."

Marion, a week later, rings him, "I've been thinking about Kirsty. You asked me not to tell her and I did. I was worried and I wanted someone to worry with, but it was your news. I'm sorry. I won't pass on anything you've asked me to keep."

Notice that her explanation is still in there: she was worried. It's allowed in, after the ownership, as context. It just isn't standing in for it.

> **Two ways to read a single sentence of criticism**
>
> Some people's wiring meets criticism with a sharp, almost physical sting. It arrives fast and it arrives whole: a passing comment about one thing can land as rejection of the entire person, and the first few minutes are about getting through the feeling rather than hearing the content. People who experience this often describe it as something that happens *to* them rather than something they choose, and they can be mortified afterwards by how strongly they reacted.
>
> Other people's wiring hears a comment about one thing as a comment about one thing. If someone says "you left the gas on", that's information about the gas.
>
> **If criticism hits you like that,** you don't have to respond in the first minute. You can say, "Give me a minute, that's landed hard and I want to hear it properly," and come back to it once the sting has passed. Owning the act afterwards is still owning it, and it's often more honest than anything you'd have said in the first ten seconds.
>
> **If your wiring hears it lightly,** know that for some people the first reaction is out of proportion to what you said, and it's not a performance. Make the request small and specific, say what you're not saying, and give them time. The point will usually come back to you, clearer, once the wave has gone through.

> **From the research.** "Rejection sensitivity" describes a tendency to expect rejection anxiously, to see it quickly in ambiguous behaviour, and to react strongly. In one set of studies, people high in it were quicker to read rejection into a partner's thoughtless behaviour. Strong, fast emotional reactions are also common in ADHD, and a review found them across the lifespan, adding substantially to everyday difficulties. Research on rejection sensitivity in ADHD specifically is only just beginning: one small recent study of five students described it as felt in the body and followed by withdrawal.
> *Downey and Feldman (1996) · Moderate: three studies, mostly with students. Shaw, Stringaris, Nigg and Leibenluft (2014) · Strong: a large review. Rowney-Smith, Sutton, Quadt and Eccles (2026) · Early: five people in focus groups. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is a popular term; it isn't a formal diagnosis.*

> **Say it**
>
> If you're raising it: "I'm not saying you're a bad person. You're not. I'm saying one thing hurt, and I'd like us to be able to look at that one thing."
>
> If you're owning it: "You're right, I did that. I'm not proud of it. It doesn't make me a terrible person, and it was still wrong of me. I'm sorry."
>
> If shame has just hit you: "Hang on. I'm hearing this as 'you're awful', and I don't think that's what you said. Give me a minute and say it again?"

## One small thing to try

Think of one thing you did in the last month that you've been avoiding owning. Write it as a single sentence that starts "I" and has a verb you did. Under it, write: *That doesn't make me ___*, and fill in the verdict you're afraid of. Read both lines together. Most people find the first sentence becomes much easier to say once the second one is written down, because they can see how far apart the two really are.

## What to remember

- Guilt is about an act and tends to lead to repair. Shame is about the self and tends to lead to hiding, defending and blaming.
- Owning an act concedes a point. It doesn't concede the round, or your whole character.
- People own things more easily when their self isn't on trial. You can help that along on either side of the conversation.
- Kindness to yourself about a mistake makes amends more likely. It isn't the same as letting yourself off.
