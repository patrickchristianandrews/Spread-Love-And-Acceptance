# 5. What you meant and what landed

*A Wednesday morning, a meeting room in an office in Leeds, on a video call to a client (made up).*

Gemma spent three weeks on the analysis. She built the model, found the error in the client's own figures, and wrote the summary that the whole proposal now rests on. In the meeting, Osman, who leads the team, introduces everyone. When he gets to her he says, warmly, "And Gemma's just the numbers side, so if you've got any detailed questions, she's your person."

Gemma smiles at her camera. After the call she goes to make a tea she doesn't want and stands in the kitchen for a while.

That afternoon she tells him it bothered her. Osman is genuinely surprised. "I was bigging you up," he says. "I was telling them you're the expert. I didn't mean it like that at all."

"I know you didn't mean it," she says. "It still happened."

## Six words, two true stories.

Quickly, the layers. On the surface, a friendly introduction in a client meeting. One layer down, the word "just", which shrinks whatever comes after it, applied to the person who did the work the proposal stands on. Below that, Gemma has been passed over to present twice this year, and the word fell on a bruise that was already there. Below that, Osman thinks of "the numbers side" as the important side, so to him the sentence was praise; he has no idea it sounded like a footnote. And at the root, the oldest problem in communication: what's sent isn't always what arrives.

Now slowly, because both of them are telling the truth.

Osman's intent was good. He wanted the client to know Gemma was the expert. Gemma's experience of the impact was real. In front of the client, the person who did the core work was introduced as a support function. Both statements are true at the same time, and neither cancels the other.

## Intent belongs to the speaker. Impact belongs to the listener.

Psychology's plainest finding about conversation is that what's meant and what's heard are two different things. A message leaves one head carrying an intention and arrives in another head through a filter: the listener's mood, their history, what was said last time, the exact words chosen, the tone. What comes out the other side is the impact.

Each person in a conversation is the only real expert on one half of this. Osman is the expert on what he meant. Nobody can tell him he meant to belittle her, because he didn't. Gemma is the expert on how it landed. Nobody can tell her it didn't sting, because it did.

The trouble starts when one person tries to use their half to overrule the other's.

**"I didn't mean it"** is a true statement about intent, offered as if it settled the question of impact. It's usually said in good faith, and it usually makes things worse, because it sounds like *so you have no right to be hurt.*

**"You did it to put me down"** is a true statement about impact, offered as if it proved something about intent. It's equally common, and equally unfair, because it takes a sting and turns it into a motive.

> **From the research.** The authors of *Difficult Conversations*, from the Harvard Negotiation Project, describe this pattern as a central trap in hard conversations: we assume we know other people's intentions from the effect their actions had on us, and we expect our own good intentions to cancel out the harm we did. Their advice is to disentangle the two: say what the impact was without assigning an intent, and when you're told about an impact, take it seriously before explaining what you meant.
> *Stone, Patton and Heen (1999) · Theory: a practical book drawing on negotiation research and the authors' casework, widely used, though it isn't a controlled study.*

A sentence that has helped a great many people stuck at this point: *I believe you didn't mean to hurt me. Not meaning to hurt someone doesn't mean you had no part in it.* Both halves are true. The first half tells the other person their intent has been heard and believed. The second tells them it doesn't end the conversation.

On the About page of the website, I mention spending years being told my intent and my impact were different things, without anyone being able to show me where the gap opened. This chapter is an attempt at showing where.

## Where the gap opens.

The gap between meaning and landing usually opens in one of a handful of places.

**The words themselves.** One word can carry a meaning the speaker never put in it. "Just", "only", "actually", "obviously", "to be fair". Osman heard his sentence as a whole and meant it as praise. Gemma heard the "just" first, because it was the word that touched what she was worried about.

**The tone and the timing.** The same sentence lands differently said slowly at the end of a good day and quickly at the start of a bad one. Spoken, tone carries a lot of the meaning. Written, almost all of it is lost, and readers fill the gap with their own.

**The setting.** Said privately, "you're the numbers person" might have been fine. In front of a client, in the moment when roles are being set, it set hers small.

**The history.** A comment lands on whatever was already there. Two missed chances to present gave "just" a weight it wouldn't otherwise have had.

> **From the research.** In a series of experiments, Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues had people send short messages, some sarcastic and some serious, by email or by voice. Senders were confident their tone would come across. Over email, recipients identified the tone correctly only a little more often than chance, and were themselves confident they'd got it right. The authors put it down to egocentrism: when we write, we hear our own tone in our head, and forget the reader can't.
> *Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005) · Moderate: five lab experiments with students; exact figures vary between reports, and texting has changed since, but the pattern is widely accepted.*

## One word, sent at the wrong moment.

Writing is where intent and impact drift furthest apart, because the reader gets the words and none of the voice.

A few weeks after the curry night, Fran texts Dele from work: "Mum's asked if we can do Sunday lunch instead of Saturday. I said yes, hope that's OK?" Dele, who is in the middle of something, replies: "Noted."

Fran reads it four times on the bus. To her, "Noted." with a full stop is what a manager sends when they're annoyed with you and have decided not to say so. She spends the evening braced for an argument about her mother.

| Said | "Noted." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *I'm not happy about this, and I'm being curt so you know.* |
| Meant | *I've read it and added it to my calendar. Sunday is fine.* |
| A better way | "Sunday's fine, I've put it in the calendar. Busy now, talk later x" |

Nothing about Dele's reply was unkind, and nothing about Fran's reading was unreasonable. One word, without a voice, gave her mind a gap to fill, and she filled it with the version she was most worried about. Researchers who study written messages have a plain name for the speaker's half of this: egocentrism, meaning that we hear our own tone so clearly in our heads that we forget the reader can't.

There are two fixes, one for each side. The sender can spend five more words. The reader can treat a short message as short, and check before deciding it's cold: "Is Sunday really OK? Your 'noted' sounded a bit frosty!" If Dele was frosty, he'll say so. Usually, he'll send back a confused face and a heart.

## What helps, on each side.

**If you caused the impact,** take the impact first and the intent second. You don't have to give up your intent. You just have to put it in the right place. "I can see that came out as 'just the numbers person', in front of the client, after you'd done the core of the work. I don't see you that way. It's still what I said." Then, if it helps, say what you meant.

| Said | "I didn't mean it like that at all." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *Your reaction is a misunderstanding, and you should drop it.* |
| Meant | *I respect your work, and I'm horrified you think I don't.* |
| A better way | "I can see how 'just the numbers side' landed, especially in front of the client. You did the work this proposal stands on and I made it sound like a footnote. I'm sorry. I'll put it right with them on Friday's call, and I'd like you to present the model." |

That last sentence is the part that changes something. Osman doesn't need to beat himself up for a careless word. He needs to see it, own it and put it right where it happened.

**If you received the impact,** describe it without assigning a motive. Gemma's "I know you didn't mean it. It still happened" is close to perfect. It believes his intent and keeps the impact on the table. If it doesn't land, she can add the specific part: "It was the word 'just'. In front of the client, it made me sound like support."

And if you catch yourself sure of someone's motive from how something felt, try holding it as a question: "When you said that, it came across as if you were putting me down. Was that what you meant?" Most of the time, the answer surprises you.

> **Two ways to read "I said exactly what I meant."**
>
> Some people's wiring chooses words for what they literally say. If they said "you're the numbers person", they meant exactly that: you're the person who knows the numbers. They didn't put "only" or "less important" in the sentence, so they're baffled when someone finds it there. Some also speak directly, with fewer softeners, and are told they sound harsh when they meant to sound clear.
>
> Other people's wiring hears connotation as part of the message. "Just" isn't neutral; it shrinks. A missing "thank you" isn't an absence; it's a statement. To them, a speaker is responsible for the shades of their words as well as the dictionary meanings.
>
> **If you speak literally,** the shades of a word are part of what arrives, whether or not you put them there. You don't have to start hinting. You can add one plain sentence of meaning: "To be clear, I think your part is the most important bit of this." People mostly can't hear what you meant unless you say it out loud.
>
> **If you hear the shades,** it can help to know that for some people the shades really aren't in the sentence when it leaves them. Before you decide what they meant, ask. "Did you mean 'just' as in 'only'?" Their answer may be a genuine and slightly horrified no.

> **From the research.** In one study, people's reactions to everyday events were filmed, and other people tried to guess from the videos what had happened. Observers were less accurate at reading autistic people's reactions than other people's, and described them differently. The autistic people weren't less expressive; they expressed things differently. The authors suggest that part of the difficulty in these encounters lies with the observer.
> *Sheppard, Pillai, Wong, Ropar and Mitchell (2016) · Early: a small set of lab studies, though it fits other work on two-way misreading.*

## Impact doesn't need a villain.

Something important hides in this chapter. Impact without bad intent still deserves an apology, and the apology doesn't make the speaker a bad person. Owning impact is one of the cheapest, most effective repairs there is, precisely because it doesn't require anyone to have meant harm. "I didn't mean it, and I can see it hurt, and I'm sorry for the hurt" costs the speaker nothing they actually believe about themselves.

The person who received the impact gets something cheap and valuable too: the chance to be generous about intent. "I know you weren't trying to put me down" is a gift. It tells the other person their character isn't in the dock, which, as chapter 3 showed, makes it much easier for them to look at what happened.

> **Say it**
>
> When you've been hurt by someone who probably didn't mean it: "I don't think you meant it like that. It still landed badly, and I'd like you to know how."
>
> When you've caused an impact you didn't intend: "I didn't mean it that way, and I believe you that it hurt. Both of those are true. I'm sorry for how it landed."
>
> When you're not sure what they meant: "When you said that, it came across as if you were putting me down. Was that what you meant?"

## One small thing to try

Before you send your next message that matters, read it aloud in a flat, neutral voice, like a station announcement. Any sentence that sounds harsher, colder or more sarcastic than you meant is a sentence the other person may hear that way. Then paste it into the *Chat analyzer* (spreadloveandacceptance.com/signal-translator), which reads a message for how it may land and offers a clearer version that keeps your point. The *Say it so it lands* workpaper (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-09) has a three-part shape for anything hard: the fact, how it felt, and what you're asking for.

## What to remember

- Intent belongs to the speaker; impact belongs to the listener. Each is the expert on their half.
- "I didn't mean it" is true about intent and doesn't settle impact. "You did it to hurt me" is true about impact and doesn't prove intent.
- Not meaning to hurt someone doesn't mean you had no part in it. You can own the impact without owning a motive you didn't have.
- Writing loses tone. Read important messages aloud, flatly, before you send them.
