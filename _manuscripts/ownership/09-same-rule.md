# 9. Your own part, by the same rule

*A Friday night, six weeks after the phone-call argument, Lorraine and Pete's house in Leicester (made up).*

Things have been better since Pete told his mum to ring him directly. So Lorraine is surprised when he comes off a call with his mother looking tight around the mouth.

"Mum said you told her, last week, 'Well, you know what he's like.' When she asked why I hadn't called."

Lorraine remembers saying it. It had felt like a harmless, slightly weary aside, the kind of thing you say about someone you love.

"Oh," she says. "I'm sorry you took it that way. I didn't mean anything by it."

There's a pause. Pete raises his eyebrows, very slightly. Lorraine hears what she's just said, and goes red.

## The sentence came out of the other mouth.

In the second it takes her to go red, Lorraine sees all of it at once. A throwaway line, repeated by his mother. Under it, a small disloyalty: talking about Pete's failings with his mum, the very go-between role she'd said she didn't want. Under that, her reflex, "sorry you took it that way", the exact deflection she'd found so maddening from him six weeks ago. And under that, the reason the reflex fires in everyone: being on the receiving end of a complaint feels completely different from making one, and rules that look so clear when we apply them to other people go blurry when they're turned on us. A bookkeeper would call it a question of balance. A standard only counts as fair if it weighs the same on both sides.

This chapter is the one the rest of the book depends on. Everything so far has been about how to help someone else own their part. All of it applies, word for word, to you. If the scripts only ever point outward, they stop being tools for understanding and become tools for winning, and the other person will feel the difference within a sentence.

## A fair rule binds the people who make it.

Politics, at its simplest, is the study of how a group makes and keeps its rules: who decides, how, and what makes a rule feel legitimate to the people who live under it. One answer comes up again and again, across very different societies. A rule feels fair when it applies to the people who made it as well as everyone else. The moment those in charge exempt themselves, people stop treating the rule as a rule. They start treating it as an instruction from someone more powerful, and they obey it only while they're being watched.

A household is a very small political system, and so is a friendship. "We tell each other when something hurts" is a rule. "When one of us is told, we own our part without the 'but'" is a rule. If one person holds the other to those rules and quietly exempts themselves, the rules stop working, however good they are. They turn into a preference wearing a uniform.

So the most persuasive thing you can do, if you want someone to own their part more often, is to own yours visibly and promptly, including the small things, including the things you'd rather not.

## The double standard is easy to miss in yourself.

Nobody sets out to apply one rule to their partner and a softer one to themselves. It happens because of how we see.

> **From the research.** Emily Pronin and colleagues found that people readily see biases in other people's judgements and much less readily in their own. In one study, participants judged a fellow student's self-serving explanation of a test result as biased, and their own very similar explanation as accurate. Even when told how a bias works, people tended to insist that their own view was objective.
> *Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002) · Strong: three studies, and a large replication in 2021 found the same pattern.*

> **From the research.** In a classic set of studies, people working on something jointly, including married couples, tended to accept more responsibility for it than their partners gave them, because their own contributions came to mind more easily than the other person's.
> *Ross and Sicoly (1979) · Strong: an often-repeated finding, though the popular claim that couples' shares "add up to more than 100%" couldn't be confirmed from the original paper, so it isn't repeated here.*

Put those together and you get the everyday double standard. Your own reasons are vivid to you; theirs are a blur. Your effort is obvious to you; theirs is half invisible. Your "you know what he's like" was affectionate, because you know what you meant; their "sorry you feel that way" was a dodge, because you only saw the effect.

One correction belongs here, because it's the kind of thing popular psychology tends to overstate.

> **From the research.** For decades, textbooks taught that people explain their own behaviour by circumstances and other people's by character. When Bertram Malle pulled together more than a hundred studies of that idea, the pattern turned out to be close to zero on average. Other self-serving tendencies, like the ones above, remain well supported.
> *Malle (2006) · Strong: a large meta-analysis, which is a useful reminder that a famous finding can be weaker than its reputation.*

So the double standard is no iron law of the mind. It's a tendency, and tendencies can be checked.

## The mirror question works on you first.

There's a question that does more to expose a double standard than any amount of argument: *If I'd done the same thing to you, what would you expect me to own?*

You can ask it of the other person. If they would clearly expect an apology from you for the very thing they won't apologise for, the conversation changes. They may see it and laugh, a bit sheepishly. Or, if they expect accountability from you and still won't offer it themselves, you've learned something important: the problem may be less about understanding and more about unwillingness (chapter 8).

But the question is most useful turned on yourself first, quietly, before the conversation even starts. *If Pete had said "you know what she's like" to my mother, what would I want him to say?* Lorraine knows the answer at once. She'd want him to say, "I shouldn't have said that to your mum. It made you look bad to her, and that's not my place."

So that's what she says, about two minutes after "sorry you took it that way".

| Said | "I'm sorry you took it that way. I didn't mean anything by it." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *Your reaction is the problem, the same as always.* |
| Meant | *I'm embarrassed, and I don't want this to be a big deal.* |
| A better way | "No, scrap that. That's exactly what I can't stand when you do it. I said 'you know what he's like' to your mum. It made you look bad to her, and it's the very thing I said I didn't want to be part of. I'm sorry. I won't discuss you with her again." |

Pete's shoulders come down. And six weeks of Lorraine asking Pete to own his part suddenly look fair, because she's just shown him she'll do the same.

## Taking all the blame isn't owning your part.

There's an opposite mistake, and kind, conflict-averse people make it all the time. To end the argument, they take the whole thing on: "No, you're right, it's all my fault, I'm sorry, let's forget it." It feels generous. It ends the evening.

It also leaves the other person's part untouched, which means the same thing will happen again. It quietly teaches both of you that the way through a disagreement is for one person to absorb it. And it isn't quite true, which the other person usually senses, so the apology doesn't even land as well as it should.

Owning your part means the part that's actually yours: no bigger, no smaller. That can be less comfortable than taking all of it, because it means you still have to raise their part afterwards. But it's the only version that leaves both of you with the true picture.

## When you don't owe an apology.

Sometimes you look, carefully, and find that you don't owe one. Or you owe one for a much smaller thing than the one you're being asked to apologise for. That happens, and the same standard that asks you to own your part also protects you from owning what isn't yours.

People get pressed into false apologies all the time, usually to end a row. It feels like peace-making. It costs more than it seems to. It teaches the other person that pressure works, it leaves the real issue unexplored, and it can leave you carrying a resentment that comes out later, sideways.

You can decline kindly, and you can do it without denying the other person's hurt. "I can see you're really upset, and I'm sorry you're hurting. I've thought about it, and I don't think I did what you're describing. I'd like to understand why it looked that way to you. Can we go through it?" That keeps the door open and keeps the feeling respected. It also keeps the truth intact, which matters more in the long run than how the evening ends.

If you're not sure whether you owe one, it's fine to say so, and to ask for time: "I'm not sure yet. Let me think about it properly, and I'll come back to you tomorrow." Then come back, whichever way you've decided.

## What counts as your part.

Owning your part doesn't mean taking all of the blame to end the argument. That's its own kind of dodge, and it leaves the other person's part untouched. It means finding the bits that were yours, however small, and owning those fully.

Your part might be the thing you did. It might also be:

- **How you raised it.** Saving it up for weeks and delivering it all at once. Raising it when they were walking in the door. Starting with "you always".
- **The guess you offered as a fact.** "You did it on purpose." "You don't care."
- **The hint you expected to be understood.** "Fine." The banging pans. The silence that you wanted noticed and that they took at face value.
- **Your reaction, where it went further than the event.** The feeling is yours to have. What you did with it may be another matter: the door, the tone, the thing said to their mother.

Karin, from chapter 4, found two of these when she looked. She'd said nothing on the night of her fortieth, smiled through the cake, and then sent "Honestly, Thursday really hurt" two days later, which Nell read at a supermarket checkout and spent the afternoon dreading. And her Monday message, "It's obvious our friendship isn't a priority for you anymore", was a guess presented as a fact. When Nell came round on the Wednesday with her full apology, Karin added one of her own, "I should have said something on the night, instead of letting it stew and then dropping it on you by text. And I said you don't care about our friendship. I don't actually think that. I was hurt."

It took her about twenty seconds. It made the rest of the evening twice as easy, because Nell no longer felt like the only person in the room who'd got something wrong.

Fran found one too, about the barbecue in chapter 6. When Dele corrected the year, she didn't say anything. She caught his eye across the garden, gave him a look that would have stripped paint, and was cool with him for the rest of the afternoon, waiting for him to work out why. He knew the look meant something, and not what, because a look is a message he finds very hard to read, so he spent three hours feeling punished for something he couldn't name. That evening, before she asked him her question, she said: "Before anything else, I owe you one. I went cold on you all afternoon and expected you to guess why. You'd have stopped in a second if I'd just told you. That wasn't fair." Dele, who had been bracing for a row, looked so relieved that she nearly laughed.

Hinting is a perfectly normal way of communicating, and for a lot of people it's the polite one. But when the other person's wiring doesn't pick up hints easily, a hint that isn't received and then a coldness that follows can feel, from their side, like being blamed for failing a test nobody told them they were sitting. Owning that part is no admission that you were wrong to be hurt. It's an admission that the message never arrived.

None of these cancels what they did. Chapter 7 still holds: two people can both be responsible for different things. But owning yours, first and plainly, is the fastest way to make it safe for them to own theirs.

> **Two ways to read a rule**
>
> Some people's wiring applies a rule the same way every time. If "we don't discuss each other with family" is a rule, it's a rule on Friday as much as on Monday, for you as much as for me. People wired like this notice inconsistencies quickly and say so plainly, and are often told they're being rigid, or keeping score.
>
> Other people's wiring treats rules as guides that bend with context. A throwaway line to a mother-in-law, said fondly, feels different from a complaint, and holding it to the same standard feels pedantic, even a little unkind.
>
> **If you apply rules exactly,** your sense of fairness is a real strength in a relationship, and it lands better when it's pointed at yourself first. "I said I'd do X and I didn't" is the kind of consistency that makes other people trust your consistency about them.
>
> **If you flex rules by context,** it can help to know that for some people the flex itself feels like unfairness: a rule that changes depending on who broke it. Naming the flex out loud helps, "I know I said we don't do this. I did it, and here's why it felt different at the time. Does that seem fair to you, or is it one rule for me?"

> **Say it**
>
> When you catch yourself deflecting: "Hang on. That's exactly what I don't like when you do it. Let me try again."
>
> The mirror question, asked kindly: "If I'd done the same thing to you, what would you want me to say?"
>
> Owning your part before theirs: "Before we get to the bigger thing, I want to own my bit. I raised it badly, and I saved it up. That was mine."

The *Check-ins* page on the website suggests a weekly talk where each person raises one thing, and each person owns one thing (spreadloveandacceptance.com/check-ins). Building the second half into the habit is the simplest way to keep one rule for both.

## One small thing to try

Before your next difficult conversation, ask yourself the mirror question in private: *If they'd done exactly this to me, what would I want them to own?* Write the answer down. Then look for anything in your own recent behaviour that the same answer would apply to. If you find something, own it first, before you raise theirs.

## What to remember

- Everything in this book applies to the reader. Scripts that only point outward turn into tools for winning.
- A rule only feels fair when it binds the person who makes it.
- We see bias in others far more easily than in ourselves. The mirror question is the quickest check, and it works best on yourself first.
- Owning your part means finding the bits that were yours and owning those fully, without taking all the blame to make it stop.
