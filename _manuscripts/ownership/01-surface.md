# 1. The surface and the fuller picture

*Tuesday, twenty to seven, a terraced house in Leicester (made up, like every scene in this book).*

Lorraine has her coat half off when she asks whether Pete rang his mum back.

"I'll do it later," he says, from the sofa, laptop open.

"Fine."

He takes her at her word and carries on with the spreadsheet he's been fighting since four. Twenty minutes later she is banging pans in a way that he can hear is about him, and he has no idea why. He asked. She said fine. When he goes in and asks what's wrong, she says, "Nothing, it's fine, honestly," and puts the colander down a little harder than a colander needs.

By nine they're arguing, and the argument is about the phone call, which neither of them actually cares about very much.

## A moment has a surface and a depth.

Take that scene in a few quick strokes, the way it arrives once you've learned to look for the layers.

On the surface, a question about a phone call and a one-word answer. One layer down, it's the third time this week his mother has rung, and the third "later". Below that, when Pete doesn't ring back, his mother rings Lorraine instead, to ask if he's all right, and Lorraine has been taking those calls for a month without anyone agreeing she would. Below that, two things no camera could film: Lorraine feels like the household switchboard, and Pete feels nagged about a conversation with his mother that he's been dreading for reasons he hasn't said out loud. And at the root, an old pattern that turns up in offices and families alike: a job that nobody owns lands on whoever notices first.

That took a few seconds to see. It will take the rest of this chapter to go through slowly, because each layer is doing something different.

**The surface** is what a camera would catch: the words, the tone, the last thirty seconds. "I'll do it later." "Fine." Most arguments are conducted entirely at this layer, which is why they so often feel like arguing about nothing. On the surface, nothing much has happened.

**The pattern** is the surface repeated. One "later" is a busy evening. Three in a week, about the same call, is a shape. Lorraine can see the shape because she's been counting, without meaning to, every time her own phone lights up with his mother's name. Pete can't see it, because to him each "later" was its own reasonable evening.

**The cost** is who pays for the pattern and how. Here it's Lorraine, paying in small calls and a vague, rising feeling that she's managing something that isn't hers. Costs like this rarely get mentioned, because they arrive in instalments too small to complain about.

**The meaning** is what each person takes the moment to say about the two of them. To Lorraine, "later" has started to mean "you'll handle it". To Pete, her question has started to mean "you're failing at something again". Neither of them has said either sentence. Both of them are reacting to it.

**The root** is the thing underneath that would still be there if you changed the people: an unowned job, two different speeds, an old hurt, a body running on empty. Roots matter because they keep growing new surfaces. Solve tonight's phone call and next week the same root will push up a different argument, probably about the car.

## Two people can be accurate about the same minute and still disagree.

When the argument finally happens, Pete says, reasonably, "I said I'd ring her later. It's half seven. Later hasn't happened yet." Every word of that is true.

Lorraine says, "You always do this. You leave it and I end up dealing with her." Every word of that is true too, at a different depth. But "always" sounds like an exaggeration from where Pete is standing, so he corrects it ("I rang her on Sunday"), and now they're arguing about Sunday.

This is the most common way a conversation about an apology breaks down. Two people are answering two different questions, each accurately, and each one hears the other's accurate answer as a dodge. Pete is answering *what happened this evening?* Lorraine is asking *what has been happening?* He thinks she's blowing up a small thing. She thinks he's shrinking a big one. They're both right about the layer they're on.

> **From the research.** When Roy Baumeister and colleagues asked people to write about a time someone angered them and a time they angered someone else, the two kinds of story came out differently. The person who caused the hurt tended to describe a single, closed incident with reasons that made sense. The person who was hurt tended to describe something that had been building, with lasting effects, and the other person's behaviour as harder to understand. Each side then sees the other's account as distorted.
> *Baumeister, Stillwell and Wotman (1990) · Moderate: people's own written stories, which show how we remember rather than what happened; the pattern has been found again in later work.*

The finding is useful because it takes the blame out of the gap. Pete isn't lying when he says it was one evening, and Lorraine isn't exaggerating when she says it's been weeks. They're standing in different seats. From the seat of the person who did the thing, you see your reasons and this one moment. From the seat of the person it was done to, you see the effect and every time before.

## The surface wins arguments, and loses the point.

There's a reason the person standing on the surface tends to win these arguments on points. The surface can be checked. "I said later, and it's half seven" is a fact anyone can verify. The lower layers can't be checked in the same way. "It's been weeks" is a pattern, and patterns are made of many small moments, each of which, taken on its own, can be explained away.

So the person who's carrying the pattern does what people do when they can feel something real that they can't quite prove. They reach for bigger words. "Always." "Every single time." "You never." These words are an attempt to make the lower layer visible, to say *this is bigger than tonight*. And they're a gift to the person on the surface, because "always" is almost never literally true, and one counter-example ("I rang her on Sunday") is enough to make the whole complaint look exaggerated.

This is how a real grievance loses an argument. The facts were on the hurt person's side the whole time; they were just spread across a month instead of sitting in one evening. If you're carrying a pattern, the fix is to swap the big words for small, countable ones: three calls this week, four this month. If you're on the surface and hear an "always", the fix is to hear it as *this has happened more than you've noticed*, and ask how often, before you reach for Sunday.

## Sort the facts from the guesses.

Philosophy has a branch with a forbidding name, epistemology, which asks a plain question: how do you know what you think you know? It turns out to be one of the most practical tools for an argument at home, because in the middle of a row almost nothing being said is actually known. Most of it is memory and inference and pattern-matching, dressed up as fact and delivered with total confidence.

Try it on the scene. Some of what Lorraine and Pete each believe is fact, the kind a camera or a call log could check:

- His mother has rung three times this week.
- Lorraine has taken four calls from her this month.
- Pete said "later" on Tuesday, and on Thursday, and tonight.

And some of it is guesswork, however likely it feels:

- "He doesn't care how much this is costing me."
- "She thinks I'm useless with my family."
- "He's avoiding his mum because he's scared of the conversation about her moving."
- "She's in a mood about something else and taking it out on the phone call."

The third guess might be right, and it might even be kind. It's still a guess. When two people argue their guesses as if they were facts, the other person can always find a hole, because a guess about someone's insides will never be quite what's in there. When they put the facts on the table first and offer the guesses as questions, the other person has something they can agree with before they get to the part they'll dispute.

That sorting is the first move of almost every chapter in this book. What happened, in words a camera would accept? What do I think it meant? Which of those am I sure of?

## The gap between layers is where apologies go missing.

This chapter comes first for a reason. When someone wants an apology, they nearly always want it for something in the lower layers: the pattern, the cost, the meaning. When someone offers one, they nearly always offer it for the surface.

So Pete, an hour later and wanting the evening back, says, "Sorry I didn't ring her. I'll do it now." And he does, there and then, in the hall. It's a real apology for a real thing, and Lorraine still feels as though nothing has been said, because nothing has been said about the month of calls she's been taking. Pete, having apologised and acted on it, now feels that nothing he does is ever enough.

Neither of them is being unreasonable. The apology was for the top layer, and the hurt was two layers down.

| Said | "Sorry I didn't ring her. I'll do it now." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *I'll make tonight's problem go away so we can stop talking about it.* |
| Meant | *I didn't realise this was such a big deal, and I want us to be all right.* |
| A better way | "I've been leaving Mum to you, haven't I? Every time I say later, she rings you. That's not fair on you. I'll ring her now, and from now on I'll tell her to call me instead of you." |

The better version isn't longer because longer is better. It's longer because it reaches the layer where the hurt actually is: the pattern ("every time"), the cost ("she rings you") and a fix that changes the setup rather than tonight.

The same gap opens at work, often in writing. Dawn, who manages a small team at a housing association, emails Bryn on a Friday afternoon, "As per my email on Tuesday, can you send the figures by Monday?" Bryn reads it on his phone at the bus stop and feels his face go hot.

What Bryn hears is *you ignored me, and now I'm putting it in writing.* What Dawn meant was that she needs the figures for a board paper, she's anxious about Monday, and she can't remember whether she was clear on Tuesday. Three words ("as per my") have, in a lot of offices, come to carry a sharp edge, and they were sitting on top of a worry that never made it into the email. A version that would have carried better is barely longer: "Hi Bryn, I need the figures for the board paper on Tuesday. Could you get them to me by Monday lunchtime? I'm not sure I made the deadline clear last time, so flagging it now." Nobody meant anything unkind, and Bryn spends his weekend feeling told off. (Chapter 6 has what he does on Monday.)

## Seeing the layers is a skill, and it can be learned.

Some people see the lower layers of a moment almost instantly. Others see the surface clearly and the depths only when someone points to them. Neither is better. A person who reads every layer at once can be exhausting to argue with, because they're three layers down while you're still on the words, and from your side it looks as if they're making a mountain out of a phone call. A person who stays on the surface can seem careless, when really they're being literal about the thing in front of them.

On the website's About page I take an eight-word sentence apart: "don't worry about it, I'll sort it out." Said kindly in passing, it's reassurance. Said for the third time in a month, about the same job, it also means a job with no owner and a decision put off again. Both of those readings are in the sentence. Most people take a sentence in as one impression, and they're not wrong to. Taken as parts, it shows you where the parts and the impression disagree. That disagreement is where this whole book lives.

You don't need to see everything at once. You need a habit of asking, after the surface, "and underneath that?" two or three times. The answers usually come quicker than you'd expect, and they're usually less dramatic than you feared.

> **Two ways to read "Fine."**
>
> Some people's wiring takes a word at its word. "Fine" means fine. If it didn't, surely the person would have said so; that's what words are for. To a brain that works like this, Lorraine's "fine" was information, and Pete acted on it correctly. The banging pans came out of nowhere.
>
> Other people's wiring hears the tune more than the words. A short "fine", in a flat tone, after a pause, is a door left ajar: *I'm not going to say it now, and I'd like you to notice.* To a brain that works like this, Pete ignored something obvious.
>
> **If your brain works like Lorraine's,** the word you say may be the only thing that arrives. Try saying the layer: "It isn't fine yet. Can we talk about your mum's calls after tea?" Saying it plainly sends the message on a channel the other person actually receives, and it costs you nothing.
>
> **If your brain works like Pete's,** a flat, quick "fine" is often a sign there's something under it. You don't have to guess what. You can ask: "Fine as in fine, or fine as in later?" Most people will tell you, and some will laugh, which helps.
>
> Neither way of hearing is the correct one. They're different settings, and two people on different settings each find the other hard to read. The root underneath is one that will keep turning up in this book: what's sent isn't always what arrives.

> **From the research.** The idea that misunderstanding runs both ways between people who are wired differently has a name: the double empathy problem. It began as a short argument by Damian Milton that the difficulty sits between two kinds of mind rather than inside one of them. Since then, an experiment passing a story along chains of people found that chains of autistic people and chains of non-autistic people each passed the story on about equally well, while mixed chains lost detail faster and reported less rapport.
> *Milton (2012) · Theory: an argument without data of its own. Crompton and colleagues (2020) · Early: one small lab task with 72 people; a replication has since been published.*

## What helps when you're stuck on different layers.

Name the layer you're on, out loud. It sounds clumsy, and it works far better than it should. "I think you're talking about tonight, and I'm talking about the last month. Can I do mine first, and then I'll hear yours?" Most people relax the moment they realise they aren't being accused of something they don't recognise.

Agree the facts before the meanings. Start with what a camera could check: three calls, four calls, "later" on Tuesday. If you can agree those, you've agreed something, and that changes the temperature in the room. Then offer your reading as a reading: "What it's started to feel like to me is…"

Ask about their layer before defending yours. "What does it look like from where you're sitting?" is the most underused sentence in any argument. You'll often find their lower layers are just as real as yours, and they've been carrying them just as quietly.

Look for the root before you fix the surface. If the root is an unowned job, the fix is ownership: Pete tells his mother to ring him, and the job has an owner again. If the root is two different speeds, the fix is a time to talk. If the root is a tired body, the fix might be bed, and the conversation tomorrow.

The site's *The logic behind it* page has a short version of this habit: describe the moment in "camera words" first, with no "rude" and no "weird", then walk the logic one step at a time (spreadloveandacceptance.com/the-logic-behind-it). The *Chat analyzer* does a version for messages: paste in a text you've had, or one you're about to send, and it shows how it might land and where it turned (spreadloveandacceptance.com/signal-translator).

> **Say it**
>
> "I think we're on different layers of this. You're talking about tonight, and you're right about tonight. I'm talking about the last few weeks. Can we put the facts down first and then each say what it's felt like?"
>
> Or, if you're the one being told you've missed something: "I can tell there's more to this than the phone call. What am I not seeing?"

## One small thing to try

Next time an argument seems to be about nothing, write it down afterwards in three lines. First line: what a camera would have seen, in plain words. Second line: one layer down, what's been happening, with any numbers you actually know. Third line: one more layer, what you think it means, written as a guess. Read it back the next day. Notice which line the argument was really about.

## What to remember

- Every moment has a surface and a fuller picture underneath, and two people can be standing at different depths of the same minute.
- The person who did something and the person it was done to usually see different stories, and both stories have real things in them.
- Sort what a camera could check from what you've inferred. Agree the first before you argue the second.
- Most missing apologies are apologies made to the wrong layer.
