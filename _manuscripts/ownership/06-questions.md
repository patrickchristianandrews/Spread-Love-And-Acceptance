# 6. Questions that open a door

*A Monday evening, a few weeks after the engagement dinner. Fran and Dele's flat in Cardiff. Made up.*

The taxi-fare apology went well, in the end. But a fortnight later it happens again, smaller: at a friend's barbecue, Dele corrects the year Fran says she started her job. She catches his eye across the garden and he looks puzzled, then, a second later, stricken.

This time, instead of telling him what he did, Fran tries asking. On the sofa that evening she says, "Can I ask you something about the barbecue? No rush to answer. When I said 2019 and you said 2018, what was going on for you?"

Dele is quiet for a long time. Then he says, "I don't know. I'd like to think about it. Can I tell you tomorrow?"

Fran's first instinct is to hear that as a dodge. She says, "OK," and means it, mostly.

At breakfast the next morning, Dele says, "I think when someone says something that isn't right, it's like a picture hanging crooked. I fix it before I've thought about whether anyone else cares about the picture. I did it again. I'd like to stop doing it when you're telling a story. Would it help if I wrote it down and told you after?"

## Telling invites a defence. Asking invites a look.

Quickly: on the surface, a question about a year. One layer down, the same habit as the curry night, now with a name on it. Below that, a difference in the order Fran and Dele need things: she wants an answer now, to know they're all right; he needs time before he can say anything he's sure is true. Below that, the realisation that his "I don't know" was the most honest answer in the room. And at the root, something that applies to every kind of communication, from a thermostat to a choir: a loop works when each side leaves room for the other's signal to come back.

Now slowly.

When you tell someone what they did wrong, their mind does what minds do with incoming criticism: it checks the claim against its own version and looks for the places that don't match. There are always some. Your account is from your seat; theirs is from theirs. So they correct the details, and you defend your account, and before long you're arguing about whose version of the barbecue is right.

When you ask, something different happens. The other person has to go and look at the moment themselves. What they find there is theirs. People hold on to conclusions they reach themselves far more firmly than conclusions handed to them, and a person who has looked for themselves usually finds more than you'd have dared to say.

## Asking beats imagining.

There's a common piece of advice in relationships: try to see it from their point of view. It's well meant. The research suggests it doesn't work nearly as well as people think.

> **From the research.** In 25 experiments, Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Nicholas Epley asked people to predict what another person was thinking or feeling. Telling them to imagine the other person's perspective didn't make their guesses more accurate, and sometimes made them more confident without being any more correct. What did improve accuracy was getting the other person's perspective directly, by asking them and listening.
> *Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018) · Moderate: many experiments with consistent results; mostly lab tasks with strangers, which aren't the same as close relationships.*

That's the case for questions in one sentence. You can spend an hour imagining what Dele was thinking at the barbecue, and you'll mostly produce a more confident version of your own guess. Or you can ask, and find out about the crooked picture.

## Questions that open a door.

These are the kind of questions that tend to help someone see their own part. They're open, they're about one specific moment, and they leave room.

- "What part of what happened do you think was yours?"
- "If you could replay that moment, what would you do differently?"
- "Can you see why that hurt me, even though hurting me wasn't what you meant?"
- "What was going on for you right before it happened?"
- "What do you think it looked like from where I was standing?"

And one that belongs to a later chapter but is worth trying now: "If I'd done the same thing to you, what would you want me to say?" Chapter 9 is about why that question is so revealing, for both people.

None of these is a trick. Each one only works if you actually want the answer, including an answer you weren't expecting.

## Questions that close a door.

Some sentences are shaped like questions and work like accusations.

**The leading question.** "Don't you think that was a bit selfish?" There's only one answer you'll accept, and both of you know it.

**The courtroom question.** "So you admit you knew I'd hate it?" This turns any honest reflection into evidence for the prosecution, and teaches the other person that thinking out loud is dangerous.

**The rhetorical question.** "Do you have any idea how that felt?" It isn't asking. It's telling, at volume.

**The why question, asked hot.** "Why would you do that?" can be a real question, but in the middle of a row it usually sounds like "there is no acceptable reason for what you did". "What was going on for you when…" asks the same thing without the charge.

If someone has had a lot of courtroom questions over the years, they may treat every question as one. It can take a few rounds of genuinely open questions, and genuinely listening to the answers, before they believe you.

## Music calls it call and response.

In a lot of music, from work songs and gospel to folk and jazz, one voice sings a phrase and another answers it. The first phrase is the call. What matters, and what beginners get wrong, is that the call leaves room. It stops. There's a rest, a beat of silence, and the response comes into that space. If the first singer barrels straight on, the response has nowhere to go, and you get two people singing over each other.

A question is a call. The silence after it is the rest in the bar, and it's part of the music, not a gap in it. Most of us are bad at rests. We ask a question, and two seconds into the silence we fill it: we rephrase it, or answer it ourselves, or add a second question, or explain why we asked. The other person, who had just started to look inward, has to come back out and deal with what we've added.

So ask, and then stop. Count, silently, if it helps. The answer that comes after five or ten seconds of quiet is usually more honest than the one that would have come after two.

The same pattern runs through anything that corrects itself over time: a thermostat checks the room and adjusts, a choir listens and retunes. Each answer you get shapes the next question you ask. That's a feedback loop, and it only works if the signal is allowed to come back.

> **Two ways to read this: "I don't know. Can I tell you tomorrow?"**
>
> Some people's wiring needs processing time before it can answer a question about their own behaviour. The honest answer isn't available yet. It's being assembled, slowly and carefully, and anything they said right now would be a guess, or whatever would end the conversation fastest. For a brain like this, "I don't know yet" is the truthful answer, and "let me think" is a promise to come back with a real one.
>
> Other people's wiring answers on the spot and expects the same. A pause, or "I don't know", feels like a dodge: a way of not answering, buying time until the other person drops it.
>
> **If you need time,** say so in a way that makes the return visible: "I don't know yet. I want to give you a real answer. Can I tell you tomorrow at breakfast?" Then come back, without being asked. That return is what turns "I don't know" from a dodge into a promise.
>
> **If you answer fast,** try treating "I don't know yet" as an appointment rather than a refusal. Let it go for now, and notice whether they come back. If they do, you'll often get the best answer of the whole conversation. If they reliably don't, that's a pattern, and it's worth talking about on its own (chapter 10).

> **From the research.** Accounts from autistic people often describe needing more time to process a conversation, especially an emotional one, and a sense that attention tends to run deep on one thing at a time rather than spread across several at once. Researchers have called this pattern of attention "monotropism".
> *Murray, Lesser and Lawson (2005) · Theory: an influential account, drawing on research and the authors' own experience, not a test of it. Experiences vary a great deal from person to person.*

## What to do with the answer.

When someone gives you an honest answer, even a partial one, the next thing you do decides whether they'll ever answer honestly again.

Don't pounce. If Dele had said "I suppose I do correct you quite a lot," and Fran had said "Exactly! So you admit it!", she'd have won the point and lost the conversation. People learn very fast whether reflection is safe. Punish it once, and next time you get the defence instead.

Instead, say back what you heard, and leave room for more. "So it's like a crooked picture. You fix it before you've thought about whether it matters to anyone. That makes sense of a lot, actually." That tells him the answer arrived, and it invites the next one.

**Said:** "Why do you always have to correct me?"
**Heard:** *There's something wrong with you and I'm sick of it.*
**Meant:** *I want to understand why this keeps happening, because I don't think you mean to hurt me.*
**A better way:** "Can I ask you about the barbecue? No rush. When you corrected the year, what was going on for you?" Then stop, and wait.

> **Say it**
>
> "Can I ask you something about yesterday? You don't have to answer straight away."
>
> "What part of that do you think was yours?"
>
> "If you could do that moment again, what would you change?"
>
> "I don't need an answer tonight. Would tomorrow work?"
>
> And when the answer comes: "Thank you. That helps me understand it."

The website's *Rewind the talk* tool walks through a conversation that went wrong one step at a time, including a step for seeing the other person's side and a practice script for trying again (spreadloveandacceptance.com/carrier-wave-decoder). Its step on "their side" works best combined with this chapter: imagine first if you must, then go and ask.

## One small thing to try

Next time you're about to tell someone what they did, turn it into one open question about a specific moment. Ask it, then say nothing at all for ten seconds, counted silently. If they say "I don't know", say "That's fine. Will you tell me when you do?" and leave it there.

## What to remember

- Telling someone what they did invites them to defend their version. Asking invites them to look.
- Imagining someone's point of view mostly makes you more confident in your own guess. Asking them is what actually improves understanding.
- A question is a call; the silence after it is part of the answer. Leave the rest in the bar.
- "I don't know yet" can be the most honest answer available. Treat it as an appointment, and notice whether they keep it.
