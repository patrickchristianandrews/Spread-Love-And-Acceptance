# 11. A limit that doesn't steer them

*A Tuesday evening in March, a phone call between Norwich and a flat in Ipswich (made up).*

It's happened again. Euan's partner is expecting a baby, and they'd agreed to tell nobody until the twelve-week scan. They told Marion, because she's his mum and she was so happy, and asked her to keep it to herself for three more weeks. On Sunday, Kirsty rang to congratulate them.

Euan rings his mother. He's done the work from chapter 3: he keeps to the one act, he says clearly that he isn't calling her a bad mother. Marion says, "Well, I can't do anything right, can I?" He says that isn't what he said. She says, "You're making me feel like a criminal for being happy." He explains again. She cries. He finds himself apologising. Forty minutes later they are exactly where they started, and Euan can feel the shape of every other call like this one, stretching back about a decade.

The next morning, he writes something down.

## The loop has a shape.

Quickly. On the surface, one more broken confidence and one more circular call. One layer down, the same pattern, now with years of evidence: every attempt to talk about the act ends in a conversation about Marion's feelings. Below that, Euan has tried nearly everything in this book, carefully and kindly, and nothing has moved. Below that, he is starting to dread his own mother's calls, and to share less, and to feel guilty for both. And at the root, a question of state and setting: the same conversation, in the same circumstances, will keep producing the same result.

Now slowly.

There comes a point, after you've explained clearly, more than once, and tried the questions and the gentleness and the timing, when the next step isn't another explanation. It's deciding what *you* will do when ownership doesn't come. Not to punish. Not to force it. Just to stop taking part in a loop that's hurting you both.

## The limit is about you, not about them.

This is the line that matters most. A limit says what you will and won't keep doing. It doesn't say what the other person must do, think, admit or feel.

"You have to admit you were wrong" is not a limit. It's a demand, and it puts you back where you started: trying to make someone own something. They can't be made to, and the demand usually makes them dig in.

"I'm not going to keep going round this in circles" is a limit. It's about your participation. The other person stays entirely free. They can agree, disagree, be upset, come back later or never. You're just no longer going to spend forty minutes a call on a loop.

That difference is what lets a limit protect your dignity without trying to control the other person. It's the same principle as the website's care-is-not-control rule: a "no", a request or a limit given out of care or respect for the relationship isn't control. Control is trying to run what someone else thinks, feels or does. A limit is running what you do.

This is what Euan wrote down, and then said, calmly, on the next call.

> "Mum, I want to sort things out with you, and I'm always willing to hear your side. But I can't sort out something that keeps happening if we can't talk about what actually happened. I'm not going to keep arguing about whether I'm allowed to be upset. I'm going to stop when it goes round in circles, and we can come back to it when we're both ready to talk about our own parts. I'll tell you everyday things, like always. Big news, I'll tell Kirsty myself first."

Look at what's in it. A wish to resolve things. An offer to hear her. A clear description of the loop. Two things Euan will do: stop the circular conversation, and change what he shares and in what order. And nothing that requires Marion to change her mind about anything.

## A limit is not a punishment.

Check a limit before you use it, because a limit can turn into a weapon without anyone quite noticing. A few honest questions help.

- **Is it about my actions?** "I'll stop the conversation" is. "You'll stop talking to Kirsty" isn't.
- **Can I keep it calmly?** A limit you can only hold while furious tends to come out as a threat.
- **Does it leave a door open?** "We can come back to this when we're both ready" keeps the relationship going. "I'm done" doesn't.
- **Would I accept the same limit from them?** If Marion said, "I'm not going to keep going round in circles about this," would that feel fair? If yes, it's probably a limit. If it would feel like a punishment, it probably is one.

And a limit isn't a judgement on the other person's character. Euan doesn't need to have decided why his mother can't own it (shame, almost certainly, from chapter 8). He doesn't need a word for her. He needs a way of being her son that doesn't involve a forty-minute loop every few weeks.

## Start with the body.

Holistic practices start somewhere that most advice about difficult conversations skips: the body. The idea is simple. A limit said from a racing body comes out sharp, fast and loud, and sounds like an ultimatum even when the words are mild. The same limit said from a settled body sounds like information.

The field is a mixed one. Some of what goes under the name "holistic" is well studied and much is debated or unsupported. The plain parts, though, have some decent evidence behind them, and slow breathing is one of the plainest.

> **From the research.** A systematic review of slow breathing (fewer than ten breaths a minute) in healthy adults found it went with changes in heart-rate patterns linked to calm, and with people reporting feeling more relaxed and less anxious. The authors note that the mechanisms are still debated.
> *Zaccaro and colleagues (2018) · Early: a review of only 15 small studies of varying quality; promising, and far from settled.*

Before Euan made that call, he walked round the block twice and breathed out longer than he breathed in. It's not magic. It just gave his body a few minutes to come down, so that the sentence he'd written arrived at the volume he meant.

> **Two ways to read stating a limit.**
>
> Some people's wiring states a limit plainly, as information. "I'm going to stop talking about this now, and we can try again tomorrow." To them it's a clear, kind sentence: no guessing, no games, a time to come back.
>
> Other people's wiring signals limits indirectly: a sigh, shorter answers, a change of subject, "anyway…". A plainly stated limit can sound cold or final to them, like a door shut in their face.
>
> **If you state limits plainly,** add one sentence of warmth and one of return. "I love you, and I'm going to stop here. We'll try again tomorrow after work." The warmth tells them it isn't a punishment; the time tells them it isn't the end.
>
> **If you signal limits indirectly,** it can help to know that for some people sighs and short answers don't arrive as a limit at all. They keep going because they genuinely haven't received the message. Saying it out loud can feel blunt to you, and will often be a relief to them: "I need to stop for tonight."

## A limit at work.

At work, limits tend to be quieter and more practical, and they work the same way. Think back to Gil and the templates in chapter 7, and imagine it had gone differently: every time Nadine raises the missed deadline, Gil agrees, and nothing changes, and the next conversation starts with the same agreement. After the third month, Nadine stops having the conversation.

Instead, she tells him something about what she'll do. "I'm going to send the report on the sixth with whatever sections are in. If yours isn't there, it'll go out with a line saying it's to follow. I'd much rather it was in, and I'm happy to talk about anything that would help."

She hasn't threatened him or gone over his head, and she hasn't demanded that he admit anything. She has changed what she does, said so in advance, kept the door open and left the choice with him. The report goes out on the sixth either way. Gil, as it happens, gets his section in on the fifth for the next four months running.

## What happens after the limit.

Sometimes a limit, held calmly, changes the pattern. Without a loop to run around, the other person has to deal with the actual issue or leave it. Some people, after a few rounds, start to come back to things on their own, especially when there's less heat and more room. Marion, a month later, rings Euan and says, without being asked, "I shouldn't have told Kirsty about the baby. I'm sorry, love." It's not a long apology. It's the first one in ten years.

Sometimes nothing changes. The other person keeps doing the thing, keeps avoiding it, and keeps being upset by the limit. That's painful, and it's also information. You can't do their inner work (chapter 8). You can decide how close you stay, what you share, and how much of your life goes into the loop. Those are your decisions, and you're allowed to make them kindly.

And sometimes, honestly, the limit shows you something about your own part. If you find yourself needing the same limit with lots of different people, ask yourself, privately and without beating yourself up, whether something in how you raise things is feeding the loops too. Chapter 9 again: the same rule for both.

| Said | "Fine. Forget it. I'm done talking about this." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *I'm punishing you by leaving.* |
| Meant | *I can't do another forty minutes of this, and I don't know how to stop it nicely.* |
| A better way | "I want to sort this out, and I don't want to keep going in circles. I'm going to stop for now. Can we try again at the weekend, and talk about our own parts?" |

> **Say it**
>
> The limit, calmly: "I want to resolve this with you, and I'm willing to hear your side. I can't resolve it if we can't agree on what happened. I'm not going to keep arguing about whether my feelings are allowed. We can come back to it when we're both ready to talk about our own parts."
>
> The shorter version: "We're going round in circles. I'm going to stop for now. Same time next week?"
>
> A limit about what you share: "I'll keep telling you everyday things. Big news, I'm going to tell people myself first from now on."

The website's *Kind ways to say no* page has scripts for refusing something without a long list of reasons, built on three kinds of "not now" (spreadloveandacceptance.com/refusals). The same three-step shape works for limits: say why the request or the relationship matters, say honestly what you have left, and offer something instead.

## One small thing to try

If you're in a loop with someone, write your limit down before the next conversation, in one paragraph. Check it against the four questions: about my actions, keepable calmly, door open, would I accept it from them. Then, before you say it, breathe out slowly a few times. Say it once. Don't explain it three times.

## What to remember

- After explaining clearly several times, the next step is deciding what you'll do, instead of trying harder to make them see.
- A limit describes what you'll keep doing or stop doing. It never requires the other person to admit, think or feel anything.
- Check that a limit isn't a punishment: about you, calm, door open, and fair if it were turned round.
- Say it from a settled body. The same words land completely differently at a lower volume.
