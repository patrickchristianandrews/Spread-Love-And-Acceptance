# 7. When it gets turned round on you

*A Thursday, just after eight in the morning, a tenement flat in Glasgow (made up).*

Tegan has been awake since ten past two, when the last of Joss's friends finally left. It's the third time this month he's had people round on a work night. She has a presentation at ten. She catches him in the kitchen, buttering toast.

"Joss, last night. It went on till two again. I've got this presentation and I've had about four hours."

"Sorry you feel that way," he says. "It wasn't that loud."

"It was loud enough that I heard every word of the argument about penalties. It's the third time this month."

Joss puts the knife down. "OK, well, since we're doing this. You leave your washing in the machine for days. You never buy loo roll. And your Ruairidh stayed here for a whole week in September and you didn't even ask me."

Tegan opens her mouth to defend the loo roll, and is late for the bus.

## The list arrives, and the first issue disappears under it.

There's a lot under the toast. A complaint about noise met with three complaints back. A man ambushed before breakfast, reaching for the nearest defence, which is attack. Some truth in what he said: Ruairidh did stay a week without anyone asking Joss, and it did bother him, and he never said so. A flat with no way of raising things, so that complaints get saved up until one person goes first and the other empties the drawer. And underneath, a problem of structure more than character: two separate issues put on one line, so that neither has an owner and neither can be fixed.

The counter-list is one of the most effective ways to make an issue vanish, and part of what makes it effective is that it's usually partly fair. If Joss had invented his complaints, Tegan could dismiss them. Because some of them are real, she has to deal with them, and the moment she does, the conversation about two o'clock in the morning is over. Each person ends up defending their own record instead of looking at what they did. Nobody apologises for anything, because apologising now would feel like losing.

> **From the research.** In John Gottman's long-running studies of couples discussing a disagreement, defensiveness, which includes meeting a complaint with a counter-complaint, was one of a small set of behaviours that predicted later unhappiness and separation. Couples who could hear a complaint without immediately returning one did markedly better.
> *Gottman and Levenson (1992); Gottman (1994) · Moderate: careful observational studies of married couples, mostly in the United States; the predictions are strong for groups and much less certain for any one couple.*

## Both of you can be responsible for different things.

One idea takes most of the heat out of this. Two people can both be in the wrong in the same week, about different things, and neither one cancels the other. Joss's late nights are one thing. Tegan's boyfriend staying a week is another. The washing machine and the loo roll are a third and fourth. Each one has its own owner and its own fix.

Accountability is not a competition with a winner. It isn't settled by working out who has done more wrong overall and then letting the bigger offender apologise. It's settled one item at a time.

What I got wrong doesn't wipe out what you got wrong, and what you got wrong doesn't wipe out mine.

## Audit work keeps every finding on its own line.

Audit work has a habit I've always valued. When you find a problem, you write it up as its own finding, with its own owner, its own fix and its own date for checking that the fix held. If you find another problem in the same place, it's a second finding. You don't merge them, and you certainly don't net them off against each other. A failure in one department doesn't cancel a failure in another. Two findings, two fixes.

The About page puts it more sharply: mixing up two different findings is how organisations keep solving the wrong problem. The same is true in a flat. If the late nights and the boyfriend's week go onto one line, the only possible outcome is a vague truce in which nobody changes anything. Kept on separate lines, each one has an owner and a fix. Joss can agree to a midnight cut-off on work nights. Tegan can agree to ask before anyone stays more than two nights. The washing and the loo roll might need a rota, or just a box by the door.

Running a home, or a friendship, isn't so different from running a team. Work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one. A complaint that's on everyone's list is on nobody's.

## Accountability isn't a scoreboard.

Underneath the counter-list there's usually a scoreboard. Somewhere in each person's head is a running total: what I've put up with, what you've got away with, who gave way last time. When Tegan raises the late nights, Joss's scoreboard lights up. He's been quietly counting Ruairidh's week since September, and now the score looks unfair: she's complaining about one thing, and he's been sitting on one of his own for a month.

Scoreboards feel like fairness. They aren't much use for it, because each person's scoreboard is kept by them, from their seat, with their own effort counted in full and the other person's half-remembered (chapter 9 has the research on that). Two fair-minded people with two scoreboards will reliably each find themselves behind.

The way out is to keep noticing what's fair, and to move it off the private scoreboard onto a shared list, one per line, where both people can see them and each one can be dealt with on its own. Joss's complaint about Ruairidh deserved a hearing in September. It was never going to get a fair one as ammunition in October.

## The same turn happens at work.

It isn't only flatmates. In an office, it often sounds more polite and works the same way.

Nadine, a project manager at an engineering firm in Derby, raises with her colleague Gil that he's missed the last three deadlines for his section of the monthly report. Gil listens, nods, and says, "Fair enough. Though to be honest, I'd have got it in on time if your templates weren't always late going out."

Nadine hears *this is your fault, really.* What Gil means is that he's embarrassed about the deadlines, and that there's a real problem with the templates which nobody has fixed. He could have said both, in the right order: "You're right, I've missed three. That's on me, and I'll get the next one in by the fifth. Separately, can we look at when the templates go out? I think it's making it harder for a few of us."

The template problem may be completely real. In the better version, it doesn't disappear. It gets its own line, its own conversation and, with luck, its own fix. What changes is that it stops being used to cancel the first issue.

And Nadine's better way, if the counter arrives anyway: "That's worth looking at, and I'll take it away. Can we agree the deadline first, and then talk about the templates?"

## Finish one issue first.

When the counter-list arrives, the instinct is to answer each item as it comes, and every answer pulls the conversation further from where it started. Try something different: acknowledge that the other items exist, promise to come back to them, and hold your ground on doing one at a time.

That isn't a way of dodging your own part. It only works if you mean it: if you really will come back to the washing, and really will own the parts that are yours. Meant, it's often a relief to the other person, because they've just learned that their complaints will get a proper hearing instead of being swatted away.

| Said | "OK, well, since we're doing this. You leave your washing in the machine for days." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *Your complaint doesn't count, because you're worse.* |
| Meant | *I feel ambushed, and I've been sitting on some things too.* |
| A better way (from Tegan, once she's caught her breath) | "That's fair. Some of those are real, especially Ruairidh staying without asking. I want to talk about all of them, and I'll own my part. Can we finish the late nights first? Then yours, properly, as its own conversation. Tonight after tea?" |

And if Joss can find it in himself, a better way from him: "Sorry. That came out as a counterattack. You're right about last night. Can I say the Ruairidh thing too, after we've sorted the noise? It's been bothering me."

> **Two ways to read a second topic**
>
> Some people's wiring holds several threads at once and sees how they connect. To a mind like this, the washing machine and the late nights genuinely are part of the same picture, which is how the two of you share the flat, and raising one while talking about the other isn't a comeback. It's the bigger view. Leaving a connected thread unsaid can feel like losing it, and some people worry, with reason, that if they don't say it now they'll forget it entirely.
>
> Other people's wiring takes one topic at a time, and hears any second topic as a counterattack: a way of changing the subject so the first one doesn't have to be answered.
>
> **If you see the connections,** you don't have to drop the thread, just park it visibly. "That's connected, and I want to come back to it. Can I write it down so neither of us loses it, and finish this one first?" Writing it down solves the forgetting, and asking first makes it clear you're not swapping topics.
>
> **If you work one thread at a time,** it can help to know that "and also the washing" may not be a deflection. Ask: "Is that connected, or is it a separate thing? Either way, can we write it down and come back to it?" If they agree, it was probably connection. If they can't let it go, it may be defence, and chapter 8 has more on why.

## When it goes round in circles, stop.

Sometimes, however carefully you do it, the conversation loops. You raise the issue, you get a deflection, you explain, you get the list, you separate the items, and somehow you're back at the list. A third time round, the same words come out with more heat and less hope.

At that point, the most useful thing you can do is stop trying to convince. Not because you've given up on the issue, and not as a punishment. A loop is a sign that one or both of you can't hear anything new right now, and every extra lap makes the next conversation harder. Say so plainly, and set a time to come back.

"I think we're going round in circles, and I don't want to keep doing that. Can we leave it for now and try again on Sunday, one thing at a time?"

Chapter 11 is about what to do if the loop turns out to be the pattern, not just the morning.

> **Say it**
>
> When the list arrives: "I'm willing to talk about what I've done wrong, and I'll own my part. Can we finish this one first? What I got wrong doesn't cancel what you got wrong, and the other way round."
>
> When you notice you've just done it: "Sorry, that came out as a comeback. You're right about this one. Can I raise mine after?"
>
> When it's going in circles: "We're going round again. I'd like to stop for now and come back to it on Sunday."

For a shared home, the *One owner per job* workpaper turns recurring jobs like the loo roll into named owners with a backstop, so they stop turning up in arguments about something else (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-03).

## One small thing to try

Next time you're in a disagreement where several things are flying about, take a piece of paper (or a note on your phone) and draw a line down the middle. Write each issue on its own line, with whose it is. Agree which one you're doing first. Cross nothing off until it has an owner and a fix. It looks a bit formal for a kitchen. It works far better than shouting about loo roll.

## What to remember

- The counter-list works because it's usually partly true. That's why it needs handling rather than dismissing.
- Two people can both be responsible for different things in the same conflict. One person's mistakes don't cancel the other's.
- Keep each issue on its own line, with its own owner and fix. Finish one before starting the next.
- If the conversation loops a third time, stop and set a time to come back.
