# Afterword: after the repair

*A Sunday in June, the care home in Harrogate again (made up).*

Lewis is in the car park at twenty past one. He's been there at twenty past one, or near enough, for six visits out of seven since they set up the leave-by alarm. The seventh he was ten minutes late, texted at 1:35, and walked in with the tea while Gregor was folding Dad's shirts.

Today, as they walk in together, Gregor says, "You're early. Are you ill?"

Lewis says, "I set the alarm for half twelve by mistake. I've been sitting here for forty minutes eating satsumas."

They're both still laughing when they get to their dad's room, and he asks what's so funny, and neither of them can quite explain.

## The laugh comes after.

There's a reason this book ends with a laugh, and a reason it comes last.

A shared laugh is one of the oldest ways people repair things after tension. Shoulders drop, breath comes back, and the room feels a little safer. People who study laughter and play describe it as a signal, mostly unspoken, that says *we're on the same side again.*

> **From the research.** In a study of 71 couples filmed talking about how they met, the share of the conversation in which both partners laughed at the same time went with feeling closer and more supported, and rating the relationship more highly, over and above how much each laughed on their own. Later work by the same researchers suggests shared laughter may help by making people feel more alike.
> *Kurtz and Algoe (2015) · Early: one observational study of couples, which shows laughter and closeness go together but can't show which causes which. Claims made for "laughter therapy" more widely run well ahead of the evidence.*

Look, though, at when Gregor and Lewis laugh. Not in April, in the car park, when Lewis arrived forty minutes late with the satsumas. A joke then would have been a way of skipping the hard part, and Gregor would rightly have felt brushed off. The laugh comes in June, after the apology, after the setup, after six visits that showed the change was real. It can only land because the repair is already done.

That's the rule for laughter in this book, and it's a simple one. Laugh *with*, never *at*. And laugh after, never instead.

## What this book was for.

If you take one thing from these pages, I'd like it to be the pair of sentences from the start, now with everything in between them.

Owning something you did is not the same as admitting you're a bad person. You can concede a point without conceding the round. You can own an impact you never intended. You can be sorry for the specific thing without agreeing with every reading of it. And you'll find it far easier to own things once you've stopped treating each one as a verdict on your whole self.

Saying sorry for someone's feelings is not the same as taking responsibility for your own actions. The feeling is theirs. The verb is yours. A good apology names the verb, keeps the weight on your side of the ledger, shows you can see how it landed, and then changes something they'll be able to see.

And underneath both: almost every moment has a surface and a fuller picture, and two people who care about each other can stand at different depths of the same minute without knowing it. Most of what goes wrong between people goes wrong in that gap. Most of what goes right starts with one of them asking, "What am I not seeing?"

# How the ideas connect

This book borrows ideas from thirteen fields. They look far apart. Underneath, they keep describing the same few patterns, called roots here. Learn a root once and you'll recognise it everywhere: a budget with no safety margin and a person with no spare energy fail for the same reason.

**The seven roots, in the middle**

- **Balance.** What flows in has to match what flows out, or something runs dry.
- **Signal and noise.** What's sent isn't always what arrives.
- **Feedback loops.** Small things repeated, with a check each time, shape the whole.
- **Limited capacity.** Everything has a limit, and a system with no spare room breaks on an ordinary bad day.
- **Ownership and structure.** Work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one.
- **Quiet incentives.** Things drift towards whatever the setup quietly rewards.
- **State and setting.** The same message lands differently depending on the state you're in and the place you're in.

**The thirteen fields, around them**

A bookkeeper's ledger, a choir's call and response and a nervous system on high alert are describing, in their own languages, the same few things that happen between two people at a kitchen table.

# Every script in one place

Rewrite any of these until they sound like you. The chapter number tells you where the thinking behind each one is.

**When you're on different layers (1)**
"I think we're on different layers of this. You're talking about tonight, and you're right about tonight. I'm talking about the last few weeks. Can we put the facts down first, and then each say what it's felt like?"
"I can tell there's more to this than the phone call. What am I not seeing?"

**When you hear "sorry you feel that way" (2)**
"Are you sorry I'm upset, or sorry about what happened? They're different, and I'd like to know which."
"I'm not asking you to be responsible for my feelings, and I'm not asking you to agree with every word. I'm asking you to own your part: the words or choices that were yours. And I want the same standard to apply to me."

**When you'd say "sorry you feel that way" and mean it (2)**
"I can see this really hurt, and I'm sorry about that. I don't understand yet what I did. Will you tell me, and give me a minute to think about it?"

**When shame is in the room (3)**
"I'm not saying you're a bad person. You're not. One thing hurt, and I'd like us to look at that one thing."
"You're right, I did that. It doesn't make me a terrible person, and it was still wrong of me. I'm sorry."
"I'm hearing this as 'you're awful', and I don't think that's what you said. Give me a minute and say it again?"

**Giving a genuine apology (4)**
"I did [the specific thing]. I can see it [the effect on you]. That was mine. I'm sorry. Next time I'll [one change]."
"Can I tell you what was going on? Not as an excuse. I just don't want you thinking I didn't care."
"I can see why it looked like that, and I gave you the reason to think it. Can I tell you what it was actually like for me?"

**Intent and impact (5)**
"I don't think you meant it like that. It still landed badly, and I'd like you to know how."
"I didn't mean it that way, and I believe you that it hurt. Both are true. I'm sorry for how it landed."
"When you said that, it came across as if you were putting me down. Was that what you meant?"

**Questions (6)**
"What part of that do you think was yours?"
"If you could do that moment again, what would you change?"
"Can you see why that hurt me, even though hurting me wasn't what you meant?"
"I don't need an answer tonight. Would tomorrow work?"

**When it's turned round on you (7)**
"I'm willing to talk about what I've done wrong, and I'll own my part. Can we finish this one first? What I got wrong doesn't cancel what you got wrong, and the other way round."
"Sorry, that came out as a comeback. You're right about this one. Can I raise mine after?"

**Shutdowns (8)**
"I can see you can't talk right now. That's OK. Come back when you can. I'll be here."
"I'm not walking out on this. I can't speak yet. Give me an hour."

**The same rule for both (9)**
"If I'd done the same thing to you, what would you want me to say?"
"Hang on. That's exactly what I don't like when you do it. Let me try again."

**When sorry has started to sound cheap (10)**
"I know you've heard sorry from me before. This time I want to change something you'll be able to see."
"I believe you mean it. I don't need another sorry. What can we change so it doesn't happen again?"

**A limit (11)**
"I want to resolve this with you, and I'm willing to hear your side. I can't resolve it if we can't agree on what happened. I'm not going to keep arguing about whether my feelings are allowed. We can come back to it when we're both ready to talk about our own parts."
"We're going round in circles. I'm going to stop for now. Same time next week?"

# The research behind this book

Every study here was checked on 20 October 2026 against the published record or a reliable summary of it. The label after each one says how much weight it can bear: **strong** (large reviews or consistent, repeated findings), **moderate** (good studies with limits), **early** (small or first studies), **mixed** (studies disagree), **debated** (the field itself is contested) and **theory** (an idea or argument rather than a test of one). A study is a pointer, never a verdict about you.

- **Baumeister, Stillwell and Wotman (1990).** Victim and perpetrator accounts of interpersonal conflict. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* People who caused a hurt described a single, understandable incident; people who were hurt described something ongoing with lasting effects. Moderate. Ch 1.
- **Breines and Chen (2012).** Self-compassion increases self-improvement motivation. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.* Self-compassion after a moral lapse went with more motivation to make amends and not repeat it. Moderate. Ch 3.
- **Chapman and Thomas (2006).** *The Five Languages of Apology.* Northfield. The basis of the apology-languages idea; popular and tested very little. Theory. Ch 4 (via the website).
- **Crompton and colleagues (2020).** Autistic peer-to-peer information transfer is highly effective. *Autism.* Mixed autistic and non-autistic chains lost more detail than chains of either alone. Early. Ch 1.
- **Downey and Feldman (1996).** Implications of rejection sensitivity for intimate relationships. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* People who anxiously expect rejection read it more readily into ambiguous behaviour. Moderate. Ch 3.
- **Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018).** Perspective mistaking. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* Imagining another's perspective didn't improve accuracy; asking them did. Moderate. Ch 6.
- **Fehr, Gelfand and Nag (2010).** The road to forgiveness. *Psychological Bulletin.* Across 175 studies, receiving an apology was one of the stronger correlates of forgiveness. Strong. Ch 4.
- **Frantz and Bennigson (2005).** Better late than early. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology.* Apologies worked better after the hurt person felt heard. Moderate. Ch 4.
- **Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006).** Implementation intentions and goal achievement. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology.* Specific if-then plans helped people follow through. Strong. Ch 10.
- **Gottman (1994).** *What Predicts Divorce?* Erlbaum. **Gottman and Levenson (1992).** Marital processes predictive of later dissolution. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* Defensiveness and related patterns predicted later trouble in couples. Moderate. Ch 7.
- **Gottman and Silver (1999).** *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work.* Crown. Flooding, repair attempts and the value of real breaks. Moderate. Ch 8.
- **Herz (2009).** Aromatherapy facts and fictions. *International Journal of Neuroscience.* Smells can affect mood, best explained by expectation and association. Debated. Ch 12.
- **Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004).** Removing the shadow of suspicion. *Journal of Applied Psychology.* Apologies repaired trust better for competence mistakes than for integrity breaches. Moderate. Ch 10.
- **Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005).** Egocentrism over e-mail. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* People overestimate how well their tone comes across in writing. Moderate. Ch 5.
- **Kurtz and Algoe (2015).** Putting laughter in context. *Personal Relationships.* Shared laughter went with closeness and support in couples. Early. Afterword.
- **Lewicki, Polin and Lount (2016).** An exploration of the structure of effective apologies. *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research.* Acknowledging responsibility mattered most, then offering repair. Moderate. Ch 2.
- **Malle (2006).** The actor-observer asymmetry in attribution. *Psychological Bulletin.* The textbook "actor-observer" pattern was close to zero on average. Strong. Ch 9.
- **Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde and Hankin (2004).** Is there a universal positivity bias in attributions? *Psychological Bulletin.* A large self-serving bias on average across 266 studies, smaller in some cultures. Strong. Ch 8.
- **Milton (2012).** On the ontological status of autism: the "double empathy problem". *Disability & Society.* Misunderstanding between different kinds of mind runs both ways. Theory. Ch 1.
- **Murray, Lesser and Lawson (2005).** Attention, monotropism and the diagnostic criteria for autism. *Autism.* Attention that runs deep on one thing at a time. Theory. Ch 6.
- **Okimoto, Wenzel and Hedrick (2013).** Refusing to apologize can have psychological benefits. *European Journal of Social Psychology.* Refusing to apologise raised short-term self-esteem and feelings of power. Moderate. Ch 2.
- **Phung, Penner, Pirlot and Welch (2021).** What I wish you knew. *Frontiers in Psychology.* Autistic young people described shutdown as involuntary and often misread. Early. Ch 8.
- **Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002).** The bias blind spot. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.* People see bias in others more than in themselves. Strong. Ch 9.
- **Ross and Sicoly (1979).** Egocentric biases in availability and attribution. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* People in joint efforts claim more responsibility than others give them. Strong. Ch 9.
- **Rowney-Smith, Sutton, Quadt and Eccles (2026).** The lived experience of rejection sensitivity in ADHD. *PLOS One.* Five students described rejection sensitivity as felt in the body. Early. Ch 3.
- **Schumann (2014).** An affirmed self and a better apology. *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology.* Reflecting on a core value first led to fuller, less defensive apologies. Moderate. Ch 3.
- **Schumann and Dweck (2014).** Who accepts responsibility for their transgressions? *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin.* Believing personality can change went with more responsibility-taking. Moderate. Ch 3.
- **Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow (2006).** Promises and lies. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes.* Consistent trustworthy actions restored trust, except after deception. Moderate. Ch 10.
- **Shaw, Stringaris, Nigg and Leibenluft (2014).** Emotion dysregulation in ADHD. *American Journal of Psychiatry.* Strong emotional reactions are common in ADHD across the lifespan. Strong. Ch 3.
- **Sheppard, Pillai, Wong, Ropar and Mitchell (2016).** How easy is it to read the minds of people with autism spectrum disorder? *Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders.* Observers misread autistic people's reactions more often; the reactions differed without being any smaller. Early. Ch 5.
- **Stone, Patton and Heen (1999).** *Difficult Conversations.* Viking. Disentangle intent from impact; look at contribution instead of blame. Theory. Ch 5.
- **Tangney and Dearing (2002).** *Shame and Guilt.* Guilford. **Tangney, Wagner, Fletcher and Gramzow (1992).** Shamed into anger? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* Guilt goes with repair; shame with hiding, blame and anger. Moderate. Ch 3.
- **Tavris and Aronson (2007).** *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me).* Harcourt. How self-justification protects us from seeing our own mistakes. Theory. Ch 8.
- **Zaccaro and colleagues (2018).** How breath-control can change your life. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience.* Slow breathing went with calmer body signals and feeling more relaxed. Early. Ch 11.
- **Zheng, Wang, Chiu and Shum (2022).** Time perception deficits in children and adolescents with ADHD: a meta-analysis. *Journal of Attention Disorders.* Young people with ADHD judged time less accurately on average. Moderate. Ch 10.
