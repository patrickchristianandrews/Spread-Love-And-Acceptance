# 4. Three ways to say sorry

*A Thursday evening, a tapas place in Bristol, Karin's fortieth (made up).*

Karin booked the table for eight people at half past seven. Nell, who has been her closest friend since they shared a flat at university, arrived at twenty past eight, kissed her, said "Sorry, sorry, work," and sat down. For the next hour her phone lay face up beside her plate, and she answered it three times, once taking it outside. She left before the cake because she had an early start.

Karin didn't say anything on the night. On Saturday she texts: "Honestly, Thursday really hurt."

Over the next week, Nell apologises three times. Each one is sincere, in its way, and each one is a different kind of apology.

## Three apologies, quickly.

**The first** comes on Saturday afternoon: "I'm really sorry you felt I let you down on Thursday. That's the last thing I'd want." It's about Karin's feeling. Nell is genuinely sorry Karin is upset, and has said nothing at all about the lateness or the phone.

**The second** comes on Monday, after Karin replies that it wasn't just a feeling: "OK, I'm sorry I was late. But you know what work's like at the moment, and to be fair you did move it from Friday." It names an act, the lateness, and then hands the weight of it straight back: to her job, and to Karin's change of date.

**The third** comes on Wednesday, in person, after Nell has thought about it properly: "I was nearly an hour late to your fortieth, and then I was on my phone for half of it, and I left before the cake. I was stressed about a deadline, but that was mine to deal with, and it was your night. I can see it made you feel like you came second to my inbox, on the one evening you'd asked for. I'm sorry. I'd like to take you out properly, just us, and next time something matters to you, the phone stays in my bag."

Those are the three kinds of apology this chapter is about. The first is a **deflection**: regret about the other person's feelings, no action named. The second is **partial ownership**: an action named, and then the responsibility shifted. The third is **genuine accountability**: the behaviour named, its effect understood, the responsibility kept, and something different offered for next time.

## The deflection looks after the apologiser.

Chapter 2 covered the first kind in detail, so only a short note here. A deflection isn't necessarily insincere. Nell meant it. But it can only ever be about the other person's inner state, and it leaves the thing that happened untouched. Karin can't accept it without agreeing, in effect, that the problem was how she felt.

## The partial apology has a "but" in it, and the "but" cancels the rest.

The second kind is the most common, and the most frustrating to receive, because it sounds so nearly right. It names the act. It uses the word sorry. And then a small word arrives and undoes the lot.

Bookkeepers have a useful way to see this. In double-entry bookkeeping, every entry has two sides: what goes out of one account goes into another, and the books only balance if both sides are recorded. When Nell says "I'm sorry I was late", she makes an entry: a debit against her own account. Then "but you moved it from Friday" posts an equal and opposite entry straight back to Karin's. The net effect on Nell's account is zero. On paper there's an apology. In the ledger nothing has moved.

That's why people so often say, after a partial apology, "you didn't actually apologise", and why the person who gave it is so baffled. They did say sorry. They can point to the word. What they didn't do was leave the entry standing.

The "but" usually comes in one of four shapes:

- **"But you…"** The responsibility is moved across to the other person. "But you made me angry." "But you moved the date."
- **"But I…"** It's moved into a circumstance, which then does the work of an excuse. "But I was exhausted." "But work's been mad."
- **"But it…"** It's made smaller. "But it was only an hour." "But it wasn't a big deal at the time."
- **"But we…"** It's spread thinly across both people until nobody in particular owns it. "But we were both tired." "But we'd both had a drink."

The things after the "but" are sometimes perfectly true. Karin did move the date. Nell was exhausted. Truth isn't the problem. Placement is. Put straight after the ownership, they read as a counterclaim. Kept until the other person has felt heard, or offered as context once the responsibility is clearly on your side of the ledger, they can actually help.

> **From the research.** Two studies by Cynthia Frantz and Courtney Bennigson found that apologies worked better when they came later in a conversation, after the hurt person had had a chance to explain how they felt, than when they came right at the start. The proposed reason: by then, the person felt heard and understood.
> *Frantz and Bennigson (2005) · Moderate: one study of students' real conflicts and one of imagined ones; small samples.*

## Genuine accountability has four working parts.

Look again at Nell's third apology. It does four things, more or less in this order.

It **names the behaviour**, specifically, in words a camera would accept: nearly an hour late, on the phone for half of it, left before the cake.

It **keeps the responsibility**. The deadline gets a mention, because it's true and it's part of the picture, but it comes with "that was mine to deal with". The context is there. It just isn't carrying the weight.

It **shows the effect was understood**: "you came second to my inbox, on the one evening you'd asked for." That sentence tells Karin that Nell has actually stood where she stood.

It **offers something different**: a repair now (taking her out) and a change for next time (the phone stays in the bag).

There's nothing magic about those four. They line up with what research finds people value most: acknowledgement of responsibility first, then an offer of repair. And none of them requires Nell to beat herself up, or to agree that she's a terrible friend. Chapter 3 is the reason that matters.

> **From the research.** Across 175 studies with more than 26,000 people, a meta-analysis of what goes with forgiveness found that receiving an apology was one of the stronger links, alongside the hurt person's empathy and how intentional the harm seemed. Situational factors, such as whether there was an apology, mattered more than the forgiving person's personality.
> *Fehr, Gelfand and Nag (2010) · Strong: a large meta-analysis, though most of the studies are correlational, so it shows what tends to go together rather than what causes what.*

## You can be sorry without agreeing with every reading of what happened.

On the Monday, before the third apology, Karin wrote something else: "It's obvious our friendship isn't a priority for you anymore."

Nell doesn't believe that. She's been Karin's friend for twenty years and would walk into traffic for her. If genuine accountability meant agreeing with every word of Karin's interpretation, Nell would be stuck: either she lies, or she refuses the whole thing.

It doesn't mean that. Accountability means honest acknowledgement of your own behaviour and its effect. It doesn't require automatic agreement with every conclusion the other person has drawn from it. Nell can say, truthfully:

> "I can see why Thursday made it look like you weren't a priority. I'd have read it that way too. It isn't true, and I want to show you it isn't. But I understand why you thought it, and I'm the one who gave you the reason."

That's not a "but" cancelling the apology. It's a disagreement about one conclusion, offered after the ownership is complete, with the cause still placed squarely on her side. There's a difference between "I'm sorry, but you're wrong that I don't care" (which reads as a counterattack) and "I did this, I see why it looked like that, and here's what's actually true for me" (which reads as someone who has taken it in and is being honest with you).

Disagreeing about meaning is fine. Disagreeing about whether the thing happened, when it plainly did, is where it goes wrong.

## Make the apology the size of the thing.

There's one more way an apology misfires, and it has nothing to do with which parts it contains. It's the size.

A small thing wants a small, quick apology. If you bump into someone's elbow and spill their tea, "Sorry, that was me, let me get you another" is perfect, and a ten-minute speech about how careless you've been would be strange and slightly exhausting. A big thing wants a proper one: time set aside, the full four parts, a repair that costs you something. "Soz about Thursday x" after missing a friend's fortieth is the wrong size, however sincere the x.

Getting the size wrong in either direction sends a message of its own. Too small, and it says *I don't think this mattered.* Too big, and it can say *this is now about how bad I feel*, which is chapter 3's collapse, or it can sound like a performance. People are oddly precise about this. Most of us can tell, within a sentence, whether an apology is the right size for what happened.

If you're not sure, ask. "I think I owe you a proper apology for Thursday, in person. Can I come round on Sunday?" The question itself tells the other person you've judged the size about right.

## The goodwill reserve.

One more idea from finance. A business keeps a reserve: money set aside for the bad month nobody planned for. Relationships have something similar. Call it goodwill: the stock of trust and warmth that lets a friendship absorb a late arrival without wobbling.

Every unacknowledged hurt is a small withdrawal from that reserve. Each one on its own is affordable. A run of them, especially with deflections or partial apologies that leave the books unbalanced, can empty it, and then one ordinary bad evening is enough to cause real damage. A genuine apology, with a real repair, is a deposit. It doesn't erase the withdrawal. It tops the reserve back up, and often a little beyond where it started, because both people now know the friendship can survive a mistake and talk about it.

**Said:** "OK, I'm sorry I was late. But you know what work's like at the moment."
**Heard:** *I'll say the word, and your hurt is still your problem.*
**Meant:** *I really am sorry, and I need you to know I wasn't being careless; I was drowning.*
**A better way:** "I was late, and I was on my phone, and it was your birthday. That's on me. Can I tell you what was going on at work? Not as an excuse. I just don't want you thinking I didn't care."

That last version keeps the explanation (which Nell needs to say, and which Karin may well want to hear), and moves it to after the ownership, as an offer.

> **Two ways to read this: the explanation.**
>
> Some people's wiring treats a full explanation as part of honesty. If they leave out *why* it happened, they feel they're hiding something, or giving a false picture of their own motives. Explaining is how they show they've thought it through and how they make sense of it themselves.
>
> Other people's wiring hears any explanation, especially an early one, as an excuse. The why sounds like a reason not to be blamed, and it pulls the conversation away from the hurt and towards the apologiser's day.
>
> **If you need to explain,** own the act first and then ask: "Do you want to know what was going on, or not right now?" If the answer is not now, keep it for later. It hasn't gone anywhere.
>
> **If explanations sound like excuses to you,** it can help to know that for some people an apology without the why feels incomplete and slightly dishonest. Once they've clearly owned it, the explanation that follows is often them trying to give you the whole truth. Wriggling tends to come before the ownership, and seldom after it.

> **Say it**
>
> To give a genuine one: "I did [the specific thing]. I can see it [the effect on you]. That was mine. I'm sorry. Next time I'll [one concrete change]."
>
> To ask for one without a fight: "I don't need you to agree with every word of how I've seen it. I'd just like you to say what you did, without the 'but'."
>
> To disagree with a reading while still owning the act: "I can see why it looked like that, and I gave you the reason to think it. Can I tell you what it was actually like for me?"

The *Apology languages* page treats these parts as a menu: regret, responsibility, making it right, changing, and asking for forgiveness. It suggests asking on a calm day which ones the other person needs most (spreadloveandacceptance.com/apology-languages). Its "Try" line is worth stealing: write an apology in three parts (what happened, that it was yours, one thing you'll do), then offer it and ask if it covers it.

## One small thing to try

Look back at the last apology you gave, by text if you can find it. Find the "but". If there's one, rewrite the message with the "but" and everything after it moved to a new paragraph that starts "Can I tell you what was going on?" Notice how differently the first paragraph reads once it stands on its own.

## What to remember

- A deflection apologises for the other person's feelings. A partial apology names the act and then hands the weight back. Genuine accountability names the act, keeps the responsibility, shows the effect was understood and offers something different.
- A "but" straight after "sorry" works like an equal and opposite entry in a ledger: the books show an apology and nothing has moved.
- Explanations are fine. Put them after the ownership, as an offer.
- You can own your behaviour fully without agreeing with every conclusion the other person has drawn from it.
