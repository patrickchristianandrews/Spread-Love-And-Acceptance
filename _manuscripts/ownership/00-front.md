## Before you start

Two sentences sit underneath this whole book, and it would be unfair to make you wait until chapter 9 to find them.

The first is that **owning something you did is not the same as admitting you are a bad person.** A great many apologies never get made because the person who owes one hears the request as a request to stand up and agree that they are, at bottom, unkind or selfish or useless. Nobody signs that, so they argue about the details instead, and the details are where the evening goes to die.

The second is that **saying sorry for someone's feelings is not the same as taking responsibility for your own actions.** "I'm sorry it made you feel that way" can be meant with complete sincerity. It can also, and often does, leave the other person holding the thing they came to talk about, with a polite note attached saying it was their reaction that was the problem.

Most of the trouble in between those two sentences comes from a gap. On the surface, a moment is small: a text not answered, a joke at the wrong time, a promise that slid. Underneath, it can be enormous, and the two people in it are often standing at different depths without knowing it. One is answering a question about Tuesday. The other is asking a question about the last three years. Both are telling the truth about the layer they can see.

This book is about that gap, and about what helps when an apology is owed, or wanted, or stuck.

### Who it's for

It's for the person who keeps asking for an apology and keeps getting something that sounds like one but isn't. It's equally for the person who keeps being asked, who feels they *have* apologised, several times, and can't work out what the other person wants from them. You are very probably both, at different times, with different people. I've written it so that the same standard applies to everyone in it, including you, and including whoever handed it to you.

It works for couples, and just as well for a parent and a grown-up child, for friends, for flatmates arguing about a frying pan, and for colleagues who have to sit opposite each other on Monday whatever happened on Friday. It's about the ordinary hurts that people who basically wish each other well do to each other, mostly without meaning to.

### How it's built

Each chapter starts with a short scene. Everyone in those scenes is made up, the way everyone in the examples throughout the book is made up: the people, their names, their kitchens and their arguments. Where the book uses something true about the person who made the website and these books, it comes from the website's About page and says so.

A lot of what makes an apology work is never said out loud. People are expected to absorb it, the way you absorb which fork to use, and plenty of us never quite did. So this book says the unwritten rules out loud, one at a time, in plain words, and then tests them on examples. If some of that feels obvious to you, someone else reading the same page needed it spelled out, and the reverse will be true a few pages later.

You'll find the same few things in every chapter, in slightly different places:

- **A moment taken apart.** Early on, I lay the scene out in a few quick strokes, from what a camera would have seen down through the layers to the root of it. Then I slow down and go through each layer properly. It's a way of seeing that you can learn, and by the end you'll be doing it faster than I do.
- **Said, heard, meant, and a better way.** One moment, written out four ways: the words, what the other person took from them, what the speaker intended, and a version that would have carried better.
- **Two ways to read this.** A short box in each chapter. People's brains are wired differently, and two kinds of wiring can read the same sentence in opposite ways. Some people take words very much at their word; others hear mostly the tune underneath. Some people need time to process before they can own anything; others need an answer now to feel safe. The box translates in both directions, and neither way of being wired is treated as the normal one.
- **Say it.** Words you could use, written to be changed. Make them sound like you.
- **One small thing to try.** One. Not a programme.
- **What to remember.** A few lines to take away.

Along the way, the book borrows ideas from other fields where they explain something better than the usual relationship language does. An economist, a musician, a bookkeeper and a debating coach would each describe a bad apology differently, and each would notice something the others miss. You don't need to know anything about any of those fields. A one-page map near the end shows how the ideas connect.

Where research helps, it's in short notes marked **From the research**, with an honest word on how strong it is: *strong*, *moderate*, *mixed*, *early*, *debated* or *theory*. Small studies are called small. A study is a pointer, never a verdict about you or anyone you love.

### What this book won't do

It won't give you a word for the other person. You'll notice there are no labels for people anywhere in it, and that's on purpose. A label ends the conversation in your head before it has happened in the room. The book describes what people do and what it does to the people around them, and leaves the verdict out.

It won't teach you to make someone apologise. You can't, and the attempt tends to make things worse. What you can do is make it easier for them to see the fuller picture, harder to slide away from the specific thing, and clear what you will and won't keep doing. Chapter 11 is about that last part.

And it won't let you off. Every tool in here works in both directions, and most of them were written with the reader's own part in mind first.
