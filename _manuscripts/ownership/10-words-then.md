# 10. Words, then what happens next

*A Sunday, half past two, the car park of a care home in Harrogate (made up).*

Gregor has been sitting in his car for forty minutes. He and his brother Lewis agreed in the spring that they'd visit their dad together every other Sunday, at half past one, so that one of them could talk to Dad while the other sorted out his room, his laundry and whatever the staff needed signing. Lewis arrives at ten past two, running, carrying a bag of satsumas.

"I'm so sorry," he says, before Gregor's even out of the car. "I'm really, really sorry. I completely lost track. I'm sorry."

It's the fourth time since April. Every apology has been warm and real. Gregor believes every one of them.

"Lewis," he says, "I don't want another sorry. I want you here at half one."

## The apology was never the problem.

Quickly. On the surface, a man late, with satsumas and a heartfelt apology. One layer down, the fourth time, which turns an accident into a pattern. Below that, Gregor's real question has quietly changed from *is he sorry?* to *does sorry mean anything?* Below that, Lewis means every word, and still can't get there on time, and he's beginning to dread these Sundays because each one ends in him failing again. And at the root, the oldest lesson of behavioural science: good intentions don't run a system. Setups do.

Now slowly.

Lewis's apologies are, on any reasonable measure, good ones. He names the act ("I lost track"). He keeps the responsibility. He clearly feels it. What's missing is the part that comes after the apology: anything changing next time. Words of ownership are where repair begins, and if nothing changes afterwards, the words lose value every time they're spent. By the fourth time, "I'm sorry" from Lewis sounds to Gregor like a currency that's been printed too freely.

## Real ownership shows up in what happens next.

In audit, the step that gets skipped more than any other is the follow-up: going back, months later, to check that the fix actually happened and actually held. The About page says it plainly: skipping that step is exactly how the same problem turns up again three years later. Without it, all you really have is a document.

An apology without a follow-up is that document. It says the right things. Nobody has checked whether anything changed.

So what does changed behaviour look like? Rarely a dramatic transformation. It's smaller and more ordinary than that, and Dele, from chapters 2 and 6, is a fair example of how it tends to arrive.

After the curry night, he corrected Fran in public twice more over the summer. The first time, at the barbecue, he only realised when he saw her face across the garden, and it took a night's thinking before he could say what had happened. By August, at a friend's birthday, he heard himself start ("Actually, it was…"), stopped halfway through the word, and said, "No, sorry, carry on, I'm doing the thing." Fran laughed, and finished her story. By the autumn, he was writing corrections in his phone and mentioning one, perhaps, on the way home, if it mattered, which it mostly didn't.

That's what change looks like from the outside. The person notices sooner: first only when it's pointed out, then halfway through, then before. The gap between doing the thing and owning it shrinks, from a night to a minute to a moment. And next time goes differently, though rarely perfectly: a slip, then two good evenings, then a smaller slip. Nobody needs a perfect record. What tells you the ownership was real is the direction of travel.

That kind of change is far more convincing than a better-worded apology, and far more realistic than a promise never to do it again. "I'll never do it again" is the single least believable sentence in any apology, because almost everyone has said it and almost nobody has kept it.

> **From the research.** In a lab study of trust, Maurice Schweitzer and colleagues found that trust damaged by untrustworthy behaviour recovered when people then saw a consistent run of trustworthy actions; a promise to change sped the recovery up. When the original breach had involved a lie, though, trust never fully recovered, even with a promise, an apology and a run of good behaviour.
> *Schweitzer, Hershey and Bradlow (2006) · Moderate: a carefully designed lab game, which isn't the same as a family, but the pattern fits a lot of other work.*

> **From the research.** A related set of studies found that an apology repaired trust better than a denial when a mistake was about competence (getting something wrong), while integrity breaches (doing something you knew was wrong) were much harder to repair with words.
> *Kim, Ferrin, Cooper and Dirks (2004) · Moderate: two experiments using a work scenario.*

Both point in the same direction. For ordinary mistakes, apology plus consistent change works well. When honesty was part of what broke, words carry much less weight, and only a long run of behaviour will do.

## Fix the setup, not the person.

Lewis's story gets more interesting from here. He doesn't need to be a better, more caring brother. He's already a caring brother; that's why the apologies are so heartfelt. What he needs is a different setup.

Behavioural science has spent decades on the gap between what people intend to do and what they actually do. One of its most practical findings is that a specific plan for *when* and *how* beats a general intention to try harder.

> **From the research.** A meta-analysis of 94 tests of "implementation intentions" (simple if-then plans such as "when it's 12:45 on a visiting Sunday, I'll put my coat on and leave, whatever I'm doing") found they helped people follow through on their goals noticeably better than intentions alone, with a medium-to-large effect on average.
> *Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) · Strong: a large meta-analysis across many kinds of goals; effects vary by goal and person.*

So the useful conversation between Gregor and Lewis isn't about sorry. It's about the setup:

- A leave-by time instead of an arrival time. Lewis's phone goes off at 12:45, labelled "LEAVE NOW, DAD", rather than at 1:30.
- The satsumas bought on Saturday, so there's no stop on the way.
- A backstop: if Lewis isn't in the car park by 1:35, Gregor starts on the laundry and texts once, and Lewis brings the tea when he gets there.
- A check after a month: is it working? If not, what's the next change?

This isn't Gregor running Lewis's life. Lewis designs it; Gregor agrees to the backstop. And nobody has to be a worse person for needing it.

> **Two ways to read this: sorry, and late again.**
>
> Some people's wiring keeps track of time loosely. An hour can feel like twenty minutes when they're absorbed, and "I'll leave in a minute" can genuinely become forty without them noticing it pass. They often care intensely about the thing they're late for, and are mortified each time. More effort and more sincerity don't fix it, because the problem isn't caring; it's a clock that runs differently inside.
>
> Other people's wiring keeps time closely, and reads repeated lateness as a message about priorities: *if it mattered to you, you'd be here.* When the apology is heartfelt and the lateness repeats, the apology starts to feel like a performance.
>
> **If time slips away from you,** the most convincing apology is a change to the setup, said out loud: "I'm sorry. I'm putting an alarm on for when I need to *leave*, instead of when I need to arrive, and if I'm not there by twenty-five to, start without me." That shows you've understood the cost and taken ownership of the fix.
>
> **If you keep time closely,** it can help to know that for some people lateness really isn't a measure of how much they care. Judging by the setup instead of the sincerity can free you both: "I believe you're sorry. What would make it work next time?" If they then build the setup and it starts to change, you've got your answer.

> **From the research.** A meta-analysis of 27 studies found that children and teenagers with ADHD judged time less accurately and less precisely than their peers, on average, across different kinds of timing task.
> *Zheng, Wang, Chiu and Shum (2022) · Moderate: a meta-analysis of lab timing tasks in children and adolescents; adult experience may differ, and many people with very different wiring also struggle with time.*

## When the hurt included a lie.

The trust research has an uncomfortable finding in it, and it deserves its own section. When what broke trust included a lie (the transfer hidden, the "I was at work" that wasn't true), apologies and promises carry much less weight, and trust recovers more slowly, sometimes never completely.

That doesn't mean repair is impossible. It means the words have to be smaller and the behaviour has to be longer. "I'll never lie to you again" is exactly the kind of big promise that a person who was lied to can't believe, because they've just learned that this person's words and actions can come apart. What they can believe, gradually, is a run of evidence: being told things before they find out, being shown the statement without asking, a small awkward truth offered when a small easy lie would have done.

Corinne, in chapter 8, didn't ask Rafe to promise anything. She asked that, for a year, they each mention any transfer from the joint savings over fifty pounds before it happened, and that they look at the joint account together once a month. Rafe found it awkward for about three weeks, and then ordinary. By the following spring, she'd stopped looking.

If you're the one who lied, the most useful thing you can do is volunteer the evidence before you're asked. If you're the one who was lied to, it's fair to ask for evidence for a while, and fair to tell the other person roughly how long "a while" might be, so that it has an end.

## Words still matter.

None of this means the words are pointless. The words come first. They tell the other person that you've seen what happened and that you're on their side of it. Without them, a change in behaviour can feel cold, or go unnoticed, or be put down to luck.

The words open the account. The behaviour pays into it. And the follow-up, a month later, is how both people find out whether it worked.

**Said:** "I'm so sorry. I'm really, really sorry. I completely lost track."
**Heard:** *The same apology as last time, and nothing will change.*
**Meant:** *I hate letting you down and I don't know how to stop.*
**A better way:** "I'm sorry. That's four times, and I know my sorry is starting to sound cheap. I don't think trying harder is going to fix it. Can we sort out a setup? I'll do a leave-by alarm, and if I'm not here by twenty-five to, start without me. Shall we check in a month whether it's working?"

And from Gregor, his side of the same moment: "I believe you're sorry. I don't want any more sorries. I want us to make it work. And thank you for the satsumas."

> **Say it**
>
> When your apologies have started to sound cheap: "I know you've heard sorry from me before. This time I want to change something you'll be able to see."
>
> When you're receiving the fourth apology: "I believe you mean it. I don't need another sorry. What can we change so it doesn't happen again?"
>
> A month later: "Can we check how it's going? Is the new way working for you?"

The *What keeps coming back?* workpaper on the website is a monthly look-back for exactly this: the things that keep slipping, and what change to the setup might catch them (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-04). The *90-second daily check-in* is deliberately small enough to keep doing on a bad week, which is when it matters most (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-13).

## One small thing to try

Think of an apology you've made more than once. Instead of making it again, write down one change to the setup that would make the thing less likely: an alarm, a backstop, a different time, a reminder in a different place. Tell the other person about the change, and leave out how sorry you are. Then put a date in your diary, a month from now, to check whether it held.

## What to remember

- Words of ownership are where repair starts. Changed behaviour is what makes them believable.
- Change shows up as noticing sooner, repairing faster and doing it differently next time. Nobody needs a perfect record.
- Fix the setup, not the person: specific if-then plans beat trying harder.
- Go back and check. An apology with no follow-up is a document nobody has read since.
