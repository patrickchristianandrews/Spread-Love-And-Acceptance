# 12. Putting it together

*A Saturday morning in May, Roath Park in Cardiff, a lap of the lake (made up).*

Fran's sister's wedding is in three weeks. There'll be a long table, speeches, both families, and a great many stories told by a great many people, with all the details slightly wrong.

Fran and Dele have decided to talk about it in advance, and they've chosen where. Not in the car after an event, when they're both tired and trapped. Not at the kitchen table, face to face, which feels to Dele like an interview. They're walking, side by side, at nine in the morning, with coffees, round the lake. There are swans. Nobody is in a hurry.

"Can we make a plan for the wedding?" Fran says. "Not because I think it'll go wrong. Just so we both know."

Dele has thought about it already. "If I hear something wrong in a story, I'll write it in my phone, and I'll tell you afterwards if it matters. It won't, mostly." He thinks for a moment. "And if I slip, you could touch my arm. I'll know."

"And if *I* get upset about something and go quiet," Fran says, "you could ask me straight out, instead of guessing. I'd rather be asked."

They do a second lap because the coffee isn't finished.

## The whole thing in one place.

That walk is most of this book in about four minutes. Two people who've had a hard time over the same thing, both choosing the setting, both owning a part, each asking for something specific, each offering something specific, each translating for the other's wiring, and a plan made in advance instead of an argument after.

It won't always go like that. It went like that this time because of everything that happened before: the curry, the car, the barbecue, the crooked picture, the breakfast answer. Most good conversations are built on earlier, worse ones.

This is the book on one page, in the order that tends to work.

## The one-page guide.

**Before you talk**

1. **Settle your body first.** A racing body turns any sentence into an ultimatum and hears every reply as an attack. Walk, breathe out slowly, eat something, or wait until tomorrow (chapters 8 and 11).
2. **Choose the time and the place.** Pick a moment when nobody has just walked in the door, nobody else is listening and neither of you is trapped. Side by side often works better than face to face.
3. **Find the layers.** Surface, pattern, cost, meaning, root. Ask "and underneath that?" two or three times (chapter 1).
4. **Sort what you know from what you've guessed.** Facts a camera could check go first. Guesses about their insides are offered as questions (chapter 1).
5. **Ask yourself the mirror question.** If they'd done this to you, what would you want them to own? Is there anything of yours that the same answer covers? (chapter 9).

**When you raise it**

6. **Own your part first, if you have one.** Briefly and fully, without the "but" (chapters 4 and 9).
7. **Name one specific behaviour.** In camera words. One thing; any list can wait (chapters 2 and 7).
8. **Say what you're not saying.** "I'm not saying you're a bad person." Shame is listening for the verdict (chapter 3).
9. **Believe their intent, and keep the impact on the table.** "I don't think you meant it. It still landed badly" (chapter 5).
10. **Ask, then wait.** One open question about one moment. Leave the rest in the bar. "I don't know yet" is an appointment (chapter 6).

**When it goes sideways**

11. **If you get "sorry you feel that way",** go back to the specific behaviour and say what you're asking for (chapter 2).
12. **If it gets turned round,** agree the other items exist, write them down, and finish one first (chapter 7).
13. **If they shut down,** treat it as *can't* until it proves to be *won't*. Give time and see whether they come back (chapter 8).
14. **If it loops a third time,** stop, and set a time to come back (chapters 7 and 11).

**Afterwards**

15. **Agree one change to the setup, and a date to check it.** Words open the account; behaviour pays into it (chapter 10).
16. **If the pattern never moves,** decide what you'll do. What they do is theirs. A limit, said calmly, with the door left open (chapter 11).

You won't use all sixteen in any one conversation. Most conversations need three or four. The list is there so that, when a talk goes wrong, you can look back and find which step went missing.

## The setting is part of the conversation.

People who work with scent and the senses start from a point that most relationship advice skips: the setting isn't neutral background. Light, noise, smell, temperature and time of day all shape what a person can take in. The same sentence lands differently in a bright, loud kitchen at six in the evening and on a quiet walk at nine in the morning.

The field of aromatherapy itself makes claims that range from reasonable to very doubtful, and the evidence for specific oils doing specific things is thin. The broader idea underneath, that what reaches our senses affects our mood, has better support, though probably not for the reasons the bottles suggest.

> **From the research.** A scientific review of studies on scent and mood concluded that smells can affect mood, physiology and behaviour, but that these effects are best explained by psychology (what we expect from a smell, and what it reminds us of) rather than by the chemicals acting directly on the body.
> *Herz (2009) · Debated: a careful review, and the wider field it describes is full of small, weak studies; claims about particular oils remain unproven.*

For the purposes of this book, that's actually the useful finding. You don't need a particular oil. You need a setting that tells both your bodies they're safe enough to think. For some people, that's a familiar smell, like coffee on a walk or the kitchen after a meal you both like. For others it's quiet, or softer light, or being outdoors, or not being looked at directly. Ask the other person what helps. Tell them what helps you.

> **Two ways to read where to sit.**
>
> Some people's wiring finds eye contact expensive. Holding someone's gaze while also listening, thinking and choosing words is a lot of work at once, and something has to give, usually the thinking. Side by side, walking, driving (on an ordinary day), or in writing, they can give the conversation their full attention.
>
> Other people's wiring reads eye contact as the sign that someone is listening. Looking away, or wanting to talk while doing something else, can feel like being half-heard, or like the subject isn't being taken seriously.
>
> **If eye contact costs you,** say so, and offer the setting that helps: "I listen better when we're walking. It's not that I don't care. It's that I can think." Most people are glad of a reason.
>
> **If you need eye contact to feel heard,** try a walk once, and judge by what the other person says rather than where they look. If the answers are more thoughtful side by side, you've found something useful. And you can always ask for a moment of looking at the end: "Can you look at me while you say that bit?"

## The scripts, by who it's with.

The ideas stay the same with everyone. The wording changes. These are starting points, to be rewritten until they sound like you.

**With a partner**

- Raising it: "Can we talk about Saturday? I'm not saying you're a bad partner. One thing hurt, and I'd like us to look at it together."
- Owning yours: "I did that, and I can see what it did. That's mine. I'm sorry. Next time I'll…"
- When you hear "sorry you feel that way": "I'm not asking you to be responsible for my feelings. I'm asking you to say what you did, without the 'but'."

**With a parent or a grown-up child**

- Raising it: "Mum, I love you, and I'm not calling you a bad mum. I asked you to keep something to yourself and it got passed on. I'd like you to see why that mattered."
- When shame takes over: "I can hear this landing as 'you're awful'. That's not what I'm saying. Can we look at just the one thing?"
- A limit: "I'll keep telling you everyday things. Big news, I'm going to share myself first."

**With a friend**

- Raising it: "Can I tell you something about Thursday? It's not a big drama. It's been sitting with me, and I'd rather say it than let it grow."
- Disagreeing with a reading while owning the act: "I can see why it looked like I didn't care, and I gave you the reason to think it. Can I tell you what was really going on?"

**With a flatmate**

- When the list arrives: "Some of that's fair, and I want to talk about it. Can we finish the late nights first, and then do yours properly?"
- Making it structural: "Shall we write the jobs down with names next to them, so they stop turning up in other arguments?"

**At work**

- Raising an impact: "I don't think you meant it like that. In front of the client, 'just the numbers side' made my part sound small. Could you put it right on Friday's call?"
- Owning one: "I can see how that came across. You did the core of this and I made it sound like a footnote. I'll correct it with the client."
- Keeping it to one issue: "There are a few things we could cover. Can we take this one to the end first?"

**With anyone, when it's going round in circles**

- "I want to sort this out, and I don't want to keep going round. I'd like to stop for now and come back to it on Sunday."

## One conversation, start to finish.

| Said (before the wedding, the old way) | "Please don't correct me at the wedding. You always do it and it's humiliating." |
|---|---|
| Heard | *You're a liability in public and I'm dreading you.* |
| Meant | *I'm nervous about my family, and I want to know you're on my side.* |
| A better way (the walk round the lake) | "Can we make a plan for the wedding? Not because I think it'll go wrong. I get nervous around my family, and I'd love to know we've got a way of handling it. What would help you, if you hear something wrong in a story?" |

Everything is in that last version: the setting chosen, her own part owned (her nerves), the request specific and future-facing, no verdict on his character, and an open question that hands him half the plan.

## One small thing to try

Pick the one script in this chapter you're most likely to need in the next month. Rewrite it in your own words, out loud, until it sounds like something you'd actually say. Save it in the notes on your phone, under a title you'll remember when your heart's going. You'll be surprised how much easier it is to find words you wrote when you were calm.

## What to remember

- Settle the body, choose the setting, find the layers, sort fact from guess. Most of the work happens before the conversation starts.
- Own your part first, name one specific thing, say what you're not saying, ask and then wait.
- When it goes sideways, there's a move for each way it goes: back to the behaviour, one issue at a time, time for a shutdown, a stop for a loop.
- Afterwards, change the setup and check it held. If nothing ever changes, decide what you'll do.
