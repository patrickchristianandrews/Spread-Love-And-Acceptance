A gentle note: this library is general education about how people tend to think, feel and get along. It is not diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t know your situation. Where the evidence is thin, mixed or debated, each entry says so.

Emotions aren’t the opposite of good sense. They are fast signals about what matters to us. Psychologists have argued for a century about exactly what they are, but they agree on a lot about how to live with them well. These entries cover the main ideas, and the program’s habit of naming feelings before trying to solve anything.

What emotions are: two views

Also known as: emotions, basic emotions, constructed emotion, Ekman, Barrett, feelings.

In a nutshell

One long-standing view says there are a few basic emotions, each with its own facial expression. A newer view says the brain constructs emotions from body sensations, context and learned concepts. The debate matters for how we read each other.

Going deeper

Paul Ekman’s research in the 1960s and 1970s suggested that some facial expressions of emotion, such as happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust and surprise, are recognised across cultures. This supported the idea of a small set of built-in basic emotions.

Lisa Feldman Barrett’s theory of constructed emotion argues instead that emotions are not hard-wired circuits. The brain predicts and interprets body sensations using context and concepts learned from culture and experience. On this view, the same sensation (a racing heart) might become excitement, fear or anger depending on the situation.

Whatever view you take, a practical lesson follows: faces and body language don’t reliably tell us what someone feels. People express the same emotion differently, and different emotions similarly. Asking is more accurate than guessing.

What the research says, and its limits

Ekman’s cross-cultural studies were influential but have been criticised for methods that may have inflated agreement. Research by Maria Gendron, Barrett and colleagues with remote communities found less universal recognition. A 2019 review led by Barrett concluded that facial movements are not reliable indicators of specific emotions. Many emotion researchers take positions between the two views, and the debate is ongoing.

Emotion regulation: Gross’s process model

Also known as: emotion regulation, managing emotions, regulate, calming down, Gross.

In a nutshell

Emotion regulation is how people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they express them. James Gross described five points where regulation can happen, from choosing situations to changing how you respond.

Going deeper

Gross’s process model lays out a sequence. Situation selection: choosing whether to enter a situation (not starting a hard talk at bedtime). Situation modification: changing it (moving to a quieter room). Attentional deployment: shifting attention (focusing on the problem rather than the tone). Cognitive change: changing how you think about it (seeing tiredness rather than malice). Response modulation: changing how you express or respond to the emotion once it is there (breathing slowly, or holding back a sharp reply).

Strategies earlier in the sequence often work better and cost less effort. Choosing a good time for a conversation is easier than staying calm through a badly timed one.

The program’s tools act at different points: the weather check helps with situation selection, the calm-down kit helps with modification and response, and the tone filter works on attention and interpretation.

What the research says, and its limits

Gross published the process model in 1998 and it has become the dominant framework in emotion regulation research, with a large body of experimental and correlational work. Researchers now emphasise flexibility: using different strategies for different situations seems more important than any single “best” strategy.

Reappraisal versus suppression

Also known as: reappraisal, suppression, bottling up, hiding feelings, reframing, cognitive reappraisal.

In a nutshell

Reappraisal means changing how you interpret a situation; suppression means hiding the emotion you feel. Research suggests reappraisal generally has better effects on wellbeing and relationships than habitual suppression.

Going deeper

Suppose your partner arrives late. Reappraisal might mean thinking “traffic is awful today; they’re probably stressed,” which changes how you feel. Suppression means feeling just as annoyed but hiding it behind a neutral face.

Suppression takes effort and doesn’t reduce the underlying feeling much. It can also make conversations feel strange: the other person senses something is off but can’t tell what. Studies have found that suppression during a conversation can increase stress in both people.

Reappraisal isn’t always appropriate. Some situations call for acting on emotion, not reinterpreting it. And forcing a positive spin on something genuinely unfair can feel like self-gaslighting. The skill is using reappraisal where it fits, and expression where that fits.

What the research says, and its limits

Gross and Oliver John (2003) found that people who habitually used reappraisal reported more positive emotion and better relationships, while habitual suppressors reported the opposite. Emily Butler and colleagues (2003) found that when one person suppressed emotions during a conversation, their partner’s blood pressure rose and rapport fell. Cultural research suggests suppression has smaller costs in some cultures where emotional restraint is the norm.

Naming feelings: affect labelling

Also known as: affect labeling, affect labelling, name it to tame it, putting feelings into words, naming emotions.

In a nutshell

Putting a feeling into words, such as “I’m feeling anxious,” can reduce its intensity a little. Researchers call this affect labelling. It is a simple, well-studied tool.

Going deeper

It seems too simple to work, but naming an emotion appears to change how the brain processes it. When people label emotional images with words, brain regions associated with emotional reactivity tend to show less activity, and regions associated with regulation more.

Affect labelling isn’t the same as analysing or dwelling on feelings. It is simply noticing and naming: “that’s frustration,” “I feel overwhelmed.” It can be done silently, out loud, or in writing.

This is part of why check-ins start with naming feelings, and why the program encourages a one-sentence feeling update rather than a long debate.

What the research says, and its limits

Matthew Lieberman and colleagues published a key brain-imaging study in 2007 (“Putting feelings into words”). Jared Torre and Lieberman reviewed the evidence in 2018. Effects are real but modest, and mostly measured in laboratory tasks. The popular phrase “name it to tame it” is associated with Daniel Siegel.

Emotional granularity

Also known as: emotional vocabulary, feelings words, emotional differentiation, feelings wheel, Barrett.

In a nutshell

Emotional granularity means being able to tell apart similar feelings, like disappointed, lonely, resentful or worn out, rather than just feeling “bad.” Higher granularity is linked to better coping.

Going deeper

Someone with low granularity might say “I feel bad” or “I’m stressed” for many different experiences. Someone with high granularity can distinguish “I feel left out” from “I feel overlooked” from “I feel exhausted.” Each suggests a different response.

Precise words help others help you. “I’m upset” leaves your partner guessing. “I feel taken for granted” points straight at the issue.

Granularity can be learned. Reading, talking about feelings, and tools like feelings lists or wheels expand vocabulary. People who struggle with this aren’t failing; they may simply not have had the practice, or may have alexithymia.

What the research says, and its limits

Lisa Feldman Barrett and colleagues (2001) introduced the concept of emotion differentiation. Todd Kashdan, Barrett and Patrick McKnight reviewed research in 2015 linking higher granularity with better emotion regulation and less use of unhelpful coping, such as heavy drinking under stress. Most studies are correlational.

Alexithymia: difficulty naming feelings

Also known as: alexithymia, can't describe feelings, emotional blindness, not knowing what I feel.

In a nutshell

Alexithymia is a personality trait, not a diagnosis, involving difficulty identifying and describing your own feelings. It is common, varies in degree, and is more frequent in autistic people.

Going deeper

A person with high alexithymia may feel something intense but find it hard to say what it is. They might notice body sensations (a knot in the stomach) without connecting them to an emotion. Asked “how do you feel?”, they may honestly not know.

This can lead to misunderstandings. A partner may think they are being evasive or cold, when they are actually struggling to find the answer.

Helpful approaches include asking about body sensations or situations rather than feelings (“what happened in your body?” or “was it more like annoyed or more like tired?”), giving time, offering choices, and using written check-ins. Alexithymia is not a lack of feeling; it is a difficulty with labelling.

What the research says, and its limits

Peter Sifneos coined the term in 1973. The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), developed by Michael Bagby, James Parker and Graeme Taylor in 1994, is the most widely used measure. A 2019 meta-analysis by Emma Kinnaird and colleagues estimated that around half of autistic people have high levels of alexithymia, compared with a much lower share in the general population. Research suggests some difficulties with emotion recognition often attributed to autism may be better explained by co-occurring alexithymia.

Emotional contagion

Also known as: catching moods, mood spreading, contagious emotions, bad mood spreading.

In a nutshell

Emotions spread between people, often without either noticing. A tense person can make a room tense; a calm one can help settle it.

Going deeper

Elaine Hatfield, John Cacioppo and Richard Rapson described emotional contagion as the tendency to automatically mimic and synchronise with others’ expressions, voices and postures, and so to catch their feelings.

At home, this means one person’s stressful day can become everyone’s stressful evening. That isn’t anyone’s fault, but it is worth noticing, because it means moods can be caught and passed back and forth in escalating loops.

It also works positively: a warm greeting or shared laughter can lift a room. And it suggests a practical step: when you come home stressed, naming it (“I’ve had a rough day; it’s not about you”) can prevent it being misread and caught.

What the research says, and its limits

Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson published Emotional Contagion in 1994, building on earlier papers. Research supports emotional contagion in face-to-face settings. A large 2014 Facebook study suggested small contagion effects online, but was controversial for its ethics and the tiny size of its effects.

Shame and guilt

Also known as: shame, guilt, feeling bad about yourself, embarrassment, Tangney.

In a nutshell

Guilt says “I did a bad thing”; shame says “I am bad.” Research suggests guilt tends to lead toward repair, while shame tends to lead toward hiding, defensiveness or blame.

Going deeper

June Tangney and colleagues have spent decades studying the difference. Guilt focuses on a specific action, and so points toward fixing it: apologising, making amends. Shame focuses on the whole self, which feels much harder to fix, so people tend to withdraw, hide or lash out.

This matters for conflict. When criticism is framed as a verdict on someone’s character (“you’re so selfish”), it tends to provoke shame, and shame tends to provoke defensiveness. When a complaint is about a specific behaviour, it leaves space for guilt, which is more likely to lead to change.

It also matters for how people talk to themselves. Harsh self-criticism breeds shame; self-compassion makes it easier to own mistakes.

What the research says, and its limits

Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s Shame and Guilt (2002) summarises the research. Studies have linked shame-proneness with anger and defensiveness and guilt-proneness with empathy and repair. Some researchers have questioned whether the measures fully separate the two emotions, and cultural research suggests shame has different meanings in different societies.

Anger, and whether venting helps

Also known as: anger, angry, venting, catharsis, letting it out, punching a pillow, rage.

In a nutshell

Anger often signals that something important feels threatened or unfair. The popular idea that venting anger (shouting, hitting a pillow) lets it out has not held up; calming activities tend to reduce anger more.

Going deeper

Anger can be useful: it motivates people to address injustice and protect boundaries. It is often layered with other feelings, such as hurt, fear or exhaustion, and paying attention to what is underneath can help.

The catharsis idea, that anger builds up like steam and needs releasing, is intuitive but mostly wrong. Studies suggest that venting in aggressive ways tends to keep anger going, or increase it. Activities that lower arousal (slow breathing, relaxation, a calming walk) tend to reduce anger more effectively.

Talking about anger calmly is different from venting. Describing what made you angry, and what you need, can help. Rehearsing grievances repeatedly, or letting off steam at someone, tends not to.

What the research says, and its limits

Brad Bushman’s 2002 experiment found that people who hit a punching bag while thinking about someone who had provoked them became more aggressive, not less. A 2024 meta-analysis by Sophie Kjærvik and Bushman found that arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger, while arousal-increasing ones, such as some vigorous activities used for venting, did not reliably help.

Rumination and co-rumination

Also known as: rumination, overthinking, replaying arguments, dwelling, going over and over, co-rumination.

In a nutshell

Rumination is repeatedly going over problems and feelings without moving toward solutions. It tends to prolong low mood and anger. Co-rumination is doing this together, which can feel close but can also deepen distress.

Going deeper

After an argument, it is normal to replay it. Rumination happens when the replay gets stuck: the same thoughts go round, often focused on why it happened and how bad it feels, without leading anywhere new.

Susan Nolen-Hoeksema found that people who respond to low mood by ruminating tend to feel worse for longer. Distraction, problem-solving or physical activity often break the loop better than more thinking.

Co-rumination, described by Amanda Rose, is when friends or partners repeatedly discuss problems in ways that keep them alive, for example talking over a grievance with a friend for hours. It can feel supportive, and it does build closeness, but it can also increase anxiety and low mood. Balance helps: talk, then move to action or distraction.

What the research says, and its limits

Nolen-Hoeksema published her response styles theory in 1991 and many studies followed. Rose introduced co-rumination in 2002, with research mostly on adolescents showing it is linked both to friendship closeness and to anxiety and depressive symptoms. Research on rumination is extensive and generally consistent.

Writing it down

Also known as: expressive writing, journaling, diary, writing about feelings, Pennebaker.

In a nutshell

Writing about thoughts and feelings for a short time over a few days has been studied since the 1980s. The effects on wellbeing are real but small on average, and vary a lot between people and settings.

Going deeper

James Pennebaker asked people to write for about fifteen to twenty minutes on a few consecutive days about difficult experiences. Many reported feeling better afterwards, and some studies found other benefits. The idea is that putting experiences into words helps people organise and make sense of them.

Writing also has a simpler benefit: it creates a record. Memories shift and blur, especially around conflict. A few notes written at the time capture what happened before it turns into a story.

Writing isn’t for everyone, and writing that simply rehearses grievances may not help. The research suggests writing that moves toward understanding, using words like “because” and “realise,” tends to be more helpful than writing that stays stuck.

What the research says, and its limits

Pennebaker and Sandra Beall published the first expressive writing study in 1986. A 2006 meta-analysis by Joanne Frattaroli found a small average positive effect across many studies. Some later meta-analyses found smaller or inconsistent effects for specific outcomes. It is best described as a low-cost practice with modest, variable benefits.

Moods, emotions and temperament

Also known as: mood, bad mood, emotions versus moods, temperament, background feeling.

In a nutshell

Emotions are usually short and about something specific. Moods are longer, vaguer and often have no clear cause. Temperament is a longer-lasting tendency. Mixing them up causes confusion.

Going deeper

If you are angry because a plan was cancelled, that is an emotion: it has an object and passes. If you’ve felt irritable all day for no obvious reason, that is more like a mood. Moods colour how we interpret everything, often without our noticing.

This matters because people tend to explain moods by blaming whatever is in front of them. A grumpy mood looks for something to be grumpy about, and a partner is often nearby.

Temperament describes stable tendencies, like being generally calm or easily stirred. Knowing each other’s temperaments helps: some people naturally react strongly and settle quickly; others react slowly but stay affected longer.

What the research says, and its limits

Emotion researchers generally distinguish emotions, moods and traits by duration, intensity and whether they have a clear object, though definitions vary. Research on mood-congruent judgement, including by Joseph Forgas, shows that moods influence how people interpret ambiguous events. Temperament research, from Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess’s studies of infants in the 1950s onward, shows that some emotional tendencies are present early.

Grief, loss and change

Also known as: grief, loss, grieving, bereavement, stages of grief, change and loss, dual process.

In a nutshell

Grief is a natural response to loss, including losses that aren’t deaths, like a move, a job ending or a relationship changing. The famous “five stages” are not a fixed sequence; people grieve in many different ways.

Going deeper

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance in 1969, based on her work with dying patients. The stages became famous, but research doesn’t support them as a set sequence everyone moves through. Many people don’t experience all of them, or experience them out of order, or return to some.

Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut’s dual process model suggests people move back and forth between facing the loss (remembering, feeling, missing) and attending to life’s changes (new routines, practical tasks, rest). Both are part of healthy grieving; oscillating between them is normal.

In a household, people may grieve at different paces and in different ways: one wants to talk, one wants to keep busy. Neither is wrong. Understanding this can prevent a second hurt: feeling that the other person isn’t grieving “properly.”

If grief feels overwhelming or stuck, talking with a qualified professional can help.

What the research says, and its limits

Kübler-Ross’s On Death and Dying was published in 1969. Stroebe and Schut published the dual process model in 1999. George Bonanno’s research found that many bereaved people show resilience, with relatively stable functioning, and that grief trajectories vary widely. The idea of continuing bonds (Dennis Klass and colleagues, 1996) describes how people often keep a connection with those they have lost.

Resilience

Also known as: resilience, bouncing back, coping, ordinary magic, hardiness.

In a nutshell

Resilience is doing reasonably well despite adversity. Research suggests it is common rather than rare, and that it depends heavily on relationships and resources, not only on individual toughness.

Going deeper

Ann Masten called resilience “ordinary magic”: it arises from ordinary human systems like supportive relationships, problem-solving skills and a sense of agency, rather than from rare personal qualities.

George Bonanno found that after difficult events, many people show a resilient pattern: some disruption, but relatively stable functioning. This challenges the idea that everyone must be deeply damaged by hardship, while not dismissing those who struggle.

Resilience is not about never struggling or hiding pain. It often involves asking for help, adjusting expectations and resting. In families and couples, resilience is partly shared: how people support each other matters as much as how each copes alone.

What the research says, and its limits

Masten published “Ordinary Magic” in 2001. Bonanno’s 2004 paper and later work describe resilience trajectories. Researchers caution against presenting resilience as a personal trait that people should simply have, since it depends so much on circumstances and support.

Positive emotions: broaden and build

Also known as: positive emotions, joy, broaden and build, Fredrickson, happiness, good feelings.

In a nutshell

Barbara Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory suggests positive emotions widen attention and thinking in the moment, and over time help build lasting resources like relationships and skills.

Going deeper

Negative emotions tend to narrow focus: fear focuses on the threat. Positive emotions such as joy, interest and contentment tend to broaden it, making people more open, creative and connected.

Over time, these broadened moments add up. Playing builds skills; interest builds knowledge; warmth builds relationships. This offers one explanation for why small positive moments matter so much in relationships.

This doesn’t mean forcing positivity. Toxic positivity, insisting on looking on the bright side, can leave people feeling dismissed. The aim is to make room for good moments, not to banish hard ones.

What the research says, and its limits

Fredrickson published the theory in 1998 and 2001, with experimental support for the broadening effect. A related claim, the “positivity ratio” (Fredrickson and Marcial Losada, 2005), was criticised by Nicholas Brown, Alan Sokal and Harris Friedman in 2013 for mathematical errors, and the modelling part was formally withdrawn. The broaden-and-build theory itself remains influential.

Emotional safety

Also known as: feeling safe, emotional safety, safe to speak, safe space.

In a nutshell

Emotional safety is the sense that you can be honest, including about feelings and needs, without being mocked, punished or rejected. It is the ground on which difficult conversations become possible.

Going deeper

When people feel emotionally safe, they share more, admit mistakes more easily and listen better. When they don’t, they hide, defend or go quiet, and the other person often reads that as not caring.

Emotional safety is built by small responses over time: taking concerns seriously, not using shared vulnerabilities as ammunition, keeping confidences, and responding to feelings with care. It is damaged by mockery, contempt and unpredictability.

Many couples tools, such as ground rules, turn-taking and agreed pauses, are ways of creating safety when it is fragile. They signal “this is a space where we won’t hurt each other.”

What the research says, and its limits

“Emotional safety” is used widely by therapists and draws on attachment theory and research on responsiveness. In teams, the parallel concept of psychological safety (Amy Edmondson, 1999) has strong research support. Research on disclosure in relationships shows that people share more when they expect a responsive reaction.
