A gentle note: this library is general education about how people tend to think, feel and get along. It is not diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t know your situation. Where the evidence is thin, mixed or debated, each entry says so.

Households don’t exist in a vacuum. They sit inside wider families, money pressures and life stages that keep changing: a new baby, a move, a parent who needs more help, children leaving home. These transitions reshuffle who does what, often without anyone deciding. These entries gather what research says about the family and life-stage side of sharing a life, gently and without clinical claims.

Family systems and triangles

Also known as: family systems, Bowen, triangles, triangulation, differentiation, family patterns.

In a nutshell

Family systems thinking sees a family as a connected system, where each person’s behavior affects the others. Murray Bowen described how tension between two people is often routed through a third, forming a triangle.

Going deeper

Bowen observed that when two people are in conflict, anxiety often gets relieved by involving a third: a child, a parent, a friend. A couple who argue may both confide in one of their mothers; a parent may lean on a child for support against the other parent. These triangles can bring short-term relief but keep the original issue unresolved.

Bowen also described differentiation of self: the ability to stay emotionally connected to family while keeping your own thoughts and choices. Highly differentiated people can disagree without cutting off or being swept along.

The systems view is useful because it moves away from blaming one person. A pattern in a family is maintained by everyone’s moves, and any one person changing their move can shift it.

What the research says, and its limits

Bowen developed family systems theory from the 1950s, and Michael Kerr and Bowen published Family Evaluation in 1988. It has been influential in family therapy. Empirical testing is limited, although some measures of differentiation (for example by Elizabeth Skowron) have been linked to wellbeing and relationship functioning. It is best treated as a useful lens rather than a proven theory.

Family scripts and inherited patterns

Also known as: family script, family patterns, how we were raised, family of origin, inherited habits.

In a nutshell

A family script is a set of expectations about how family life should go, learned in the family you grew up in. Two partners often bring two different scripts, and clashes between them can feel like personal disagreements.

Going deeper

John Byng-Hall described family scripts as shared expectations about roles and patterns, passed down across generations. Some are repeated (“in our family, Mom handles birthdays”), some are corrective (“I’ll never do it the way my parents did”).

Partners from different families bring different scripts about chores, money, emotions, holidays and conflict. Each script feels normal to the person who grew up with it, so the other person’s way can feel wrong rather than just different.

Talking about where your expectations came from can take heat out of disagreements. It also opens the question: which parts of our scripts do we want to keep, and which to rewrite together?

What the research says, and its limits

Byng-Hall published on family scripts in 1985 and in Rewriting Family Scripts (1995). The concept comes from family therapy and is supported by clinical experience more than experimental research. Research on intergenerational transmission of relationship patterns, for example studies of how parents’ conflict relates to children’s later relationships, supports the general idea that family patterns carry forward, though not in a fixed way.

In-laws and extended family

Also known as: in laws, mother in law, father in law, extended family, boundaries with family, parents in law.

In a nutshell

Relationships with in-laws can be a source of support or of strain. Research suggests couples do best when they act as a team and each partner takes the lead with their own family.

Going deeper

Joining a family means joining its habits, expectations and history. What feels like normal involvement to one family may feel intrusive to another. Common friction points include advice about children, holiday arrangements, money, and how much contact is expected.

Being caught between a partner and a parent is painful. A widely recommended approach is for the couple to agree a position privately first, and for each person to communicate it to their own family. This protects the partner from being cast as the outsider.

In-law relationships can also be deeply valuable, especially when children arrive. Many grandparents provide practical help and warmth. Recognizing what is good alongside what is hard helps keep the relationship in balance.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on in-law relationships is smaller than on couples, but studies generally link in-law conflict with lower marital satisfaction. Studies also suggest that feeling your partner backs you up in dealings with your own family, and theirs, is linked to feeling closer as a couple. Most of this research is correlational and based on Western samples, and family norms vary a great deal across cultures.

Parenting as a team

Also known as: coparenting, co-parenting, parenting together, parenting disagreements, team parenting, parenting teamwork.

In a nutshell

Co-parenting is how parents work together: agreeing on approaches, supporting each other, sharing the work and handling disagreements. Research suggests the quality of co-parenting matters for both children and the couple.

Going deeper

Mark Feinberg described several parts of co-parenting: agreement on childrearing, support versus undermining, division of labor, and how the family is managed together. Couples can love each other and still co-parent poorly, or be distant and still co-parent well.

Undermining (contradicting the other parent in front of the children, or competing for the children’s favor) tends to cause trouble. Support (backing each other up, talking disagreements through privately) tends to help.

Parents often disagree on bedtimes, screens, discipline or food. Resolving these calmly, away from the children, and presenting a shared approach reduces conflict for everyone. The same tools as other disagreements apply: interests over positions, one topic at a time, and a regular check-in.

What the research says, and its limits

Feinberg published his framework in 2003 and developed the Family Foundations program, which in randomised trials improved co-parenting and some child outcomes. Research generally finds that co-parenting quality is linked with children’s adjustment, and that parents’ conflict, particularly hostile conflict that children witness, is associated with more difficulties for children.

Parenting styles

Also known as: parenting styles, authoritative parenting, authoritarian, permissive, Baumrind, gentle parenting.

In a nutshell

Diana Baumrind described parenting styles based on warmth and structure. Authoritative parenting, high in both, has generally been linked with good outcomes in research, though culture and context matter.

Going deeper

Baumrind described authoritarian parents (high control, low warmth), permissive parents (high warmth, low control) and authoritative parents (high warmth and clear, reasoned expectations). Eleanor Maccoby and John Martin later added neglectful or uninvolved parenting (low on both).

Partners often lean toward different styles, often influenced by their own upbringing. One may be firmer, the other more lenient. This can lead to a good-cop, bad-cop dynamic that frustrates both.

Talking about the values behind each person’s approach (safety, independence, kindness, respect) often reveals more agreement than the surface disagreements suggest.

What the research says, and its limits

Baumrind published her work from 1966 onward; Maccoby and Martin extended it in 1983. Many studies link authoritative parenting with positive child outcomes, particularly in Western, middle-class samples. Research in other cultures shows that the effects of different styles can vary, and that styles are broad descriptions rather than precise categories.

When a baby arrives

Also known as: new baby, becoming parents, transition to parenthood, new parents, after having a baby.

In a nutshell

Many couples report a dip in relationship satisfaction after a first baby arrives, alongside new joys. It isn’t universal, and it is often linked to sleep loss, less couple time and a shift in who does what.

Going deeper

A new baby brings enormous change: broken sleep, new tasks, less free time, changes in work, and often a drift toward more traditional divisions of labor, even in couples who expected to share equally.

Research suggests satisfaction tends to decline on average, and that the decline is steeper for couples who didn’t plan the pregnancy or who were already struggling. Many couples recover, and many report the relationship deepening in new ways.

Talking early about how work will be shared, before and after the baby arrives, can help prevent drift. So can protecting small moments of connection, even ten minutes a day, and being gentle about how tired everyone is.

What the research says, and its limits

Brian Doss, Galena Rhoades, Scott Stanley and Howard Markman (2009) followed couples over eight years and found declines in satisfaction after the birth of a first child, on average. Jean Twenge, Keith Campbell and Craig Foster’s 2003 meta-analysis found parents reported lower marital satisfaction than non-parents, with larger differences among mothers of infants. Programs like Gottman’s Bringing Baby Home and Feinberg’s Family Foundations aim to support couples through this transition.

Stepfamilies and blended families

Also known as: stepfamily, blended family, step parent, stepchildren, remarriage.

In a nutshell

Stepfamilies face particular challenges: insiders and outsiders, loyalty binds for children, and different family cultures. Research suggests building relationships slowly and letting the biological parent lead on discipline at first.

Going deeper

Patricia Papernow described stepfamily “architecture”: the biological parent and child have an established bond; the stepparent arrives as an outsider. Children may feel torn between parents. Each family brings its own habits and history.

A common recommendation is for the stepparent to build a warm relationship first, more like a friendly adult, while the biological parent handles discipline, at least in the early years.

Stepfamilies often take several years to feel settled. Expecting instant closeness can lead to disappointment. Patience, clear communication and realistic expectations help.

What the research says, and its limits

Papernow’s Surviving and Thriving in Stepfamily Relationships (2013) draws on research and clinical experience. Research by Mavis Hetherington, Lawrence Ganong and Marilyn Coleman, among others, has explored stepfamily dynamics. Findings suggest outcomes vary widely, and that the quality of relationships matters more than family structure.

What money means to us

Also known as: money, money scripts, money beliefs, financial stress, saving and spending, financial values.

In a nutshell

Money carries meanings beyond numbers: security, freedom, status, love. Differences in these meanings, often learned in childhood, are behind many money disagreements.

Going deeper

Some people see money as safety and prefer to save; others see it as freedom and prefer to spend on experiences. Some feel anxious talking about money; others are comfortable. These differences often trace back to family experiences: scarcity, abundance, conflict or silence about money.

Financial stress is also a common strain on relationships. When money is tight, every decision can feel high-stakes. Research suggests that how couples talk about money, and whether they feel they are a team, matters a great deal.

Practical structures, such as regular money check-ins, agreed spending thresholds and personal discretionary amounts, reduce friction. So does understanding what money means to each person.

What the research says, and its limits

Brad Klontz and colleagues have described “money scripts,” though their measures are more recent and less established. Research on financial stress and relationships, including work by Jeffrey Dew and by Rand Conger and colleagues on economic hardship, links financial pressure to relationship strain and conflict. Research by Scott Rick, Deborah Small and Eli Finkel (2011) found that people often marry those with opposite attitudes to spending, which can lead to conflict.

Life transitions: Schlossberg’s four S’s

Also known as: life changes, transitions, moving house, new job, changes, coping with change, four S.

In a nutshell

Nancy Schlossberg’s model says how people cope with a transition depends on four S’s: the Situation, the Self, their Support and their Strategies. It is a practical way to think through any big change.

Going deeper

Transitions include moving, changing jobs, retirement, illness, children leaving home, or any change that alters roles and routines. Schlossberg suggested looking at the situation (what is changing, is it chosen, how long will it last?), the self (personal strengths and past experience), support (people and resources) and strategies (ways of coping).

In couples, transitions often shift the division of labor without anyone deciding. A new job for one person may leave the other carrying more at home. A move may mean the person who is home more does most of the unpacking and setting up.

Using a transition as an explicit moment to revisit who does what, such as a fresh-start review, can prevent a temporary imbalance from becoming permanent.

What the research says, and its limits

Schlossberg published her model in 1981 and developed it in later work. It is widely used in counselling and career development. It is a practical framework rather than a tested predictive model, but it fits research on coping resources and social support.

Ambiguous loss

Also known as: ambiguous loss, loss without closure, someone present but changed, Pauline Boss.

In a nutshell

Ambiguous loss is loss without clear closure: someone physically present but psychologically changed, or psychologically present but physically absent. Pauline Boss described how this uncertainty complicates grief.

Going deeper

Examples include a family member with dementia, a loved one who has emigrated, or a relationship that has changed. There is no clear ending, no ritual, and often little recognition from others.

Boss suggests that the aim is not closure, which may be impossible, but learning to live with the ambiguity: holding two ideas at once (“they are here and not here”), finding meaning, and adjusting roles.

For carers and families, naming ambiguous loss can be a relief. It explains why grief can feel stuck or confusing, and why others may not understand.

What the research says, and its limits

Boss developed the concept from the 1970s and published Ambiguous Loss in 1999. It is widely used in family therapy and in supporting families of people with dementia. It is a conceptual framework, supported by clinical and qualitative research.

Caring for ageing parents

Also known as: ageing parents, aging parents, elderly parents, parent care, sandwich generation, siblings and care.

In a nutshell

Caring for an ageing parent brings practical work, emotional complexity and often sibling tension. Planning and dividing care explicitly, as for any shared project, can reduce strain.

Going deeper

Care for ageing parents often falls unevenly among siblings: the one who lives closest, or who is seen as the caring one, ends up doing most. This can create resentment and reopen old family dynamics.

The same tools that help households help here: listing all the tasks (appointments, finances, visits, calls), giving each an owner, and reviewing regularly. Siblings who live far away can own tasks that can be done remotely, like managing bills or researching services.

For couples, a partner’s caring role changes the household. Talking about what has shifted, and adjusting other responsibilities, helps prevent the carer from being overwhelmed.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on family caregiving, including meta-analyses by Martin Pinquart and Silvia Sörensen, finds that caregiving is associated with more stress, and that support from family matters. Research on sibling caregiving (for example by Jill Suitor and Karl Pillemer) finds that unequal division is common and linked to tension.

Holidays and family gatherings

Also known as: holidays, Christmas, family gatherings, visiting family, holiday stress, festive season.

In a nutshell

Holidays combine high expectations, extra work, family dynamics and disrupted routines. Planning together, sharing the work and agreeing on limits can make them more enjoyable.

Going deeper

Holidays often come with a hidden load: presents, food, travel, cards, hosting. This work tends to fall on whoever usually does the planning, which can make holidays exhausting for one person and relaxing for another.

Family gatherings can also bring back old roles: adult children may slip into childhood patterns, and old tensions may resurface.

Practical steps: list all the holiday tasks and share them; agree on how much time to spend with each side of the family; decide in advance how to handle difficult topics; and build in rest.

What the research says, and its limits

Research specifically on holiday stress is limited, though surveys often report increased stress around major holidays. Research on the division of household labor, emotional labor and family roles applies. Anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo’s 1987 essay described “kin work”: the cards, calls, gifts and gatherings that keep extended families connected, which she found was done mostly by women in the families she studied.

Sharing a home with housemates

Also known as: roommates, housemates, flatmates, shared house, living with friends.

In a nutshell

Housemates face the same issues as couples, like chores, money, noise and guests, often without the same closeness to smooth things over. Clear agreements made early prevent most problems.

Going deeper

Housemate conflicts often grow from small things: dishes left in the sink, noise at night, a guest who stays too long. Without clear agreements, each person relies on their own assumptions.

Agreements that help include a chore rota with clear owners, rules for shared costs, quiet hours, guest policies, and a regular short house meeting. Writing these down makes them fairer and easier to revisit.

Diffusion of responsibility is a particular risk in shared houses: when a job belongs to everyone, it belongs to no one. Assigning owners is especially helpful.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on roommates, often with university students, finds that conflict is common and that communication and clear expectations help. The general research on diffusion of responsibility and procedural fairness applies well to shared homes.

How priorities change across life

Also known as: socioemotional selectivity, getting older, priorities, ageing, older adults, life stages.

In a nutshell

Laura Carstensen’s socioemotional selectivity theory suggests that as people sense time is limited, they prioritize emotionally meaningful relationships and experiences. This helps explain shifting priorities across life.

Going deeper

Younger people, feeling time is open-ended, often seek new experiences and broad networks. Older people, or anyone facing a sense of limited time, tend to focus on fewer, closer relationships and on emotionally satisfying experiences.

This shift can explain some differences between partners or generations. It also suggests that older adults often report good emotional wellbeing, a pattern sometimes called the positivity effect.

For couples at different life stages, recognizing these shifts can help: one partner may want to slow down and deepen, the other to explore.

What the research says, and its limits

Carstensen developed the theory from the early 1990s, with a key 1999 paper with Derek Isaacowitz and Susan Charles. Research supports that time perspective shapes social goals and that older adults often show a positivity effect in attention and memory. The effect sizes and generality are still studied.

Comparing ourselves to others

Also known as: social comparison, comparing relationships, social media, keeping up, envy, other couples.

In a nutshell

People naturally compare themselves with others to judge how they are doing. Comparisons with others who seem better off, especially curated images online, can leave people feeling worse about their own relationships and homes.

Going deeper

Leon Festinger proposed that people evaluate themselves by comparing with others. Upward comparisons (with those who seem better off) can motivate but often lower mood; downward comparisons can comfort but can also breed complacency.

Social media makes upward comparison constant. Other couples’ holidays, other families’ tidy homes and other people’s milestones are displayed without the arguments, mess or struggles.

It helps to remember that most people show their best moments. Comparing your behind-the-scenes to others’ highlights is an unfair comparison.

What the research says, and its limits

Festinger published social comparison theory in 1954. Research on social media and wellbeing has found small average associations between some kinds of use and lower wellbeing, with debate about causation and effect size. Studies suggest passive scrolling and upward comparison are more linked to negative feelings than active, connecting use.

Getting used to things

Also known as: hedonic adaptation, hedonic treadmill, getting used to good things, novelty wears off, taking for granted.

In a nutshell

Hedonic adaptation is the tendency to get used to changes, good and bad, so their emotional impact fades. It helps people cope with hardship, and also explains why good things can come to be taken for granted.

Going deeper

Philip Brickman and colleagues’ famous study compared lottery winners and people who had become paralysed, finding smaller differences in happiness than expected. The study was small and has been debated, but the broader idea of adaptation is well supported.

In relationships, adaptation means that kindness can become invisible. A partner who always makes the coffee may stop being thanked, because it has become normal.

Counteracting adaptation involves noticing the good deliberately (gratitude, savouring), adding variety, and occasionally imagining life without something you take for granted.

What the research says, and its limits

Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman published the lottery study in 1978. Later research, for example by Richard Lucas, found that some life events (like disability or unemployment) can have lasting effects, so adaptation is not complete for everything. Sonja Lyubomirsky has written about slowing adaptation to positive changes.

Work, home and the spillover between them

Also known as: work life balance, work family conflict, spillover, bringing work home, long hours.

In a nutshell

Work and home affect each other. Stress from work can spill into home life, and home stress can spill into work. Recognizing spillover helps couples understand each other’s moods and plan support.

Going deeper

Researchers describe work–family conflict in both directions: work interfering with family (long hours, stress, emails at night) and family interfering with work (caring responsibilities, household crises).

Spillover also happens between partners: one person’s stressful day can affect the other’s evening, a process called crossover. Rena Repetti’s research found, for example, that after stressful workdays some parents were more withdrawn at home.

Transitions between work and home help: a walk, changing clothes, a few minutes of quiet, or simply saying “I’ve had a rough day; give me twenty minutes.” These small rituals can prevent work stress from becoming household friction.

What the research says, and its limits

Jeffrey Greenhaus and Nicholas Beutell described work–family conflict in 1985. Repetti’s studies of daily spillover were published from 1989 onward. Meta-analyses link work–family conflict with lower job and life satisfaction. Research on boundary management (Ellen Kossek and colleagues) explores how people separate or blend work and home.

How it connects to the program

  • Fix the setup, not the person (systems thinking). In you: see your habits and routines as a setup you can redesign, not a character flaw. Between you and others: use clear owners, handoffs and agreements instead of blame.

Making big decisions together

Also known as: joint decisions, deciding together, big decisions, shared decision making, moving, buying a house.

In a nutshell

Big joint decisions go better when both people’s interests are laid out, options are explored before choosing, and the process feels fair. Rushing or letting one person decide tends to leave resentment.

Going deeper

Big decisions, like where to live, whether to change jobs, or how to handle a family situation, often involve different priorities. One may prioritize security, the other opportunity; one may want to decide fast, the other slowly.

A structured approach helps: each person lists what matters to them, options are explored together, and criteria are agreed before choosing. Decision researchers also recommend considering more than one option, rather than a yes/no on a single idea.

Fair process matters as much as the outcome. Even when one person’s preference wins, feeling heard and involved makes it easier to commit.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on decision-making by Chip and Dan Heath (Decisive, 2013) and Paul Nutt found that decisions considering more than one option tend to go better than single-option decisions. Nutt’s research on organizational decisions found “whether or not” decisions failed more often. Procedural justice research supports the value of voice in joint decisions.

How it connects to the program

Where it sits in the Five Pillars: Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive.

  • Fix the setup, not the person (systems thinking). In you: see your habits and routines as a setup you can redesign, not a character flaw. Between you and others: use clear owners, handoffs and agreements instead of blame.
  • Tune how you send and receive (signal theory, the Frequency Framework). In you: know your own wiring, pace and how you hear things. Between you and others: translate across different wiring and tone; a mismatch is tuning, not a moral failing.

Talking with teenagers

Also known as: teenagers, teens, adolescents, parenting teens, teenage son, teenage daughter, adolescence.

In a nutshell

Adolescence is a time of growing independence, and some extra friction with parents is normal. Research suggests warmth, curiosity and clear, fair rules, with a gradual handover of responsibility, tend to work better than control or lectures.

Going deeper

Teenagers are working on becoming their own people. They push for autonomy, care more about peers, and often want privacy. Their body clocks also shift later, which is why mornings can be hard. None of this means the relationship is failing; it is part of growing up.

Conversations tend to go better side by side rather than face to face: in the car, cooking, walking. Asking open questions and listening more than advising keeps the door open. Lectures and interrogations tend to close it.

The program’s ideas about ownership apply well here. Handing a teenager real ownership of some household jobs, with agreed standards and room to do it their way, builds competence and reduces nagging. Explaining the reasons behind rules, and inviting their view, supports the sense of autonomy that motivation research says matters so much.

What the research says, and its limits

A 1998 meta-analysis by Brett Laursen, Katherine Coy and W. Andrew Collins found that parent–child conflict tends to become less frequent across adolescence, while conflicts in mid-adolescence can be more intense. Laurence Steinberg’s research describes adolescence as a period of growing autonomy in which warm, firm parenting is linked with better outcomes on average. Research on self-determination theory supports autonomy-supportive parenting. As with all parenting research, families and cultures differ.
