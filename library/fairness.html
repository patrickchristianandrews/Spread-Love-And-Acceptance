Fairness and the mental load at home

14 entriesAll library themes

A gentle note: this library is general education about how people tend to think, feel and get along. It is not diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t know your situation. Where the evidence is thin, mixed or debated, each entry says so.

At the heart of the program is a simple observation: work that nobody sees still has to be done, and when it builds up unevenly, it quietly turns into resentment. Sociologists and psychologists have been studying this for decades. These entries set out what they found, including a few surprises about how our own memories tilt the picture.

Equity theory

Also known as: equity, fairness in relationships, getting back what you put in, Adams.

In a nutshell

Equity theory says people feel most comfortable when what they get out of a relationship is in proportion to what they put in, compared with the other person. Feeling under-benefited leads to resentment; feeling over-benefited can lead to guilt.

Going deeper

J. Stacy Adams developed equity theory to explain feelings about pay at work: people compare their ratio of inputs (effort, skill, time) to outcomes (pay, recognition) with someone else’s. When the ratios don’t match, they feel tension and try to restore balance, by working less, asking for more, or changing how they see things.

Elaine Hatfield (writing as Elaine Walster) and colleagues applied the idea to close relationships. They found that people who felt under-benefited reported anger and resentment, while those who felt over-benefited sometimes reported guilt. Both kinds of imbalance were associated with less satisfaction than feeling things were roughly fair.

In a household, the “inputs” include paid work, chores, planning, emotional support and more, and different people weigh them differently. That is why fairness conversations work better when both people’s full picture of contributions is on the table.

What the research says, and its limits

Adams published the theory in 1965; Walster, Walster and Berscheid’s Equity: Theory and Research appeared in 1978. Equity theory is well supported in workplace research. In close relationships, the evidence is more mixed: some researchers argue that in loving relationships people focus less on tallies and more on meeting each other’s needs (see communal relationships), and that intense scorekeeping is itself a sign of trouble.

Communal and exchange relationships

Also known as: keeping score, tit for tat, communal relationships, exchange relationships, Clark and Mills.

In a nutshell

In exchange relationships, people give expecting something back. In communal relationships, people give because the other person has a need. Close relationships tend to work better on communal lines, but fairness still matters underneath.

Going deeper

Margaret Clark and Judson Mills noticed that people behave differently with friends and family than with acquaintances or business partners. In communal relationships, people respond to needs without tracking who owes what; being paid back immediately can even feel insulting.

This helps explain why keeping score in a relationship feels wrong. But communal doesn’t mean anything goes. Communal relationships rely on both people being responsive to each other’s needs. When one person keeps giving and the other doesn’t notice, the communal spirit wears thin.

The program’s approach tries to respect both truths: writing down who did what isn’t about tallying debts, it is about making needs and effort visible so that both people can respond to them.

What the research says, and its limits

Clark and Mills published their key paper in 1979 and have continued to refine the theory. Experimental studies support the distinction: in communal settings, people track each other’s needs rather than contributions. Later research suggests that in long-term relationships, a background sense of fairness still matters for satisfaction.

The second shift

Also known as: second shift, working parents, housework after work, Hochschild.

In a nutshell

The second shift is sociologist Arlie Hochschild’s name for the unpaid work at home (housework and childcare) that many working people, historically women, do after their paid job ends.

Going deeper

Hochschild and Anne Machung interviewed dual-earner couples in the 1980s and found that in many, women did considerably more of the housework and childcare, adding up to a substantial extra load over a year. Many of these couples believed they shared fairly; Hochschild described the stories couples told themselves to avoid confronting the imbalance.

Since then, men’s share of domestic work has grown in many countries, and the picture varies a lot across households. But large time-use surveys still commonly find gaps, particularly in routine daily tasks and childcare.

The point for any household, whatever its makeup, is that unpaid work is real work, and that couples can drift into arrangements neither person consciously chose.

What the research says, and its limits

The Second Shift was published in 1989 and remains influential. Its methods were qualitative (in-depth interviews and home observations), so its figures describe its sample. Large-scale time-use studies since then confirm that gaps have narrowed but persist in many countries, with differences by country, class and family type. Research on same-sex couples finds they tend to divide work more evenly, which suggests gender norms rather than inevitable differences play a big role.

The mental load

Also known as: mental load, cognitive load at home, remembering everything, household manager, default parent.

In a nutshell

The mental load is the ongoing work of remembering, planning and keeping track of what a household needs. It is often invisible, because it happens in someone’s head rather than with their hands.

Going deeper

Doing the laundry takes an hour. Noticing the laundry basket is full, remembering that school uniforms need to be clean by Monday, checking there is detergent and planning when to fit it in is a different kind of work, and it doesn’t stop when the washing machine does.

The term spread widely after a 2017 comic by the French artist Emma, which showed a woman as the household “project manager” whose partner helped when asked. The comic resonated because being asked is itself part of the load: someone had to notice, plan and delegate.

People carrying the mental load often describe feeling that they can never switch off. The person who helps when asked often feels unfairly criticized, because they are doing tasks and don’t see the invisible part. Both perspectives make sense, which is why making the thinking work visible can be such a relief.

What the research says, and its limits

Allison Daminger’s 2019 study (below) gives the concept research grounding. Survey research has also found that many people, particularly mothers in heterosexual couples, report doing most of the planning and organizing in their households. The popular term “mental load” overlaps with research concepts like cognitive labor and invisible work, but isn’t itself a formal construct.

Cognitive labor: anticipate, identify, decide, monitor

Also known as: cognitive labor, Daminger, planning work, household planning, thinking work.

In a nutshell

Sociologist Allison Daminger broke household “thinking work” into four stages: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding, and monitoring results. In her study, one partner often did more of the anticipating and monitoring.

Going deeper

Daminger interviewed couples in detail about how they managed everyday life. She found that cognitive labor could be divided into distinct parts. Anticipating means noticing a need before it becomes urgent (the child will need new shoes soon). Identifying means researching options. Deciding means choosing. Monitoring means checking that it happened and worked.

Deciding was often shared, and was the most visible part. Anticipating and monitoring were often done mostly by one person, frequently the woman in the heterosexual couples she studied, and were the least visible. This meant a couple could feel they “made decisions together” while one person carried most of the thinking.

This maps neatly onto the program’s idea of ownership: a job isn’t fully handed over until the new owner also does the noticing and checking.

What the research says, and its limits

Daminger’s paper “The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor” was published in the American Sociological Review in 2019. It was based on in-depth interviews with 35 couples, mostly college-educated, so it offers a detailed picture rather than population estimates. Later survey work by Daminger and others has explored the same dimensions in larger samples.

Invisible work

Also known as: invisible labor, invisible labor, unseen work, unpaid work, unrecognised effort.

In a nutshell

Invisible work is effort that is necessary but goes unnoticed, often because it prevents problems rather than creating something visible. When it is noticed at all, it is usually when it stops being done.

Going deeper

Sociologist Arlene Kaplan Daniels coined the term for work that is done but not recognized as work, such as volunteering, hosting and much of the care that keeps families and communities running. The concept has since been applied to many kinds of effort: keeping peace in a family, remembering birthdays, making a team function smoothly.

A defining feature of invisible work is that success looks like nothing happening. The fridge never runs out; the birthday card arrives; the argument doesn’t happen. So the effort isn’t seen, and can easily be undervalued even by the person doing it.

The program calls this unbilled work. Writing it down isn’t about claiming credit. It is about making the full picture visible so that decisions about sharing can be based on reality.

What the research says, and its limits

Daniels published “Invisible Work” in Social Problems in 1987. The concept is widely used in sociology, including in research on housework, care work and workplace “office housework.” It is a conceptual framework supported by many qualitative studies rather than a single measurable variable.

Emotional labor

Also known as: emotional labor, managing feelings, keeping the peace, emotion work, Hochschild.

In a nutshell

Emotional labor originally meant managing your feelings as part of a job, like a flight attendant staying cheerful. The term is now also used, more loosely, for the work of managing relationships and moods at home.

Going deeper

Arlie Hochschild introduced the term in The Managed Heart, studying flight attendants who were trained to display warmth regardless of how they felt. She distinguished surface acting (faking a feeling) from deep acting (trying to actually feel it), and argued that both could be tiring. She used “emotion work” for similar efforts in private life.

In popular use, emotional labor now also covers things like remembering what people like, smoothing tensions, anticipating moods and keeping everyone happy. Some writers, including Hochschild herself, have cautioned that stretching the term too far can blur useful distinctions.

Whatever the label, the effort is real. The person who always notices when someone is upset, or who manages family relationships, is doing work that deserves to be seen and shared.

What the research says, and its limits

The Managed Heart was published in 1983. Workplace research since then, including meta-analyses, links surface acting with emotional exhaustion more than deep acting. Research on “emotion work” in families is smaller but supports the idea that it is often unevenly shared and linked to feelings of fairness.

Why we each think we do more

Also known as: egocentric bias, overestimating your own share, responsibility bias, Ross and Sicoly, who does more.

In a nutshell

When couples each estimate their share of household work, their estimates usually add up to more than 100 percent. We remember our own efforts more easily than other people’s, so we tend to overestimate our share without meaning to.

Going deeper

This isn’t about dishonesty. You were there for every one of your own tasks: you remember the effort, the timing, the inconvenience. You only saw some of the other person’s tasks, and you didn’t feel the effort of any of them. So your memory is richer for your own contributions.

The effect has been found in many settings: couples, sports teams, co-authors of research papers. In one study of academic authors, the sum of each author’s estimated share on a paper was often well over 100 percent.

This is one of the strongest arguments for writing things down. A shared log corrects the natural tilt of memory, gently and without blaming anyone. It also means that when both people feel they do more, both can be honest and still be partly mistaken.

What the research says, and its limits

Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly published the classic study in 1979: spouses’ estimates of their own responsibility for household activities typically summed to more than 100 percent. The bias has been replicated in many contexts, including Eugene Caruso, Nicholas Epley and Max Bazerman’s 2006 studies of academic co-authors. Research also suggests that considering the other person’s contributions in detail reduces the bias.

Housework, fairness and satisfaction

Also known as: division of household labor, household labor, who does the housework, fair split, 50 50.

In a nutshell

Research consistently links the division of housework to relationship satisfaction. What seems to matter most is not an exact 50/50 split but whether both people feel the arrangement is fair.

Going deeper

Different couples arrive at different arrangements, and many uneven splits feel fine to both people, for example when one partner works much longer paid hours, or genuinely prefers certain jobs. Resentment tends to grow when one person feels the arrangement is unfair, unchosen or unacknowledged.

Several things seem to shape perceived fairness: whether the arrangement was discussed, whether each person’s contributions are appreciated, whether the split matches what each thinks is right, and whether the less enjoyable jobs are shared.

This is why the program focuses less on hours and more on visibility, ownership and conversation. Seeing the full picture and agreeing it together are what make an arrangement feel fair.

What the research says, and its limits

A long line of studies, in the United States and elsewhere, finds associations between perceived fairness in housework and marital satisfaction, conflict and wellbeing. Research by Sanjiv Gupta, Scott Coltrane, Paula England and others has explored how gender, income and time shape the division. A 2016 study by Daniel Carlson and colleagues found that egalitarian arrangements were associated with higher relationship and sexual satisfaction than they had been in older data. Most of this research is correlational.

Gatekeeping: when standards keep others out

Also known as: maternal gatekeeping, doing it my way, micromanaging chores, standards, redoing tasks.

In a nutshell

Gatekeeping describes how the person who usually does a job can, without meaning to, discourage others from doing it: by redoing it, criticizing the method, or holding tight to a particular standard.

Going deeper

Imagine one person always packs the children’s lunches. When the other tries, they are told the sandwich is wrong and the snack is missing. Next time, the second person doesn’t bother. The first person then feels they have to do everything. Both are frustrated, and neither intended this.

Gatekeeping can come from caring a lot about how something is done, from identity (“this is my area”), or from worry. It can also be positive, as “gate opening,” when the experienced person actively invites and supports the other.

The program’s approach to ownership helps here: once a job has an owner, the owner decides how it is done, within agreed “good enough” standards. Handing over a job means handing over the method too.

What the research says, and its limits

Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins described maternal gatekeeping in 1999. Later research, including work by Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, has refined the concept to include both gate-closing and gate-opening behaviors and found links with fathers’ involvement. Researchers caution against using the concept to blame mothers; gatekeeping is shaped by expectations on both partners and by wider social norms.

“Weaponised incompetence” and learned helplessness at home

Also known as: weaponized incompetence, strategic incompetence, pretending not to know how, doing it badly on purpose.

In a nutshell

“Weaponised incompetence” is a popular term for doing a task badly so you won’t be asked again. It names something real, but it is not a research concept, and it is often a misreading of inexperience or mismatched standards.

Going deeper

The idea spread on social media: someone shrinks the jumper in the wash, and so never gets asked to do the laundry again. It resonated because many people have experienced tasks drifting back to them.

But it is worth being careful with the label. Often what looks deliberate is actually inexperience (the person hasn’t built the skill), unclear standards (nobody said how it should be done), or gatekeeping (their attempts were corrected until they stopped trying). Assigning bad intent tends to make conversations defensive.

A more useful approach is practical: agree on the standard, allow a learning period, hand over the whole job including the thinking, and let the owner own it. If the pattern continues after that, it is a conversation about fairness, not about skill.

What the research says, and its limits

There is no established research literature on “weaponised incompetence” as such. Related concepts include social loafing (reduced effort in groups), research on gender and housework skills, and gatekeeping. Attribution research warns that assuming bad intentions from others’ behavior is a common and often mistaken bias.

The default person

Also known as: default parent, go-to person, the one who always handles it, primary caregiver.

In a nutshell

In many households one person becomes the default: the one the school calls, who knows the dentist’s number, who everyone asks where things are. Being the default is a quiet, constant load.

Going deeper

Default status often starts for practical reasons: one person was home more, or took parental leave, or is simply the one who remembers. Over time it becomes self-reinforcing: the default knows more, so they get asked more, so they know even more.

Being the default can feel like being permanently on call. It also affects careers and rest, because interruptions land on one person.

Changing it takes deliberate steps: giving the other person whole areas to own, updating contact lists, and sometimes letting things wobble while the new owner learns. The program’s ownership worksheet is designed partly for exactly this.

What the research says, and its limits

“Default parent” is a popular phrase rather than a research term, but it describes patterns that research on cognitive labor, the mental load and time use has documented, such as one partner doing more planning, monitoring and interruption-absorbing. Studies of work interruptions show that frequent switching carries a cost to focus and energy.

Noticing: the first step of every job

Also known as: noticing what needs doing, seeing mess, different thresholds, not noticing.

In a nutshell

People differ in when they notice something needs doing: some see crumbs immediately; others genuinely don’t register them until much later. Different noticing thresholds cause a lot of household friction.

Going deeper

If one person notices mess at a lower threshold, they will almost always be the one who deals with it first. Over time they do more, feel resentful, and may assume the other person is lazy. The other person, genuinely not seeing the mess yet, feels nagged about something that doesn’t seem urgent.

Thresholds can be influenced by upbringing, personality, neurotype and what each person has been responsible for in the past. They can shift with practice and ownership: people tend to notice what they are responsible for.

This is why assigning ownership often works better than asking someone to “notice more.” An owner has a reason to look. Schedules, lists and visible cues also help by taking noticing out of anyone’s head.

What the research says, and its limits

Differences in standards and noticing are discussed in sociological research on housework, including Daminger’s anticipation stage of cognitive labor. Research on attention shows that people notice what is relevant to their goals, which supports the practical value of ownership. The idea of a personal “mess threshold” is a helpful description rather than a formal measure.

Recognition and appreciation

Also known as: feeling appreciated, recognition, being thanked, unappreciated, acknowledgement.

In a nutshell

Feeling that your contributions are seen and valued is one of the strongest predictors of feeling that things are fair. Recognition costs little and changes a lot.

Going deeper

People often put up with a lot of work when they feel appreciated for it. The same load, unacknowledged, feels much heavier. Recognition can be a thank-you, a specific comment (“I noticed you sorted the car insurance, thank you”), or simply the other person knowing what was done.

Specific appreciation tends to land better than general praise. It shows the effort was actually seen.

Appreciation isn’t a substitute for fair sharing, though. Thanking someone for carrying everything while leaving the arrangement unchanged can start to feel hollow. The best results come from both: seeing the work and sharing it.

What the research says, and its limits

Research by Amie Gordon and colleagues (2012) found that people who felt more appreciated by their partners were more appreciative in return and more committed to their relationships. Workplace research also links recognition with engagement. Studies of household labor suggest that appreciation is one of the factors shaping whether an uneven split feels fair.
