Talking and listening: communication in relationships

18 entriesAll library themes

A gentle note: this library is general education about how people tend to think, feel and get along. It is not diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t know your situation. Where the evidence is thin, mixed or debated, each entry says so.

Most misunderstandings aren’t about bad intentions. They come from the gap between what one person means and what the other person hears. These entries collect what researchers and practitioners have learned about closing that gap: how to listen, how to say hard things kindly, how to ask good questions, and why tone is so easy to lose in a text message.

Active listening

Also known as: listening skills, how to listen, being a good listener, Carl Rogers.

In a nutshell

Active listening means giving full attention, trying to understand the other person’s meaning and feelings, and showing that understanding, rather than waiting for your turn to speak.

Going deeper

Carl Rogers, one of the founders of humanistic psychology, believed people grow when they feel deeply understood. He described three conditions for helpful relationships: empathy (understanding from the other’s point of view), unconditional positive regard (valuing the person), and congruence (being genuine). Active listening puts these into practice.

In everyday terms, active listening includes putting down the phone, following what the person is actually saying (not what you expect them to say), asking questions that help them say more, and reflecting back what you understand. It also includes noticing feelings, not just facts: “That sounds really frustrating.”

It is harder than it sounds. Most of us listen partly to reply: to fix, advise, defend or relate it to our own story. Those impulses are well-meant, but they can leave the speaker feeling that they weren’t really heard.

What the research says, and its limits

Rogers and Richard Farson’s 1957 essay Active Listening popularised the term. Studies of active listening, such as a 2014 experiment by Harry Weger and colleagues, have found that people whose listeners used active listening responses felt more understood, though effects on other outcomes were smaller. In couples, Gottman’s 1998 research found that formal paraphrasing was rare in real conflicts and did not predict outcomes, which suggests listening skills matter most as a general attitude of interest rather than a rigid technique.

I-statements

Also known as: I statements, I messages, I feel statements, speaking for yourself.

In a nutshell

An I-statement describes your own experience (“I felt worried when you were late and didn’t text”) instead of accusing the other person (“You never think about me”). It is meant to lower defensiveness.

Going deeper

The classic form is: “I feel [feeling] when [specific situation], because [why it matters]. I’d like [request].” The emphasis is on your experience and a specific event, rather than on judgments about the other person’s character.

I-statements can be very helpful, but they are not magic words. “I feel that you are selfish” is a you-statement in disguise. What matters is the spirit behind the words: owning your feelings, being specific, and leaving room for the other person’s view.

They can also feel stiff at first. Many people find their own natural version, such as “Can I tell you what’s been bothering me?” or “Here’s how it landed for me.”

What the research says, and its limits

Thomas Gordon introduced “I-messages” in his Parent Effectiveness Training (1970). Research on I-statements is mixed: some studies found they were received as less hostile than you-statements, while others found that listeners respond more to tone, content and context than to pronouns. They are best seen as one useful tool for a gentler start, not a guarantee.

Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

Also known as: NVC, Marshall Rosenberg, observations feelings needs requests, compassionate communication.

In a nutshell

Nonviolent Communication, developed by Marshall Rosenberg, has four steps: describe what you observed, name your feeling, name the need behind it, and make a clear request. It is popular and useful, though its evidence base is limited and it can feel formulaic.

Going deeper

Rosenberg’s insight was that judgments (“you’re lazy”) hide needs (“I need rest, and I need help”). By separating observations from evaluations, and linking feelings to needs rather than to the other person’s wrongdoing, NVC aims to make it easier for the other person to respond with care.

A full NVC sentence might be: “When I see the laundry still on the floor (observation), I feel discouraged (feeling), because I need some order in the space we share (need). Would you be willing to put it away before dinner? (request).”

NVC also asks the listener to hear the feelings and needs behind the other person’s words, even when those words are harsh. That can be a generous way of listening.

Critics point out that when used rigidly, NVC can sound scripted or even manipulative, as if feelings language is being used to win. It can also underplay real disagreements about what is fair. Many people find it most useful as a way of thinking rather than a script.

What the research says, and its limits

Rosenberg developed NVC from the 1960s and described it in Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life (first published 1999). It is widely used in mediation, schools and workplaces. Reviews of the research have found few rigorous studies: many evaluations are small, uncontrolled or qualitative, although results are generally positive. So it is best described as a promising, widely used practice with a thin evidence base.

Validation

Also known as: validating feelings, feeling validated, invalidation, that makes sense, validate.

In a nutshell

Validation means communicating that someone’s feelings or thoughts make sense, given their situation, even if you don’t agree with every conclusion. It is one of the quickest ways to lower the temperature of a conversation.

Going deeper

Validation is not agreement. You can say “It makes sense you’re frustrated; you were expecting help and it didn’t come” while still having your own view about what happened. What validation says is: your reaction is understandable, and you’re not wrong to have it.

Marsha Linehan described several levels of validation, from simply paying attention, through accurately reflecting, to recognizing how someone’s reaction makes sense given their history or the situation, and finally treating them as a capable equal.

Invalidation is the opposite: “You’re overreacting,” “It’s not a big deal,” “Don’t be so sensitive.” Even when well-meant (as reassurance), these messages can leave people feeling dismissed, and often make the feeling stronger rather than weaker.

What the research says, and its limits

Linehan developed the concept within dialectical behavior therapy in the 1990s, and wrote about levels of validation in 1997. Laboratory studies, including one by Chad Shenk and Alan Fruzzetti (2011), found that being validated during a stressful task was associated with lower negative emotion and lower physiological arousal than being invalidated. The broader idea connects with research on perceived responsiveness in relationships.

Empathy and sympathy

Also known as: empathy, sympathy, compassion, feeling with someone, cognitive empathy, emotional empathy.

In a nutshell

Sympathy is feeling for someone; empathy is trying to feel with them, understanding their experience from the inside. Both can be kind; empathy tends to make people feel less alone.

Going deeper

Researchers distinguish cognitive empathy (understanding what someone thinks and feels) from affective empathy (sharing some of the feeling). Compassion adds a motivation to help, ideally without being swept away by the other person’s distress.

Sympathy, in everyday use, often sounds like “Oh, poor you” or “At least…” It can create distance, as if looking down into someone’s situation. Empathy sounds more like “That sounds really hard. I’m glad you told me.” It doesn’t need to fix anything.

Empathy has limits. It is harder to feel for people who are very different from us, and too much affective empathy can lead to distress and burnout. That is one reason researchers suggest compassion, warm concern combined with some steadiness, may be more sustainable.

What the research says, and its limits

Lauren Wispé wrote an influential 1986 paper distinguishing sympathy and empathy. Mark Davis’s Interpersonal Reactivity Index (1980) measures several facets of empathy. Tania Singer and Olga Klimecki’s research (for example their 2014 review) distinguishes empathic distress from compassion, finding that compassion training may increase positive feelings and helping. A widely shared short animation narrated by Brené Brown made the empathy–sympathy distinction popular; it simplifies the research but captures its spirit.

Talking about how we talk

Also known as: metacommunication, meta-communication, communication about communication, content and relationship.

In a nutshell

Meta-communication is communication about communication: “Can we slow down?”, “I think we’re talking past each other,” “I need you to just listen for a minute.” It lets people fix the conversation, not just the topic.

Going deeper

Paul Watzlawick and colleagues pointed out that every message has a content level (what is said) and a relationship level (what it implies about us). “Did you pay the bill?” is about a bill, but its tone can also say “I don’t trust you” or “I’m worried.” Many arguments are really about the relationship level while being argued at the content level.

Meta-communication shifts the focus: “I think we’re arguing about the bill, but I’m actually feeling like I carry all the admin.” This can open up the real issue.

Agreements about how to talk (like check-in ground rules, when to raise topics, or signals for a pause) are also meta-communication. Making them while calm means both people have a shared toolkit when things get tense.

What the research says, and its limits

Gregory Bateson developed the idea of meta-communication in the 1950s, and Watzlawick, Janet Beavin and Don Jackson set out their axioms, including “one cannot not communicate” and the content–relationship distinction, in Pragmatics of Human Communication (1967). These are foundational ideas in communication theory and family therapy, more conceptual than experimentally tested.

Asking good questions

Also known as: curiosity, open questions, follow-up questions, being curious, conversation skills.

In a nutshell

Good questions, especially open and follow-up questions, show interest and help people say what they really mean. Research suggests people who ask more follow-up questions are liked more.

Going deeper

Open questions (“How did that feel?”, “What was the hardest part?”) invite longer answers than closed ones (“Was it okay?”). Follow-up questions show that you heard the last answer and want to know more.

In conflict, curiosity is a powerful de-escalator. Asking “What’s the most important part of this for you?” or “What am I missing?” signals that you want to understand, not just win. It also often brings up information that changes the picture.

Questions can also be used badly: interrogations, leading questions (“Don’t you think you were being unfair?”), or questions that are really statements. Tone and genuine interest make the difference.

What the research says, and its limits

Karen Huang, Michael Yeomans, Alison Wood Brooks and colleagues (2017) found across several studies that people who asked more questions, particularly follow-up questions, were rated as more likeable by conversation partners. Motivational interviewing, an approach to conversations about change developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick, emphasizes open questions and reflective listening and has a substantial evidence base in health settings.

Why tone gets lost in text

Also known as: texting, text messages, email tone, misread messages, emojis, messaging.

In a nutshell

People overestimate how clearly their tone comes across in writing. Sarcasm, warmth and seriousness are much harder to read in a text or email than the sender thinks.

Going deeper

When you write a message, you hear your own tone of voice in your head. The reader doesn’t. They fill in the gaps with their own mood and expectations, which is where misunderstandings begin. A short reply can read as cold; a joke can read as a dig.

Some practical habits help: re-reading important messages as if you were stressed, adding a word of warmth or context, using emojis where both people read them the same way, and moving sensitive topics to a call or face-to-face conversation.

When receiving, it helps to hold interpretations lightly. If a message seems off, asking (“Did you mean that as a joke?”) is kinder than assuming.

What the research says, and its limits

Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley, Jason Parker and Zhi-Wen Ng (2005) found that people sending emails were overconfident about how well recipients would detect their tone, such as sarcasm or seriousness. Media richness theory (Richard Daft and Robert Lengel, 1986) suggests that richer channels, with voice and face, are better for ambiguous or emotional messages. Research on emojis is newer and suggests they can help convey tone but are also read differently by different people.

Assertiveness

Also known as: assertive, passive, aggressive, passive aggressive, speaking up, standing up for yourself.

In a nutshell

Assertiveness means expressing your needs and views clearly and respectfully, while respecting the other person’s. It sits between passive (not speaking up) and aggressive (speaking up at the other’s expense).

Going deeper

Passive communication avoids conflict in the short term but tends to build resentment. Aggressive communication gets needs heard but damages relationships. Passive-aggressive communication expresses anger indirectly: sighs, sarcasm, forgetting on purpose. Assertiveness aims to be clear, direct and kind.

Assertive statements are usually specific and calm: “I’d like us to split the cooking more evenly. Could we talk about it this weekend?” They don’t apologize for having needs, and they don’t demand.

People from families or cultures that discouraged directness, or who have been punished for speaking up, may find assertiveness feels risky at first. It is a skill that grows with practice, ideally in low-stakes situations first.

What the research says, and its limits

Assertiveness training grew from behavior therapy in the 1950s and 1960s, including the work of Joseph Wolpe, and was popularised by Robert Alberti and Michael Emmons’ Your Perfect Right (1970). Studies of assertiveness training have generally found improvements in self-reported assertiveness and related outcomes, though many are older and small. Cultural norms affect what is seen as appropriately assertive.

Saying no kindly

Also known as: boundaries, how to say no, declining, refusing, turning down requests, overcommitting.

In a nutshell

Saying no protects your time and energy so that your yes means something. A kind no is clear, brief and, where possible, offers what you can do instead.

Going deeper

Many people find it hard to say no because they worry about disappointing others or seeming unhelpful. The result can be overcommitment, exhaustion and quiet resentment, which helps no one.

A kind no often has three parts: acknowledge the request, give a clear answer, and, if you can, offer an alternative. “Thanks for thinking of me. I can’t take that on this week. I could help next Tuesday.” Long justifications often invite negotiation; a short reason is enough.

It also helps to distinguish different kinds of no: “not now,” “not me,” and “not this way.” Knowing which you mean makes the conversation clearer.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on saying no is spread across topics like assertiveness, boundary-setting and self-control. Studies by Vanessa Patrick and Henrik Hagtvedt (2012) found that saying “I don’t” (framed as a personal rule) was more effective at resisting temptation than “I can’t,” an interesting finding about framing, though replication is limited. Research on overcommitment and burnout supports the practical value of protecting capacity.

Boundaries

Also known as: setting boundaries, personal boundaries, limits, healthy boundaries.

In a nutshell

A boundary is a limit you set about what you will do, or what you’ll accept, to protect your wellbeing. Good boundaries describe your own actions, rather than trying to control someone else’s.

Going deeper

The word is used a lot, sometimes loosely. A helpful distinction: a request asks someone else to change (“Please don’t call during work”); a boundary describes what you will do (“I don’t take personal calls during work; I’ll call back at six”). Boundaries are about your choices.

Boundaries are healthy in close relationships. They make it possible to stay close without resentment, because each person isn’t silently overextending.

They work best when communicated calmly and kept consistently. It is also worth checking a boundary isn’t being used as a weapon, for example cutting off conversation whenever something is uncomfortable. Boundaries protect you; they shouldn’t punish the other person.

What the research says, and its limits

“Boundaries” is more a clinical and popular concept than a precisely defined research construct. Related areas are well studied: work–family boundary management (for example Ellen Kossek’s work), assertiveness, and self-determination research on autonomy. Researchers note that the concept is used in many different ways, so it helps to be concrete about what a boundary means in practice.

Giving and receiving feedback

Also known as: feedback, criticism at work, constructive criticism, SBI, feedback sandwich, praise.

In a nutshell

Useful feedback is specific, about behavior rather than character, and given in a way the person can hear. Research shows feedback doesn’t always help; how it is given matters a great deal.

Going deeper

One widely used format is Situation–Behavior–Impact (SBI): describe the situation, the specific behavior, and its impact. “In this morning’s meeting (situation), when you finished my sentence (behavior), I felt I couldn’t make my point (impact).” It keeps feedback concrete.

The “feedback sandwich” (praise, criticism, praise) is popular but debated: the praise can feel insincere, and the main message can get lost. Many practitioners suggest being clear and kind rather than wrapping.

Receiving feedback well is a skill too. Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen describe common triggers: truth triggers (it seems wrong), relationship triggers (who is saying it), and identity triggers (what it means about me). Noticing which is firing can help people listen.

What the research says, and its limits

Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi’s 1996 meta-analysis found that feedback improved performance on average, but in more than a third of cases it actually made performance worse, especially when it drew attention to the self rather than the task. SBI was developed at the Center for Creative Leadership. Stone and Heen’s Thanks for the Feedback (2014) is a practitioner book. Evidence specifically comparing the feedback sandwich with alternatives is limited.

Complaints versus criticism

Also known as: complaining, criticism, how to complain, raising a problem.

In a nutshell

A complaint is about a specific behavior; criticism is about the person’s character. Complaints are healthy and necessary. Criticism tends to make people defensive.

Going deeper

“I was frustrated that the trash weren’t taken out last night” is a complaint. “You never take responsibility for anything” is criticism. The first can be solved; the second invites a defense.

Words like “always” and “never,” and phrases like “what kind of person…”, usually signal criticism. They turn one event into a verdict.

It is important to say that complaints are not a problem. Relationships where people can’t complain tend to build silent resentment. The aim is to complain well: specific, recent, about the behavior, with a request.

What the research says, and its limits

The distinction is central to Gottman’s work; criticism is the first of his four horsemen. Research on attributions in couples, for example by Frank Fincham and Thomas Bradbury, shows that seeing a partner’s negative behavior as a stable trait of the partner (rather than a situation) goes along with lower satisfaction.

Humor in hard moments

Also known as: humor, laughing together, jokes in arguments, playfulness, sarcasm.

In a nutshell

Shared, affectionate humor can defuse tension and repair after conflict. Humor that mocks or belittles has the opposite effect.

Going deeper

Rod Martin described four humor styles: affiliative (humor that brings people together), self-enhancing (seeing the funny side of life), aggressive (humor at others’ expense) and self-defeating (putting yourself down to please others). The first two are associated with better wellbeing and relationships; the other two less so.

In conflict, a well-timed light moment can be a repair attempt. It reminds both people that they are on the same side. But timing matters: joking when the other person feels unheard can seem dismissive.

Sarcasm is a special case: it can be playful between people who share the joke, but in tension it often slides into contempt.

What the research says, and its limits

Martin and colleagues published their Humor Styles Questionnaire in 2003, and much research since links affiliative humor with relationship satisfaction. Laura Kurtz and Sara Algoe (2015) found that shared laughter between romantic partners was associated with feeling closer and more supported.

Directness and indirectness

Also known as: direct communication, blunt, hints, subtext, reading between the lines, literal.

In a nutshell

People differ in how directly they say what they mean. Some say it plainly; some expect the listener to read between the lines. Neither is wrong, but mixing them leads to misunderstandings.

Going deeper

Indirect speakers use hints, context and tone: “Is anyone else cold?” may mean “Please close the window.” Direct speakers say “Please close the window.” To an indirect speaker, directness can sound rude. To a direct speaker, hints can feel unclear or even manipulative.

This difference can come from culture, family, personality or neurotype. Many autistic people, for example, prefer and use direct communication, and may miss hints that others expect to be obvious.

A practical agreement can help: when something matters, say it plainly; when you hear something that sounds blunt, assume it was meant literally rather than harshly.

What the research says, and its limits

Deborah Tannen’s sociolinguistic work (for example That’s Not What I Meant!, 1986) describes conversational styles and indirectness. Edward Hall’s high- and low-context cultures and research on politeness (Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson, 1987) explain why indirectness can be a form of courtesy. The double empathy problem (Damian Milton, 2012) reframes autistic–non-autistic misunderstandings as two-way.

Nonverbal communication

Also known as: body language, tone of voice, facial expression, nonverbal cues, eye contact.

In a nutshell

Tone, facial expression, posture and timing carry much of the emotional meaning of a conversation. But nonverbal signals are less universal and less reliable than popular claims suggest.

Going deeper

When words and tone don’t match (“I’m fine,” said through gritted teeth), people often believe the tone. That is one reason why a kind message said wearily can land as unkind.

Popular claims that “93% of communication is nonverbal” are a misreading of 1960s studies by Albert Mehrabian, which looked at single words spoken with conflicting tones, a very narrow situation. Words matter a lot.

Nonverbal cues are also read differently across people. Eye contact, for example, can signal attention for one person and be uncomfortable for another; many autistic people find eye contact effortful and listen better without it. Assuming that someone’s face or posture means what yours would mean is a common source of misunderstanding.

What the research says, and its limits

Mehrabian’s studies (1967) are often misquoted; he himself cautioned against generalizing them. Research on facial expressions, including debates between Paul Ekman and Lisa Feldman Barrett, shows that expressions are less universal and less reliably linked to specific emotions than once thought. Research on autistic communication supports the idea that nonverbal norms differ across neurotypes.

Advice or support?

Also known as: fixing, giving advice, problem solving, emotional support, do you want help or listening.

In a nutshell

When someone shares a problem, they might want solutions, or they might want to be heard. Offering the wrong one can feel like not being listened to. Asking which is wanted is a simple, powerful habit.

Going deeper

Some people show care by solving problems; others by listening and empathising. Neither is better, but a mismatch can be painful: the fixer feels rejected when advice is brushed off, and the sharer feels dismissed when handed solutions.

Research on social support distinguishes emotional support (comfort, understanding), informational support (advice), and practical support (help with tasks). Different situations and people call for different kinds.

Interestingly, some research suggests support works best when it is almost invisible: help that is given without making a big deal of it, so the person doesn’t feel incapable.

What the research says, and its limits

Niall Bolger and colleagues (2000) studied “invisible support” and found that support recipients weren’t aware of sometimes had better effects on mood than visible support. Later work suggests the effect depends on how the support is given. Research on support matching (for example by Carolyn Cutrona) supports the idea that support works best when it fits what the person needs.

Talking well at work

Also known as: workplace communication, meetings, emails at work, communicating with a boss, colleagues.

In a nutshell

Clear communication at work mostly comes down to a few habits: being specific about what you need and by when, choosing the right channel, and checking understanding.

Going deeper

Many workplace frustrations are really about ambiguity: unclear requests, unclear owners, unclear deadlines. A simple request structure (what, why, by when, and who owns it) prevents a lot of rework.

Choosing the channel matters. Quick facts suit chat; decisions suit a written record; sensitive or emotional topics suit a call or in-person conversation. Media richness research suggests matching the richness of the channel to the complexity of the message.

Closing the loop, confirming what was agreed and who is doing what, is an underrated habit. It is also what the program’s one-owner-per-job worksheet does at home.

What the research says, and its limits

Research on role ambiguity (from Kahn and colleagues, 1964, onward) links unclear expectations with stress. Daft and Lengel’s media richness theory (1986) has been influential, although later research found people adapt to leaner media over time. Psychological safety research (Amy Edmondson) shows that teams where people feel safe to speak up learn more effectively.
