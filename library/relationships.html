20 entriesAll library themes

A gentle note: this library is general education about how people tend to think, feel and get along. It is not diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t know your situation. Where the evidence is thin, mixed or debated, each entry says so.

Relationship science is younger than most people think. Much of it started in the 1970s and 1980s, when researchers began filming real couples talking, following them for years, and asking what separated the ones who stayed close from the ones who drifted. These entries cover the ideas that came out of that work, and the ones the program leans on most.

Bids for connection

Also known as: bids, turning toward, turning away, turning against.

In a nutshell

A bid is any small attempt to get another person’s attention, interest or warmth, like “look at that bird” or a sigh that invites a question. How people respond to these tiny moments, over and over, seems to matter a great deal.

Going deeper

The idea comes from John Gottman and his colleagues, who watched couples spend ordinary time together in an apartment-style lab. They noticed that people constantly reach out in small ways: a comment about the news, a question, a touch, a joke. The other person can turn toward the bid (respond with interest, even briefly), turn away (miss it or ignore it), or turn against it (respond with irritation).

What makes the idea useful is how ordinary it is. Most relationships are not made or broken in big dramatic talks. They are built in hundreds of forgettable exchanges at the kitchen counter. Someone who turns toward bids is, in effect, making lots of small deposits of goodwill. When a harder conversation comes, there is something in the account.

A missed bid is rarely cruelty. It is usually distraction, tiredness, a phone, or simply not recognizing that it was a bid at all. People differ a lot in how they make bids: some are loud and clear, some are hints, some are practical (“I made you tea”) rather than verbal. Part of getting along is learning to read the other person’s way of reaching out.

It also helps to know that turning toward can be tiny. “Mm, that’s interesting” or a look up from the screen counts. It doesn’t need a long conversation.

What the research says, and its limits

The best-known work is Gottman’s observational research, including a study of newlywed couples by Janice Driver and John Gottman (2004) that looked closely at bids during everyday interaction. Couples who were still together years later had turned toward each other’s bids much more often than couples who later separated.

This is a pattern seen in observational data, not an experiment, so it shows an association rather than proving that turning toward causes lasting relationships. The samples were mostly married, American, heterosexual couples, so it is fair to wonder how well it generalizes, though the idea fits well with broader research on responsiveness.

The four horsemen and their antidotes

Also known as: criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling, gentle startup, antidotes.

In a nutshell

Gottman named four ways of arguing that tend to go with relationships getting worse: criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Each has a gentler alternative, sometimes called its antidote.

Going deeper

Criticism is attacking the person rather than raising the problem: “You never think about anyone else” instead of “I was hoping you’d text when you were running late.” The antidote is a gentle startup: describe the situation and what you need, without a verdict on the other person’s character.

Contempt is speaking from above: mockery, sarcasm, eye-rolling, name-calling. It says “I am better than you.” Gottman considers it the most corrosive of the four. The antidote is building a culture of appreciation, noticing and saying what you value, so that respect is the default setting.

Defensiveness is protecting yourself by counter-attacking or playing the innocent victim: “Well, you forgot last week.” It is completely understandable (it is what most of us do when we feel accused), but it tends to escalate things. The antidote is taking responsibility for even a small part: “You’re right, I did forget to call.”

Stonewalling is shutting down: going silent, looking away, leaving without a word. It often happens when someone is overwhelmed (flooded) rather than uncaring. The antidote is physiological self-soothing: taking a real break, saying when you’ll come back, and calming the body before continuing.

A kind way to hold this list: nearly everyone does all four sometimes. The point isn’t to catch the other person doing them. It is to notice your own, and to have somewhere better to go.

What the research says, and its limits

The four horsemen come from Gottman and Robert Levenson’s longitudinal studies of couples in the 1980s and 1990s, summarized in Gottman’s 1994 book What Predicts Divorce?. In those samples, the four behaviors, contempt especially, were associated with later unhappiness and separation.

Gottman has reported very high prediction accuracy from these observations; other researchers have pointed out that some of those figures came from fitting models to the same data they were tested on, which tends to overstate accuracy. The behaviors themselves are well recognized in couples research, and the antidotes are widely taught, but the antidotes have been studied mostly as part of whole therapy programs rather than one at a time.

Repair attempts

Also known as: repair, making up, de-escalation, olive branch.

In a nutshell

A repair attempt is anything that stops a conversation from spiralling: a joke, a touch, “can we start again?”, “I’m getting overwhelmed.” What seems to matter most is not whether people fight, but whether repairs get made and accepted.

Going deeper

Repair attempts are often clumsy. They can be a silly face, an awkward “okay, okay, I hear you,” a hand on the arm, or asking for a pause. What makes them work is less about their polish and more about whether the other person is able to receive them. That depends heavily on how much goodwill there is in the background.

This is one reason small everyday kindness matters for conflict. When the general mood between two people is warm, a repair is heard as a repair. When the mood is sour, the same words can be heard as sarcasm or dodging. Researchers sometimes call this sentiment override: the overall feeling colors how each new message is read.

Repair also happens after the fact. Coming back later to say “I didn’t like how I spoke to you earlier” counts. So does talking about what happened once both people are calm, looking at what each was feeling and needing, without re-arguing it.

It can help to agree on repair phrases in advance, when things are calm. Some couples have a code word or a gesture that means “I’m on your side, let’s slow down.”

What the research says, and its limits

Gottman’s research describes the success or failure of repair attempts as one of the strongest markers his team observed in couples who stayed together. Like much of his work, this comes from observational studies of mostly married couples, so it shows patterns rather than proof of cause. The broader idea that conflict itself isn’t the problem, and that recovery is, is widely shared across couples researchers and therapists.

The 5:1 ratio

Also known as: magic ratio, positive to negative ratio, five to one.

In a nutshell

In Gottman’s studies, stable couples showed roughly five positive moments for every negative one during conflict discussions. It is a pattern observed in his data, not a law or a target to count.

Going deeper

The number is often quoted as if it were a rule of nature: “you need five good things for every bad one.” That is a stretch. What Gottman reported was that, in the conflict conversations he filmed, couples who stayed together tended to show around five times as much positivity (interest, humor, affection, agreement) as negativity. Couples heading for trouble were closer to one-to-one.

What the ratio points to is real and useful: negative moments carry more weight than positive ones, so a relationship needs a lot of warmth to stay balanced. That fits with a broader finding in psychology that bad experiences tend to register more strongly than good ones.

What it does not mean is that you should keep a tally. Counting would turn kindness into accounting, which tends to backfire. It also doesn’t mean negativity is bad in itself: complaints and disagreement are normal and healthy. The ratio is a reminder to keep adding warmth, not a scoreboard.

What the research says, and its limits

The figure comes from Gottman’s 1994 work on couples’ conflict discussions. It describes those samples; it has not been established as a precise threshold that applies to everyone. A different, much-publicised ratio for “flourishing” (the so-called Losada ratio of about 2.9 to 1) was formally corrected after a 2013 critique showed its mathematics didn’t hold up, which is a good reason to treat precise ratios in psychology with care.

Flooding

Also known as: flooded, emotional flooding, diffuse physiological arousal, overwhelmed in an argument.

In a nutshell

Flooding is the state of being so physically stirred up during a conflict that you can’t really listen or think clearly. The heart races, the body gets ready to fight or flee, and the conversation stops being useful.

Going deeper

When someone is flooded, their body is responding as if to a threat. Hearing narrows, patience vanishes, and it becomes hard to take in the other person’s point of view. Things said in this state are often the ones people regret.

People flood at different thresholds. Some flood quickly, especially when tired, hungry or already stressed, and some people’s wiring means they are more sensitive to raised voices or rapid back-and-forth. Men in Gottman’s samples were, on average, somewhat quicker to become flooded and slower to calm, though there is a lot of individual variation.

The most useful response is surprisingly simple: stop and take a proper break. Gottman suggested at least about twenty minutes, spent doing something genuinely calming rather than rehearsing the argument. The key is to say when you’ll come back, so the break doesn’t feel like abandonment.

What the research says, and its limits

Gottman and Levenson measured heart rate, skin conductance and other signals while couples talked, and found that high physiological arousal went along with worse conversations and worse long-term outcomes. The idea that the body needs time to settle is consistent with what is known about the stress response in general. The specific twenty-minute figure is a practical guideline from that work rather than a precisely tested cut-off, and it will vary from person to person.

Accepting influence

Also known as: sharing power, letting your partner influence you, yielding.

In a nutshell

Accepting influence means being open to being persuaded by the other person, taking their views into account and sometimes changing course because of them. It is a quiet form of respect.

Going deeper

In any partnership, both people have opinions about how things should be done. Accepting influence doesn’t mean giving in on everything. It means the other person’s view can actually move you: you ask what they think, you look for the part of their point that makes sense, and decisions come out shaped by both of you.

Its opposite is a stance of “my way or no way,” where the other person’s input is resisted as a matter of principle. Over time this tends to leave one person feeling that their voice doesn’t count, which is fertile ground for resentment.

A practical sign of accepting influence is saying “that’s a good point” and meaning it, or finding the part of a request you can say yes to even when you can’t agree to all of it.

What the research says, and its limits

A 1998 study by Gottman, Coan, Carrère and Swanson of newlywed couples found that husbands who accepted influence from their wives were more likely to have stable, happy marriages. The study was about heterosexual couples and focused on husbands because that was where the variation lay in their sample. The general principle, that shared power and mutual respect go with better relationships, is supported more broadly, including in research on same-sex couples.

Perpetual and solvable problems

Also known as: perpetual problems, gridlock, unsolvable problems, recurring arguments.

In a nutshell

Some disagreements can be solved. Others come from lasting differences in personality or values, and keep coming back. Gottman calls these perpetual problems, and suggests the goal is to talk about them well rather than to fix them.

Going deeper

A solvable problem is situational: who picks up the kids on Tuesdays, where to put the recycling. A perpetual problem is rooted in who each person is: one likes plans and one likes spontaneity; one is tidy and one doesn’t notice mess; one wants more time with family than the other.

Perpetual problems are normal. Any two people bring some. What seems to distinguish couples who cope well is that they can talk about these differences with humor, curiosity and some acceptance, rather than getting stuck in “gridlock,” where each conversation repeats the same hurt and nobody moves.

Gottman suggests that behind gridlock there are often deeper hopes or values (he calls them dreams within conflict). The person who wants to save money may be carrying a history of insecurity; the person who wants to spend may value enjoying life while it lasts. Talking about those deeper layers can soften a standoff even when the practical difference remains.

What the research says, and its limits

Gottman has estimated that roughly two-thirds of couples’ recurring conflicts are perpetual in this sense. That figure is his estimate from clinical and research observation, not a precise measured statistic. The broader point, that acceptance of differences matters alongside change, is supported by other research, including trials of integrative behavioral couple therapy, which combines acceptance with behavior change.

Love maps and knowing each other’s world

Also known as: love map, knowing your partner, curiosity about each other.

In a nutshell

A love map is Gottman’s phrase for the store of knowledge you hold about someone’s inner world: their worries, hopes, friends, current stresses and favorite things. Keeping it up to date is an act of care.

Going deeper

People change. What stressed someone last year is not what stresses them now. A love map fades unless it is refreshed by asking real questions and listening to the answers.

This is particularly important during busy seasons (a new baby, a new job, caring for a parent) when conversations shrink to logistics. Couples can end up efficient co-managers who no longer know what is going on inside each other.

Refreshing a love map doesn’t require deep talks every night. Open questions work well: “What’s been on your mind this week?”, “Who’s been hard to deal with at work?”, “What are you looking forward to?”

What the research says, and its limits

The concept comes from Gottman’s The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work (1999, with Nan Silver). It draws on his research but is a framework for practice rather than a separately tested measure. Related research supports the value of question-asking: a 2017 set of studies by Karen Huang and colleagues found that people who asked more follow-up questions in conversation were liked more by their conversation partners.

Attachment theory

Also known as: attachment, secure base, Bowlby, Ainsworth, strange situation.

In a nutshell

Attachment theory says that humans are built to seek closeness to trusted people, especially under stress, and that early experiences of care shape expectations about closeness. It began with children and was later applied to adults.

Going deeper

John Bowlby, a British psychiatrist, argued in the mid-twentieth century that a child’s bond with caregivers is a basic biological need, not just a by-product of being fed. A responsive caregiver works as a secure base (from which the child explores) and a safe haven (to which the child returns when frightened).

Mary Ainsworth developed a lab procedure, the Strange Situation, in which a toddler is briefly separated from and reunited with a caregiver. She described patterns in how children responded: secure children were upset but comforted on reunion; others seemed anxious and hard to settle, or avoided the caregiver. A further pattern, called disorganised, was described later by Mary Main and Judith Solomon.

The core insight is gentle and widely applicable: when people feel threatened, they reach for connection, and how that reaching is met matters. Many “overreactions” in close relationships make more sense when seen as someone trying to restore a sense of safety with the person who matters most.

Attachment patterns are not destiny. They are shaped by later relationships too, and many people move toward more security over time.

What the research says, and its limits

Attachment theory is one of the best-supported frameworks in developmental psychology. Bowlby’s major work was published from 1969 onward, and Ainsworth and colleagues’ Patterns of Attachment appeared in 1978. Links between infant attachment and adult outcomes are real but modest, and are influenced by many later experiences. Researchers also caution that some popular uses stretch the theory, for example treating an attachment “style” as a fixed label or blaming everything on childhood.

Adult attachment: anxious and avoidant patterns

Also known as: attachment styles, anxious attachment, avoidant attachment, secure attachment, Hazan and Shaver.

In a nutshell

Researchers found that adults show attachment patterns in romantic relationships too. Today these are usually measured as two dimensions: anxiety (worry about being abandoned) and avoidance (discomfort with closeness).

Going deeper

In 1987, Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver published a study that applied Ainsworth’s categories to adult love. They found that adults could describe themselves in ways that mirrored secure, anxious and avoidant infant patterns. This opened a large field of research.

Later work suggested that attachment is better understood as two sliding scales than as boxes. Attachment anxiety involves worry about rejection and a strong need for reassurance. Attachment avoidance involves discomfort with dependence and a preference for self-reliance. Someone low on both is what people usually mean by “secure.” Most people are somewhere in the middle, and scores can shift with circumstances and with particular relationships.

A common difficult pairing is when one person tends to pursue (seeking reassurance, wanting to talk it through now) and the other tends to withdraw (needing space, going quiet). Each person’s coping move can trigger the other’s. Seeing this as a shared loop rather than as one person’s fault is often a relief.

Attachment language can be kind when it is used to understand yourself. It can be unkind when used as a diagnosis of the other person (“you’re so avoidant”).

What the research says, and its limits

Hazan and Shaver’s 1987 paper is a landmark. Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz proposed a four-category model in 1991, and Kelly Brennan, Catherine Clark and Phillip Shaver’s 1998 work helped establish the two-dimension approach. Research by R. Chris Fraley and others suggests attachment is dimensional rather than a set of distinct types, and that it is moderately stable but can change. Most measures are self-reports. Popular books and social media often present attachment styles as more fixed and more explanatory than the research supports.

The pursue–withdraw cycle

Also known as: demand-withdraw, pursuer distancer, nag and withdraw, chase and retreat.

In a nutshell

In many relationships, one person pushes to talk about a problem while the other pulls back. The more one pursues, the more the other withdraws, and the reverse. It is one of the most studied patterns in couples research.

Going deeper

The pursuer often feels ignored and anxious: if they don’t push, nothing will change. The withdrawer often feels criticized or overwhelmed: if they engage, it will blow up. Both are trying to protect the relationship in their own way, and each person’s move makes the other’s worse.

The cycle is not tied to one gender or personality; roles can even swap depending on the topic. The person who wants change on a given issue tends to pursue, and the one who is comfortable with the status quo tends to withdraw.

Breaking the loop usually means both people changing a little at once: the pursuer softening the start and picking a good moment, the withdrawer staying present a bit longer or naming a time to come back. It helps a great deal to name the cycle together (“we’re doing the chase again”) so it becomes the shared problem.

What the research says, and its limits

Andrew Christensen and colleagues studied the demand–withdraw pattern from the late 1980s, including a 1990 paper by Christensen and Christopher Heavey showing that the person seeking change tends to be the one who demands. Later meta-analyses have linked the pattern to lower relationship satisfaction. Emotionally focused therapy, developed by Sue Johnson and Leslie Greenberg, puts cycles like this at its center and has a reasonable evidence base from clinical trials.

Perceived partner responsiveness

Also known as: feeling understood, responsiveness, intimacy process, feeling cared for.

In a nutshell

Responsiveness is the sense that someone understands you, values you and cares about you. Research suggests that feeling this way is at the heart of intimacy, in romantic relationships and beyond.

Going deeper

Harry Reis and Phillip Shaver described intimacy as a process: one person shares something, the other responds, and the first person feels understood, validated and cared for, or doesn’t. Repeated over time, these exchanges build closeness.

Note the word perceived. What matters is how the response lands for the person on the receiving end. Someone can be trying hard to help and still come across as dismissive, for example by jumping to advice when the other person wanted to be heard first.

This is why “Do you want help or do you want me to listen?” is such a useful question. It lets the helper respond in the way that will actually be felt as care.

What the research says, and its limits

Reis and Shaver proposed the intimacy process model in 1988, and Reis and colleagues have since built a large body of research around perceived partner responsiveness. It is considered one of the more robust predictors of relationship satisfaction and is studied across many kinds of relationships. As with most relationship research, much of it is correlational.

Commitment and the investment model

Also known as: commitment, why people stay, investment, alternatives, Rusbult.

In a nutshell

Caryl Rusbult’s investment model says commitment grows from three things: how satisfied you are, how attractive your alternatives seem, and how much you have invested in the relationship.

Going deeper

Satisfaction matters, but it isn’t the whole story. People also weigh what they would have without the relationship and what they have put in: time, shared friends, a home, memories, identity. High investment and few appealing alternatives can keep commitment strong even through dissatisfied patches.

Commitment in turn shapes behavior. Committed partners are more likely to accommodate (respond constructively when the other behaves badly), to make sacrifices, and to see their relationship in a favorable light. These are the kinds of habits that help relationships through rough stretches.

The model applies to friendships, jobs and other commitments too. It can be a useful, non-judgemental lens for understanding why people stay in or leave situations.

What the research says, and its limits

Rusbult introduced the model in 1980 and tested it in the early 1980s; a 1998 meta-analysis by Benjamin Le and Christopher Agnew across many studies found that satisfaction, alternatives and investments each predicted commitment. It is one of the best-supported models in relationship science.

Self-expansion and doing new things together

Also known as: novelty, trying new things together, self-expansion, date night, boredom in relationships.

In a nutshell

The self-expansion model suggests people are drawn to relationships that help them grow, and that doing new and interesting things together can bring back some of the energy of early closeness.

Going deeper

Arthur and Elaine Aron proposed that one reason falling in love feels so good is that we rapidly take on new perspectives, identities and experiences through the other person. As relationships settle, the rate of expansion slows, and life can feel samey.

Their suggestion is not that people need grand adventures. Shared novelty can be small: a new walk, a cooking experiment, a class, a puzzle, learning something together. The point is that it is new and a bit engaging for both.

This offers a gentler reading of relationship boredom. It is usually not a sign that something is wrong with either person, but that routines have crowded out discovery.

What the research says, and its limits

The Arons introduced the model in 1986. In a well-known 2000 study, Arthur Aron and colleagues had couples do either a novel, mildly challenging task together or a mundane one; couples who did the novel task reported higher relationship quality afterward. Later studies have mostly supported the idea, though effects in the lab are modest and short-term.

Love languages, and what the evidence says

Also known as: five love languages, Chapman, words of affirmation, acts of service, quality time, gifts, physical touch.

In a nutshell

The five love languages is a popular idea from Gary Chapman’s 1992 book: people give and receive love mainly through words, acts of service, quality time, gifts or touch. It is a helpful conversation starter, but research has not supported its main claims.

Going deeper

The appeal is easy to see. It gives people a friendly vocabulary for noticing that care can look different: one person feels loved when the dishes are done; another when they get a heartfelt note. That observation is genuinely useful.

Where it gets shakier is the stronger claims: that each person has one primary language, that the five categories are the right ones, and that couples who “speak the same language” are happier. Studies have generally found that most people value all five, that the categories don’t divide people cleanly, and that matching doesn’t reliably predict satisfaction.

A kinder, research-friendly way to use it: treat it as a menu rather than a diagnosis. Ask what kinds of care land best right now, and expect the answer to change with circumstances.

What the research says, and its limits

A 2024 review by Emily Impett, Haeyoung Gideon Park and Amy Muise examined the evidence and concluded that the core assumptions of the love languages framework are not well supported, while suggesting that the underlying message, that partners should learn what the other needs, is sound. Relationship researchers often point to responsiveness as the better-supported idea underneath it.

How trust is built

Also known as: trust, trustworthiness, reliability, dependability.

In a nutshell

Trust grows from experience: seeing, over time, that someone is predictable, dependable and on your side. It is built mostly in small moments and can be rebuilt, slowly, the same way.

Going deeper

John Rempel, John Holmes and Mark Zanna described three layers of trust in close relationships: predictability (I know how you’ll act), dependability (I can rely on you when it matters), and faith (a confidence that goes beyond the evidence). Early on, trust is based on specific behavior; later it becomes a broader belief about the person.

Gottman describes trust as built in the ordinary moments when one person could choose their own interest over the other’s, and chooses the relationship. Following through on small promises is part of this. So is being honest about what you can’t do.

When trust has been dented, people often want a big gesture. Research and experience both suggest reliability over time does more: saying what you’ll do, doing it, and repeating. This is slow, and that slowness is not a sign of failure.

What the research says, and its limits

The three-part model comes from Rempel, Holmes and Zanna’s 1985 paper. Holmes and colleagues have since studied how trust shapes people’s willingness to depend on each other. Gottman’s 2011 book The Science of Trust offers a broader account based on his lab work. Trust is well established as central to relationship quality, though it is measured in many different ways.

  • Notice the quiet incentives (behavioral economics). In you: spot the defaults and shortcuts that steer your own choices. Between you and others: see how unclaimed jobs drift to one person, and how fairness and thanks keep things steady.

Jealousy

Also known as: jealous, envy, insecurity, possessiveness.

In a nutshell

Jealousy is the feeling that a valued relationship is under threat from someone or something else. It is a common human emotion, and like most emotions it carries information, though what it says isn’t always accurate.

Going deeper

Jealousy mixes fear of loss with other feelings: hurt, anger, embarrassment. It is related to but different from envy, which is wanting what someone else has.

Jealousy can come from things happening in the relationship, from insecurity or past experiences, or both. It can also attach to things that aren’t people at all: a partner’s job, a hobby, time with friends. Often underneath is a simple question: “Do I still matter to you?”

Talking about jealousy tends to go better when the feeling is owned (“I noticed I felt jealous when…”) rather than turned into accusation, and when the other person responds to the underlying worry rather than just the surface complaint. Reassurance helps; so does looking together at whether there is a real need for more time, attention or clarity.

What the research says, and its limits

Jealousy has been studied from several angles: attachment researchers link it to attachment anxiety; evolutionary psychologists such as David Buss have proposed sex differences in what triggers it, though those claims are debated, partly because results depend heavily on how questions are asked. Across approaches, researchers agree that how jealousy is expressed and responded to matters a lot for the relationship.

  • Tune how you send and receive (signal theory, the Frequency Framework). In you: know your own wiring, pace and how you hear things. Between you and others: translate across different wiring and tone; a mismatch is tuning, not a moral failing.

Positive illusions: seeing each other generously

Also known as: idealizing a partner, benefit of the doubt, generous interpretation, positive sentiment override.

In a nutshell

People in happier relationships tend to see their partner a little more positively than the partner sees themselves. This generous lens seems to help relationships, within limits.

Going deeper

Sandra Murray, John Holmes and Dale Griffin found that people who idealized their partners somewhat (rated them more kindly than the partners rated themselves) tended to be happier, and that partners who were idealized tended to grow toward those views over time.

The related idea of sentiment override, from Robert Weiss, says that the general feeling in a relationship colors how each new event is interpreted. In positive override, a sharp comment is read as “they’re tired.” In negative override, a neutral comment is read as an attack.

This doesn’t mean ignoring real problems. It means that giving the benefit of the doubt, especially for small things, is a genuine relationship skill, and that a relationship’s mood affects perception, not only the other way round.

What the research says, and its limits

Murray, Holmes and Griffin published their key studies in 1996. Weiss described sentiment override in 1980. The findings are well cited, but the benefits have limits: some later research, for example by James McNulty, suggests that overly positive assumptions can backfire when there are serious problems that need addressing, so a generous lens works best alongside honest conversation.

Different conflict styles in couples

Also known as: validating couples, volatile couples, conflict avoiding couples, fighting styles.

In a nutshell

Gottman described three styles of couples who get along despite arguing very differently: validating (calm, collaborative), volatile (passionate, loud, warm), and conflict-avoiding (agree to disagree). The mismatch between styles can matter more than the style itself.

Going deeper

It is tempting to think there is one right way to disagree. Gottman’s observations suggested otherwise. Validating couples listen and compromise calmly. Volatile couples argue with feeling and also laugh and make up with feeling. Conflict-avoiding couples minimize disagreements and focus on what they share. All three styles showed stable relationships, as long as warmth outweighed negativity.

Problems seemed more likely when partners had different styles: for example, one who wants to have it out and one who wants to let it go. Each can experience the other as either aggressive or dismissive.

Knowing this can take pressure off. The aim isn’t to argue the textbook way; it is to find a way that works for both, with plenty of repair and warmth around it.

What the research says, and its limits

Gottman described these types in What Predicts Divorce? (1994) and Why Marriages Succeed or Fail (1994). They come from his observational research with a limited sample and have not been tested as extensively as some of his other ideas, so they are best treated as a helpful description rather than a formal classification.

The Michelangelo effect

Also known as: bringing out the best in each other, supporting growth, ideal self.

In a nutshell

Close partners can help each other become who they want to be, like a sculptor revealing the figure in the stone. Feeling supported in your goals is linked with both growth and closeness.

Going deeper

The idea is that partners shape each other. When someone sees your ideal self (the person you are trying to become) and treats you in ways that encourage it, you tend to move in that direction.

This is different from trying to turn the other person into who you want them to be. The support is for their own goals. It can look like encouraging a course they are nervous about, taking on a task so they have time to practice, or reminding them of progress they have made.

Support for growth can also show up in how chores and time are shared. When the load is uneven, one person’s goals tend to get quietly squeezed out.

What the research says, and its limits

Stephen Drigotas, Caryl Rusbult and colleagues described the Michelangelo phenomenon in 1999. Their studies found that partner affirmation of the ideal self was associated with movement toward that ideal and with better relationship wellbeing. The research is correlational and longitudinal rather than experimental.
