This page is for anyone who lives on their own, for any reason and at any age. Your first flat at 22. A home of your own in your forties, by choice. Living alone at 75 after losing a partner. It covers being alone without feeling lonely, running a home with nobody to share the jobs, routines for one, making and keeping friends as an adult, asking for help, simple safety basics, weekends and holidays, and what to do when loneliness gets heavy. Living alone isn’t a waiting room for something else. It can be a good life in its own right.

In short:

  • Alone and lonely are different things. You can enjoy your own company and still need people.
  • Nobody reminds you, so write it down. One list of the jobs that keep your life running, with dates.
  • Give your week a few anchors: meals, a weekly plan with people, a slow morning.
  • Adult friendships grow from repetition. The same faces, again and again, plus small invitations.
  • Reaching out is usually welcomed more than you expect. Keep doing it, and ask for a little back.
  • Keep a short “people I can call” list and one person who knows you’re okay.
  • Dating or not dating: both are fine.
  • If loneliness feels heavy or hopeless, tell someone today. Free lines are below.

Alone is not the same as lonely

Being alone is a fact: nobody else is in the room. Loneliness is a feeling: the people in your life don’t feel like enough right now. You can be alone and content. You can be lonely in a full house.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Alone is not the same as lonely

Many people choose to live alone and love it. Quiet, your own pace, the kitchen exactly how you left it. Chosen time alone tends to feel calm. Time alone you didn’t choose can feel heavy. Both are normal, and both can be true in the same week.

Loneliness isn’t a flaw. It works like hunger: a signal that you need more of something, in this case people. The answer is usually small, regular contact, not a big change.

For example (made up): Dev, 44, has lived alone for ten years by choice. He loves his evenings. But by Sunday afternoon he often feels flat. He used to think that meant living alone wasn’t working. Now he treats it as a cue: Sunday is his long walk with his sister, and the flat feeling mostly stays away.

Words you could use

To yourself: “This is a lonely moment, not a lonely life. Who could I message?”

To a friend: “I love living on my own, and I’d still love to see you more. Fancy a regular thing?”

When someone pities you: “I actually really like it. I just make sure I see people.”

Try this: For one week, notice when you feel alone and good, and when you feel lonely. Is there a pattern: a day, a time, after too little sleep? My weather today takes a minute a day and keeps your readings on this device.

A home that runs itself (with nobody to remind you)

When you live with others, someone usually notices that the milk is low or the car is due its check. On your own, every job is yours: doing it, and remembering it. The remembering is the tiring part. Nobody sees it, and nobody takes over when you’re ill or busy.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: A home that runs itself (with nobody to remind you)

The jobs nobody reminds you about:

  • Once a year: insurance renewals, taxes, the boiler or furnace service, car checks, smoke alarm batteries, dentist and health checks.
  • Once a month: bills, bank statements, subscriptions you forgot you had, clearing the fridge.
  • Every week: food shopping, bins, laundry, plants, a quick tidy.
  • Now and then: birthdays, passwords, renewing documents, the drawer of letters you’ll “deal with later.”

The fix isn’t more willpower. It’s getting the remembering out of your head and onto something that reminds you.

  • One list, one place. Every repeating job, how often, and the month it’s next due.
  • Put the yearly ones in your calendar, with a reminder two weeks before.
  • Automate what you can: direct debits, repeat orders, a standing appointment.
  • Pick a backup for each big job: who could feed the cat, pay a bill or wait in for a repair if you were ill?
  • A short “admin hour” once a week or once a month, with music and a nice drink.

For example (made up): Marisol, 23, moved into her first flat and paid a late fee twice in three months. Not because she couldn’t pay, but because nobody had ever shown her the bills came on different days. She wrote every bill on one page with its date, set them all to come out on the 2nd, and added “admin hour, Sunday 11am” to her phone. It takes her twenty minutes now.

Words you could use

To a friend: “Want to do a boring-jobs afternoon together? You bring your forms, I’ll bring mine, and we get cake after.”

To a neighbour: “If I’m ever away or ill, could I give you my number? I’m happy to do the same for you.”

Routines and rhythms for one

With nobody else’s timetable, days can blur, especially if you work from home or have retired. A few anchors help a week feel like a week. Keep them loose. Missing one isn’t failing. Pick up anywhere.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Routines and rhythms for one
  • Meals: cook once, eat twice. Freeze single portions. Set the table sometimes, even for one.
  • A start and an end to the day: a walk, a shower, opening the curtains, a “closing up” routine at night.
  • One plan with people each week, on the same day if you can.
  • One thing that’s just for you: a slow breakfast, a bath, a long call, a film you choose.
  • Get outside daily if you can. Even a short walk to the shop.

Words you could use

“Thursday is my soup-and-a-film night. Want to make it a regular thing?”

“I work from home, so I’m trying to see one real face a day. Coffee on Friday?”

Making and keeping friends as an adult

At school or college, friends came with the timetable. You saw the same people every day without planning it. As an adult, that structure goes, and many people feel they’ve “forgotten how” to make friends. You haven’t. The setup has changed.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Making and keeping friends as an adult

Friendships tend to grow from three things:

  • Being near the same people (a class, a club, a team, a regular café).
  • Seeing them again and again, without having to plan it each time.
  • Small, easy invitations that turn a familiar face into a friend.

It also takes time. One study estimated it takes around 50 hours together to go from someone you know to a casual friend, and over 200 hours to become close friends. Mostly hanging out, not just working side by side. So a weekly thing with the same people will do more than one big night out.

  • Join something that meets every week: a choir, a running club, a language class, a volunteer shift, a faith group, a games night.
  • Go back, even when the first time felt awkward. The third week is often when it starts to feel easier.
  • Make the first invite small: “A few of us are getting a drink after. Want to come?”
  • Say hello to the “regulars”: the neighbour, the barista, the person at the gym. Small chats count, and some grow.
  • Keep old friends warm with short, regular contact. A voice note counts.

For example (made up): Ruth, 71, moved to a new town to be near her son. She knew nobody. She joined a weekly walking group and kept going, even though for a month nobody said much past “Morning!” In week six, Pat asked if she wanted to try the café after. Two years later they go to the theatre together every month.

Words you could use

“I’m new round here and trying to meet people. What else do you do for fun?”

“I really enjoyed chatting last week. Do you fancy a coffee after next time?”

“I’m going to that quiz on Tuesday. Want to make up a team?”

Try this: Pick one thing that meets weekly, and go three times before you decide whether it’s for you. Need ideas? Get an idea now.

Being the friend who always asks first

When you live alone, you’re often the one who has to make plans happen. Friends with partners and children may be busy, tired, or just used to you reaching out. It can start to feel one-sided.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Being the friend who always asks first
  • Keep reaching out. Research suggests people are more pleased to hear from us than we expect, especially out of the blue.
  • Don’t keep score of who texted first. It often says more about their week than how much they care.
  • Ask for a little back, once and kindly. One small ask, not a list.
  • Spread it out. If one friend never asks, put more of your energy into the ones who do, without cutting anyone off.

Words you could use

“I love seeing you. I’ve noticed I’m usually the one who plans. Could you pick the next one?”

“No need to reply properly. Just thinking of you.”

“I know weekends are family time for you. Could we find a weekday lunch now and then?”

Friendships in your 20s

Your twenties are often the first time friendship doesn’t come built in. People finish school or college, move cities, start jobs with odd hours, pair up, or move back home. Friend groups that felt permanent can scatter in a year.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Friendships in your 20s
  • After school or college: it’s normal for some friendships to fade and a few to deepen. Pick two or three people you most want to keep, and make a regular plan with each.
  • Group chats: lovely for keeping in touch, easy to feel left out of. If a chat goes quiet or you see plans you weren’t in, ask directly instead of guessing: “Saw the photos, looked fun! Count me in next time?”
  • Moving to a new city: say yes to things for the first few months, even small ones. Join one weekly thing early, before the routine sets.
  • Coworkers can become friends, slowly. Lunch is a good first step. Keep work worries in mind, and go at their pace.
  • Friend breakups are real and can hurt as much as a romantic one. You’re allowed to grieve them.
  • Different paths are normal. One friend is engaged, one is travelling, one is back with their parents. You don’t have to be at the same stage to stay close.

Words you could use

“I’ve just moved here and don’t know many people. Is there a group or a thing you’d recommend?”

“I miss our group. Shall we set one call a month, same day, so it doesn’t rely on anyone organising?”

After a friendship ends: “I’m sad about how things went with us. I wish you well, really.”

Try this: Write down five people you’d like to be closer to this year. Next to each, write one small next step. Do one this week.

Living alone when you’re neurodivergent

For many autistic people and people with ADHD, living alone can be a relief: your senses, your routines, your rules. It can also mean nobody shares the planning, starting and remembering that executive function makes harder. Supports aren’t a crutch. They are how the home works for you.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Living alone when you’re neurodivergent
  • Make it visible: a whiteboard by the door, clear boxes, a checklist on the fridge, phone alarms with names (“bins out tonight”).
  • Body doubling: doing a task with someone nearby, in person or on a video call. Many people find it much easier to start.
  • A home that suits your senses: soft lights, a quiet corner, textures you like, the same meals if that’s easier. That’s not odd. That’s your home.
  • Make the steps tiny: “Open the letter” is a step. “Sort out the post” is a project.
  • Set up contact so it doesn’t depend on remembering: a standing weekly call, a friend who checks in, a group that meets at a set time.

Words you could use

“Could we do a body-double call on Sunday? I’ll do my post, you do your thing.”

“I love seeing people, but I need quiet after. I might leave early, and it’s not you.”

“Reminders help me. Please don’t feel you’re nagging.”

Try this: Make a How to talk to me card, a one-page “how I work” card, and share it with one friend.

Asking for help without a partner

People with partners often have one obvious person to ask. On your own, you need a few. Most people want to help and wait to be asked. Asking also makes a friendship closer, not weaker.

Write a short “people I can call” list. Keep it in your phone and on paper.

People I can call (an example to copy)

  • In an emergency: name, number, and someone else if they don’t answer.
  • If I’m ill: someone nearby who could bring food or medicine.
  • A lift to a hospital appointment: name.
  • Has a spare key: name.
  • Practical jobs (a ladder, a car, flat-pack furniture): name.
  • Money or paperwork questions: name, or a free advice service.
  • A bad night, when I just need to talk: name, and a free helpline.

Spread it out, so no one person is asked for everything. And offer the same back.

Words you could use

“I’ve got a small procedure next month and need a lift home. Could you do it? Say no if it’s tricky.”

“I’m putting together a list of people I could call in an emergency. Could I put you on it? Happy to be on yours.”

“I’m not great at asking for help, so I’m practising. Could you give me a hand moving a sofa on Saturday?”

Try this: Asking feels hard? Say no kindly works both ways: knowing people can say no makes it easier to ask.

Health and safety basics

Nothing here is about expecting the worst. It’s the kind of thing everyone who lives alone sets up once, then mostly forgets.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Health and safety basics
  • A check-in buddy: one person you message most days, even just a thumbs up. If they don’t hear from you, they check.
  • A spare key with someone you trust nearby, or a key safe.
  • An emergency contact card in your wallet and on your phone’s lock screen: who to call, any conditions, allergies or medicines.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms that work. Test them when you change the clocks.
  • Phone by the bed, and charged.
  • If you have a health condition, or are older, ask your doctor or local services about a personal alarm you can wear.
  • Tell someone if you’re going away, having a procedure, or meeting someone new.

Words you could use

“Could we be each other’s check-in buddy? Just a quick morning message. If one of us goes quiet all day, the other checks.”

“Would you hold a spare key for me? I’ll take one of yours too.”

Pets

A pet can bring routine, company, a reason to get outside, and easy chats with other owners. They also bring costs, vet bills, and the question of who looks after them when you’re away or ill. Research on pets and loneliness is mixed. A pet is lovely company, not a replacement for people.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Pets
  • Before you get one: think about cost, time, your lease, and who would help when you’re away.
  • Add a “pet backup” to your people-I-can-call list.
  • Not able to have one? Pet-sitting for friends, dog-walking for a neighbour, or volunteering at a shelter can give you some of the same joy.

Words you could use

“If I’m ever stuck in hospital, could you feed the cat? I’ll write everything down.”

Weekends and holidays

Weekends and big holidays can be the hardest times when you live alone. Others vanish into family time, and the quiet can feel loud. A little planning helps.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Weekends and holidays
  • Plan one thing for each weekend in advance, even if it’s small.
  • For big holidays, decide early what you’d like: with people, on your own on purpose, or a mix.
  • Make your own traditions: a holiday walk, a film marathon, a meal for other people on their own.
  • Say yes to invitations, or host one. “Orphans’ dinners” are common and often the best ones.
  • Volunteering on a big day can feel good and bring company.

Words you could use

“I’m around on the 25th. If anyone else is, I’m doing a big lunch. Bring nothing but yourselves.”

“I’d love to join you for a bit on the day, if that’s okay. I’ll bring dessert.”

“I’m actually spending it on my own this year, and I’m looking forward to it.”

Dating, or not dating: both are fine

Living alone doesn’t mean you’re waiting for a partner. Some people want one, some don’t, and some aren’t sure. Some people are asexual or aromantic, and a full life looks different for them. All of these are fine. Friends, family, community and your own company are real relationships, not second best.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Dating, or not dating: both are fine
  • If you’d like to date, keep your own life going alongside it. Early days: dating and new relationships can help.
  • If you don’t, you don’t owe anyone a reason.
  • If you date someone who also lives alone, you don’t have to move in. Many couples happily live apart.

Words you could use

When asked “Seeing anyone?”: “Not right now, and I’m happy. Tell me about your news!”

“I’m not looking for a relationship. My life is pretty full.”

When loneliness feels heavy

Sometimes loneliness isn’t a quiet Sunday. It’s weeks of feeling cut off, low, or like nobody would notice if you weren’t there. That’s a sign to get some support, not to try harder alone.

  • Tell one person today, even by message: “I’ve been really lonely lately.”
  • Talk to your doctor. Low mood and loneliness often feed each other, and help works.
  • Find a group: a support group, a befriending service, or a local community centre.

Words you could use

“I’m having a hard time and I’ve been really lonely. Could we talk this week?”

To a doctor: “I live alone and I’ve been feeling very low and cut off for a while.”

If loneliness turns into hopelessness, or you think about ending your life, call or text 988 in the US, call Samaritans free on 116 123 or text SHOUT to 85258 in the UK, or find your local line at findahelpline.com. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number.

Your situation

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Your situation

Your first place, in your twenties

Nobody hands you the manual. Start the one-list-of-jobs habit now, learn three easy meals, and find one weekly thing with people. Coming from a shared house? You may miss the noise more than you expect. That’s normal. See Friendships in your 20s.

On your own by choice, in midlife

You may know just what you like, and get asked “Don’t you get lonely?” more than you’d like. Keep the friendships that matter on a rhythm. Think ahead about who you’d call if you were ill, and who your backup people are.

After a breakup or divorce

Living alone can feel very different when you didn’t choose it. Go gently. On my own after a breakup or divorce is written for this, including part-time parenting.

Later in life, after a loss

Learning to run a home alone, maybe for the first time, while grieving is a lot. The jobs your partner did may be the hardest: write them down, one at a time, and ask for help with the ones you’ve never done. See Grief and later life.

To a grown-up child: “I’m managing, and I’d love help with the car insurance. Your dad always did it.”

Every kind of person

Everything here applies whatever your gender, sexuality, culture or faith. LGBTQ+ people may have a “chosen family” that matters as much as any other. People living alone with a disability or long-term illness may need more practical backup, and deserve it. When one of you is ill or disabled is written for couples, but its ideas for planning around energy and bad days work with friends and family too.

Try this this week:

  1. Write down every repeating job in one list, with the month it’s next due.
  2. Ask one person to be your check-in buddy, or to hold a spare key.
  3. Send one small invitation: a coffee, a walk, a call.

Questions people often ask

Is it bad for me to live alone?

No. Many people live alone happily and healthily. What matters more is staying connected: regular contact with people you like, and someone to call when you need help.

How long does it take to make a new friend as an adult?

Usually longer than people expect. One study estimated about 50 hours together for a casual friendship and over 200 hours for a close one. Regular, relaxed time adds up faster than occasional big plans.

I’m always the one who reaches out. Should I stop?

Not necessarily. People are often more pleased to hear from us than we expect. Keep reaching out to people you care about, ask once, kindly, for a little back, and put more of your energy where it’s returned.

What safety basics should I set up if I live alone?

A check-in buddy who expects to hear from you most days, a spare key with someone nearby, an emergency contact card in your wallet and on your phone, and working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.

Is it normal to lose friends in your twenties?

Yes. Moves, jobs and relationships pull groups apart. Some friendships fade and a few deepen. Choose the ones you most want to keep, and give each a regular plan.

I’m lonely all the time. What should I do?

Tell one person, and talk to your doctor. If you feel hopeless or think about ending your life, call or text 988 in the US, call Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK, or find your local line at findahelpline.com.
