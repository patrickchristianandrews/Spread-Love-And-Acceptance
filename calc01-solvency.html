CALC-01 · THE FULL CALCULATOR

Is the setup working for both of you? Also called “Can the load last?” or the solvency read · for 2 to 8 people

This scores one narrow thing: whether the way you share the work right now, as you logged it, can keep going. It never scores a person or the relationship. It works for any group that shares a load: partners, families, roommates, co-carers, a small team at work. ("Solvency" is an accounting word. Here it just means the setup can carry its own weight.)

A place to start a conversation, not a verdict. A low score doesn't blame anyone, and a high score doesn't mean anyone should stop speaking up.

CALC-01 puts Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person to work. Step Zero uses Pillar III, Read your state first, and the retuning count uses Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. In you: you see the whole load you carry, and check your state before you read the number. Between you and others: you get one fair read of the setup, never a score on anyone.

The math comes from Chapter II. Only have totals? Try the quick read. To follow it week by week, use REPORT-01.

Two scores, two questions. The solvency read uses balance, ownership and stress, and asks: can this week's load last as it's set up? The apex score adds retuning and asks a wider question: is the whole system working, including repair after rough moments? They can disagree, and the gap is useful.

Input 1 of 4 · Pillar I

Balance: how the hours split workload balance

The week's hours

From WP-01, Who did what

Enter this week's rough hours for each job, for each person. Rough is fine: the split is what matters. Count the invisible work too (planning, remembering, keeping track).

Swipe the table sideways to see everyone.

Compare the split with…

Each person's agreed share of the home load, in percent. They must add up to 100. A read against an agreed split can't be compared with a standard read, so the summary says which one you used.

In plain words: balance asks how many of the week's hours would have to change hands to reach an even split (or your agreed split), compared with the most that could ever need to move, which is when one person does it all. None to move scores 1.00. One person doing everything scores 0.00. With two people this is exactly the book's 1 − |pctA − pctB| ÷ 100. It doesn't matter who is carrying more, only that the load isn't shared the way you meant it to be.

Input 2 of 4 · Pillar II

Ownership: does each job have a name? ownership clarity

The job list

From WP-03, One owner per job

For each regular job, pick who is Responsible (does it) and who is Accountable (notices if it didn't happen and follows up kindly). They can be the same person. A job only counts when both are named.

JobResponsibleAccountableCounts?Remove

Don't raise this one by shortening the list. Deleting the jobs nobody owns raises the number and changes nothing at home. A real job with no owner stays on the list. Bringing that row to light is the whole point of this score.

"Everyone together" counts as a name, as the book allows. But a job that everyone is accountable for is often a job nobody notices (Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives). The read flags those.

Input 3 of 4 · Pillar III

Stress: how much each person is carrying average battery load (AS)

Everyone's battery

From WP-02, How much are you carrying? (higher = heavier)

Each person fills in their own card, about themselves, for the last 24 to 48 hours. 0 = not at all, 4 = very true. Guessing for someone else turns a reflection tool into a way of watching them, so the read waits until every card is complete.

Stress is the only input that counts against the score, as 1 − stress. A way of sharing the work that only holds up when everyone is rested isn't one that can last. This is a self-check, not a health measure.

Optional: push scores toward the ends of the scale

This runs the same total through an S-shaped curve centered at 0.5: 1 / (1 + e^(−6 × (raw − 0.5))). Middling reads move further from the middle. It doesn't make the score more accurate, and reads taken with it can't be compared with reads taken without it. It's off by default.

Reference

How it's scored

The two formulas

solvency = (balance × 0.40) + (ownership × 0.35) + ((1 − stress) × 0.25)

apex     = (balance × 0.35) + (ownership × 0.30) + ((1 − stress) × 0.20) + (retuning × 0.15)

Both are simple weighted totals, not fitted models. Each set of weights adds up to 1.00 and every input runs from 0 to 1, so each score runs from 0 to 1 too. No weight was estimated from real-world data. The book calls the first one "P(Solvency)", but it is not a probability and predicts nothing.

The four inputs

InputHow it's worked outFrom
Balance1 − moved ÷ most, where moved = ½ × Σ |share − target share| and most = 1 − the smallest target share. The target is an even share (1 ÷ people) unless you choose an agreed split. With two people and an even target, it equals 1 − |pctA − pctB| ÷ 100.WP-01
Ownershipjobs with both names ÷ all jobs listed. Measures how clear ownership is, not how fair it is.WP-03
StressEach person: their five 0–4 answers added up ÷ 20. Then the average across everyone. Counted as 1 − stress.WP-02
Retuningmoments retuned ÷ rough moments, capped at 1.00. No value when there were no rough moments. Apex only.WP-09

Balance for more than two people, worked through

Three roommates log 12, 6 and 2 hours: 20 hours in all. An even share is a third each, about 6.67 hours. Jordan is 5.33 hours over, Priya is 0.67 hours under, Lee is 4.67 hours under. So 5.33 hours would have to change hands to make it even: that's moved = 5.33 ÷ 20 = 0.267. The most that could ever need to move, if one person did all 20 hours, is 13.33 hours, or most = 1 − 1/3 = 0.667. Balance = 1 − 0.267 ÷ 0.667 = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60.

With 6 of 8 jobs owned (0.75) and batteries of 8, 12 and 4 points (0.40, 0.60 and 0.20, average 0.40):

(0.60 × 0.40) + (0.75 × 0.35) + ((1 − 0.40) × 0.25)
=    0.24      +     0.2625     +        0.15
=    0.6525  →  0.65, needs a look

A family of five logs 10, 8, 6, 4 and 2 hours (30 in all; an even share is 6 each). The gaps are +4, +2, 0, −2 and −4 hours, so 6 hours would have to move: moved = 0.20. most = 1 − 1/5 = 0.80. Balance = 1 − 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 0.75. With 9 of 12 jobs owned (0.75) and an average battery of 0.40, the read is 0.30 + 0.2625 + 0.15 = 0.7125 → 0.71, holding.

An agreed split. If two people agree on 60/40 (say one works longer hours outside the home) and log 6 and 4 hours, nothing needs to move, so balance is 1.00. Against an even split the same week would score 0.80. Use the agreed split only if everyone really agreed to it, and say so when you share the read.

Why these weights

PartSolvencyApexReasoning
Balance0.400.35Highest, because an unshared load keeps building up whether anyone notices or not.
Ownership0.350.30Close behind, because work with no owner falls to whoever notices first, every time. That turns a fixable imbalance into a repeating one.
1 − stress0.250.20Lowest of the three, because it changes most from week to week. One week of bad sleep shouldn't dominate the read.
Retuning—0.15Apex only, and small, because it's a simple self-count that's easy to overstate.

The bands

ScoreReadingFirst move
0.70 or moreWorking well: the setup is carrying its own weight.Don't change the setup. Keep the same rhythm of check-ins.

The band is read from the score as shown, rounded to two decimals, so the number and the band always agree. The edges are round numbers chosen to be easy to read: a 0.69 and a 0.70 are really the same week.

Reading the two scores together

  • Both high: the load is shared and owned, and rough moments get repaired. Keep going.
  • Solvency high, apex lower (by 0.08 or more): the setup is fine, but repair isn't happening. Work on WP-09, not the job list.
  • Apex high, solvency lower (by 0.08 or more): you repair well, but the setup keeps creating the friction. Work on the job list.
  • Both below 0.40: the setup and the repair are both struggling. Another worksheet may not be what helps most; outside support, like a counselor or a trusted mediator, is worth considering.

Edge cases, defined

  • No hours logged: balance has no value, rather than 1.00. An empty week isn't an even week.
  • No jobs listed: ownership has no value, rather than 0.00.
  • Anyone's battery incomplete: no read at all. A score is never worked out from some people's answers standing in for everyone's.
  • No rough moments: retuning has no value. Apex uses the other three weights divided by 0.85, so they still add up to 1.00, and the read says so.
  • More retunes than rough moments: retuning stops at 1.00.
  • Agreed shares that don't add up to 100%: no balance until they do.

Drift rule

If the apex score drops by more than 0.10 three weeks in a row, it's time for a planned review of the job list, not an emergency conversation. The trend log watches for this.

What this calculator will never use. Tone of voice, recordings, the mood of messages, how fast someone replies, or anything about a person that they didn't help enter themselves.

What the number is not

  • Not a compatibility score, and not a verdict on who's the better partner, parent, roommate or coworker.
  • Not a tested clinical or statistical tool. It predicts nothing.
  • Not a reason to decide a relationship's future. It looks at how the load is set up, one week at a time.
  • Not an argument-ender. "Well, the number says…" is a misuse. Its only job is to give people a calm place to start.
