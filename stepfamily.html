When two adults come together and at least one of them already has children, a stepfamily begins. Married or not, any genders, young children or grown-up ones. Stepfamilies can be warm, lively homes. They also start out with a shape that first families don’t have: a parent and child who have years together, an adult who is new, and often another home that the children belong to as well. This page is for stepparents, parents, step-grandparents and grown-up stepchildren.

The short version:

  • It takes years, not months, to feel like a family. Going slowly is normal.
  • Stepparent: be a warm adult first, and a rule-setter later, if at all.
  • Parent: lead on discipline at first, and back up your partner’s place in the home.
  • Children may feel torn. Tell them they never have to choose.
  • Two homes can have two sets of rules. Keep yours clear and calm.
  • Fair doesn’t always mean the same, but say out loud how you decide.
  • A stepparent who feels outside needs time alone with their partner, and a voice.

It takes time, and that’s normal

Many couples hope everyone will love each other quickly. Usually, it takes years. Stepfamily researchers, such as the psychologist Patricia Papernow (2013), describe closeness in stepfamilies as building slowly. A slow start isn’t a sign that something is wrong.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: It takes time, and that’s normal

Small, regular, easy time together helps most: the same Saturday breakfast, a drive to practice, a show you watch together. Big “family days” often go less well than small ordinary ones.

Words you could use

To your partner: “Let’s not expect a family by Christmas. Let’s aim for a good Saturday.”

The stepparent role: warm adult first

Children usually accept rules from someone they already trust. So a stepparent does best starting as a friendly, reliable adult, not a new boss.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: The stepparent role: warm adult first
  • The parent leads on discipline at first. The stepparent backs them up and keeps the house rules, without being the enforcer.
  • The stepparent builds connection: interest in the child’s world, being reliable, small shared things.
  • Let children choose what to call you. A first name is fine.
  • Over time, as trust grows, the stepparent can take on more. For some families, that takes years.

For example (made up): Dan moved in with Kim and her two boys. Within a month he was telling them to tidy their rooms, and the house went cold. He stepped back, let Kim handle the rules, and started a Friday chess game with the older boy. Six months later, the boys were asking him for help with homework.

Words you could use

To a stepchild: “I’m not trying to replace your dad. I’d just like us to get along.”

For the couple: “Can you take the lead on rules with your kids, and I’ll back you up?”

From the parent to the kids: “Jo isn’t in charge of rules. But Jo is an adult in this house, and we’re polite to each other.”

When children feel torn

A child may feel that liking a stepparent is disloyal to their other parent. So they may be cool with someone they actually like. This is called a loyalty bind, and it’s very common.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: When children feel torn
  • Say out loud that they don’t have to choose.
  • Never criticize the other parent in front of the children.
  • Keep one-to-one time between each parent and their own children. It reassures them they haven’t lost you.

Words you could use

From a stepparent: “You can love your mom and still like hanging out with me. You don’t have to pick.”

From a parent: “You’ll always have your mom and your dad. Sam is someone extra, not someone instead.”

The other household and the ex

The children’s other parent is part of the family’s life, even if you rarely see them. The calmer things are between the two homes, the easier it is for children.

  • The parents talk about the children. A stepparent usually stays out of parent-to-parent messages, at least at first.
  • Keep messages short, polite and about the children.
  • Don’t use children as messengers.

Words you could use

Parent to parent: “Mia has a school trip on the 12th. Can you sign the form at yours, or shall I?”

Stepparent to the other parent, if you meet: “Hi, I’m Jo. Nice to meet you. They’ve been telling me all about the football.”

Different rules in two homes

Children can handle “at this house, we do it this way.” What’s hard for them is rules that keep changing, or hearing one home criticize the other.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Different rules in two homes
  • Agree your own house rules as a couple, in private first. Keep them few.
  • Explain them once, calmly, with the parent leading.
  • Don’t compare. “Different” doesn’t mean “wrong.”
  • Where it really matters (bedtimes, medicine, screens for little ones), the parents try to agree across both homes.

Words you could use

“At Dad’s you can stay up later. Here, it’s lights out at nine. Both are okay.”

Step-siblings and half-siblings

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Step-siblings and half-siblings
  • Don’t expect instant friendship. Getting along politely is a good start.
  • Give each child some space that stays theirs, even if they’re only there some of the time.
  • A new baby can make older children feel pushed out. Keep one-to-one time with each, and don’t make them the default babysitter.
  • Deal with fights the same way for every child, whoever’s child they are.

Words you could use

“You two don’t have to be best friends. You do have to be kind.”

To an older child after a baby: “The baby needs a lot of me right now. You still matter just as much. Saturday morning is ours.”

Money and fairness between the kids

Money is a common sore spot: allowance, presents, school costs, child support, and what happens in a will. Children notice differences quickly.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Money and fairness between the kids
  • Agree as a couple how you’ll handle money for the children, before it comes up.
  • Fair doesn’t always mean the same. Ages and needs differ. But explain how you decide.
  • Presents in your home: roughly equal for children who live there, even part-time.
  • Wills and big money: talk about it openly as a couple.

Words you could use

For the couple: “Can we agree allowance and birthday budgets for all the kids, so nobody feels left out?”

Holidays and special days

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Holidays and special days
  • Plan early, with the other home too. Children shouldn’t have to arrange it.
  • Celebrate twice if that works. A second Christmas or Eid on another day still counts.
  • Make one or two new traditions that belong to this family.
  • Let children bring old traditions with them.

Words you could use

“You’re with your dad on Christmas Day this year, so we’ll have our Christmas on the 27th. Same presents, same pancakes.”

Adult stepchildren

Stepfamilies formed later in life have grown-up children, grandchildren, homes and wills involved. Adult children may worry about a parent being taken advantage of, about inheritance, or about a parent who died being forgotten.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Adult stepchildren
  • Don’t expect a parent–child bond. Friendly, respectful adults is a good aim.
  • Each partner leads with their own children, especially on money and big news.
  • Be open about wills and property, early.

Words you could use

From a parent to a grown-up child: “Marrying Pat doesn’t change what I’ve planned for you. I’ll show you, if you’d like.”

From a stepparent: “I’m not trying to be your mom. I’d like us to be friendly, and I know she matters to you.”

Step-grandparents

A step-grandchild may already have several grandparents. You don’t need to compete. Be another warm, steady adult. Follow the parents’ rules, and treat step- and biological grandchildren alike when they’re together.

Words you could use

“You can call me Bev. I’ve got a cookie jar with your name on it.”

A stepparent who feels outside

A stepparent can feel like a guest in their own home. The parent and children have shared jokes, habits and history. The parent, meanwhile, can feel pulled in two directions. Both feelings are normal. Neither of you is doing it wrong.

  • Protect couple time, even an hour a week.
  • Give the stepparent a space and a say in how the home runs.
  • Talk about it as a shared problem, not a complaint about the children.
  • A regular, calm check-in helps. Try the family plan card.

Words you could use

Stepparent: “I love you, and I love that you’re close to your kids. Sometimes I feel like a boarder. Can we talk about one small thing that would help?”

Parent: “I hear you. I’m not choosing them over you. Let’s find one evening that’s just ours.”

Every kind of stepfamily

Stepfamilies come in every shape. Two moms, two dads, unmarried partners, a partner who moved in without marrying, a stepparent with no children of their own. The ideas on this page apply to all of them.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Every kind of stepfamily

Some families face extra things. Not every school form, family member or law recognizes a same-sex or unmarried stepparent. Agree as a couple what the stepparent can do: school pickups, doctor’s visits, emergencies. Write it down, and check what your local rules allow.

Words you could use

To a school: “This is Alex, my partner. Alex is on the pickup list and can be reached if something comes up.”

When it’s going wrong

Most stepfamilies have hard years. Signs it may need more than time: the couple fights about the children most weeks, a child seems very unhappy or withdrawn for a long time, or a stepparent and child can’t be in a room together.

  • Go back to basics: parent leads, stepparent warms, small regular time together.
  • Talk at a calm time, not after a bad evening. Use Check-ins.

Words you could use

“We keep having the same fight about the kids. I don’t think either of us is wrong. Could we sit down this weekend and each pick one small change?”

Try this this week:

  1. Parent: spend twenty minutes alone with each of your children.
  2. Stepparent: do one small, easy thing with a stepchild, with no rules involved.
  3. As a couple: agree three house rules, and who says them out loud.

Questions people often ask

Should a stepparent discipline the children?

At first, the parent leads on rules and consequences, and the stepparent backs them up. The stepparent builds trust through warmth and reliability. As trust grows, they can take on more.

How long does it take for a stepfamily to feel like a family?

Usually years, not months. Closeness builds slowly in stepfamilies, and that is normal. Small, regular time together helps more than big family days.

What should my stepchildren call me?

Let them choose. A first name is fine. “Mom” or “Dad” may come later, or not at all, and neither is a measure of how much they care.

How do we handle different rules at the other parent’s house?

Keep your own few rules clear and calm, and don’t criticize the other home. Children can manage “at this house, we do it this way.” Where it really matters, the parents try to agree.

My stepchild is grown up and cold toward me. What can I do?

Aim for friendly and respectful, not close. Let your partner lead with their own child, especially on money and wills. Be steady and kind, and give it time.
