Examples

What small changes can look like

Made-up examples built from common patterns, not real people. Nobody here is a customer or a quote. Each one shows a pattern many people know, the one tool that fits it, and the kind of small change that tends to follow. Your own story will be different, and that’s fine.

None of these were fixed in a day, and none of them are perfect now. Each card ends with what was still hard, because there usually is something.

Made-up example A couple

“You never notice anything”

The pattern before
One partner kept the calendar, the birthday presents and the dentist in their head. The other helped whenever asked. Arguments kept starting with “you never notice.”
The one tool
The Who does what? quick look (also called the Lemonade Stand): one tap per everyday job for who mostly does it.
What changed
The picture showed the planning and remembering sat mostly with one name. They moved two whole jobs, the dentist and school forms, including the remembering, to the other person.
Still hard
For the first few weeks the old owner kept checking. That eased once the new owner had done it twice.

Made-up example Roommates

The sink that never empties

The pattern before
Four roommates, one kitchen. The sink filled up, someone left a sharp note on the fridge, and everyone went quiet.
The one tool
One owner per job: each regular job gets one name, and a “done by” time.
What changed
Trash, recycling, the bathroom and the kitchen each got an owner for a month at a time. The note came down. Nobody had to be the one who nags.
Still hard
One roommate works nights, so “done by 9 pm” didn’t fit. They took the morning jobs instead.

Made-up example Adult siblings

Caring for a parent from near and far

The pattern before
The sister who lived closest did nearly all the care for their mother. Her brother sent money and felt shut out. Her texts to him got shorter and sharper.
The one tool
The Chat analyzer: type a message before you send it, and see how it might land.
What changed
Her first draft was “You’ve done nothing for Mom.” She sent a specific ask instead. He took over the pharmacy orders and a Sunday phone call with their mother.
Still hard
The distance. He still can’t do the hands-on days, and some weeks that stings.

Made-up example Separated co-parents

Handovers that turned into old arguments

The pattern before
Messages about pick-up times slid into who was to blame for the breakup. The children could hear it in the doorway.
The one tool
The handover section of Separated co-parents: messages about the children only, and a short checklist for each handover.
What changed
They agreed to send logistics once a day, in the evening, kept short. Handovers became two minutes at the door with the bag already packed.
Still hard
Holidays. That conversation is still to come.

Made-up example Friends

A friendship that went quiet after a baby

The pattern before
Two friends used to talk every week. After one had a baby, calls stopped. The other felt dropped and stopped calling first.
The one tool
How you connect: a one-page card on how you like to talk and keep in touch.
What changed
Swapping cards showed one of them hates phone calls when tired but loves voice notes. They now send voice notes when a hand is free, and have a ten-minute call every other Sunday.
Still hard
Some weeks neither of them manages it. They’ve agreed that’s okay.

Made-up example Coworkers and co-founders

The co-founder who did all the admin

The pattern before
Two co-founders of a small business. One quietly did payroll, contracts and chasing invoices. It showed up as short, sharp messages in the team chat.
The one tool
The business partners section of At work: list the background jobs, and give each one an owner.
What changed
Seeing the list together, the other co-founder took invoicing. They added a ten-minute Friday check-in about who has what next week.
Still hard
In busy launch weeks the check-in still slips.

Made-up example More than two partners

Three adults, one home, and long hours

The pattern before
Three partners shared a home. Cooking and bedtime went to whoever was in. The one working long shifts was quietly seen as “not pulling their weight.”
The one tool
Who does what?, with paid work hours counted alongside the jobs at home.
What changed
With the shifts on the page, the picture looked fairer than it felt. The long-hours partner took the weekend cooking and the bills, jobs that fit their days off.
Still hard
Planning around three calendars.

Made-up example Foster and kinship caregivers

A grandmother raising her grandson

The pattern before
After each visit with his mom, her grandson was wound up for days, and so was she. Evenings after contact often ended in shouting.
The one tool
My weather today: a one-minute check on how she was doing herself.
What changed
She noticed contact days were stormy for her too, not only for him. She planned quiet, easy evenings after them and asked a neighbor to do that school drop-off.
Still hard
The visits themselves. That part isn’t hers to fix.

Made-up example Someone on their own

“There’s no one to do this with”

The pattern before
Living alone after a breakup, they thought these tools were for pairs. Meanwhile they said yes to every favor at work and in the family, and had no evenings left.
The one tool
Say no kindly: short, neutral ways to turn something down.
What changed
They picked one line and used it twice in a week. Two evenings came back.
Still hard
The guilt after saying no. It gets smaller with practice, slowly.

Made-up example Any two people

One wants to talk now, one needs space

The pattern before
In every disagreement one person followed the other from room to room, and the other went silent. Both felt unheard.
The one tool
The pause plan: a word for “I need a break,” how long the break lasts, and how you come back.
What changed
They agreed on a word and a forty-minute break, with the one who paused coming back to restart. Most arguments now end the same evening.
Still hard
In the heat of it they still forget the word sometimes.
