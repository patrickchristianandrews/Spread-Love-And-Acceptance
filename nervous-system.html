Your nervous system, in plain words

Your nervous system is how your body notices things, reacts and settles again. Feeling wired, frozen or flat is your nervous system doing its job, sometimes a bit too well. Here is how it works, what the popular ideas get right and wrong, and what helps it settle.

Not medical advice. This page explains general ideas from published research. If something on it sounds like you and it worries you, talk to a doctor.

Last checked: October 2026

The short version:

  • Your nervous system has a “go” side and a “rest” side. Both are normal, and you need both.
  • Stress is a body response with a fast part (adrenaline) and a slower part (cortisol). It is meant to switch off again.
  • What helps it settle: a longer out-breath, movement, cool water on the face, a safe person, and sleep.
  • Popular ideas such as polyvagal theory and “fawning” give useful words, but they aren’t settled science.
  • If panic, feeling unreal or low mood keeps coming back, see a doctor: when to get help.

Calm your nervous system now

Pick one. None of these takes more than a few minutes, and none needs anything you don’t have.

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for 4, out for 6, for a minute or two. Start a one-minute Breathe break
  • Cool your face. Splash cold water on your face, or hold a cool, wet cloth on your cheeks and forehead for up to 30 seconds. It slows the heart. Skip it if you have a heart condition. Why it works
  • Move for a few minutes. Walk, climb the stairs, shake out your arms. Movement has the best evidence of anything for stress and low mood. The research
  • Hum or sing quietly. It slows your out-breath without you having to count. Humming and singing
  • Turn the world down. Fewer sounds, softer light, headphones, a quieter room. Sensory tools
  • Find a safe person. Sit with someone, hold a trusted hand, or send a message. Calming together

What your nervous system does

Your nervous system has two big parts. The central nervous system is your brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system is all the nerves that reach out to the rest of your body.

Some of it you control, like moving your hand. Much of it runs on its own: your heartbeat, breathing, digestion, sweating and pupils. That automatic part is the autonomic nervous system, and it has three branches.

The three branches of the autonomic nervous system
BranchNicknameWhat it does
Sympathetic“Fight or flight,” the acceleratorSpeeds the heart and breathing, sends blood to muscles, slows digestion, widens the pupils.
Parasympathetic“Rest and digest,” the brakeSlows the heart, helps digestion, saves energy. It is most active when you are calm.
EntericThe gut’s own networkA web of nerves in the gut that manages digestion, talking with the other two.

The accelerator and the brake work together all day, not as an on-off switch. Health isn’t “always calm.” It is being able to speed up when you need to and slow down again afterward.

The stress response: fast and slow

When your brain senses a threat, real or imagined, two systems kick in.

  • The fast one (seconds): the sympathetic nerves and the adrenal glands release adrenaline. Your heart races, you breathe faster, your muscles tense and your stomach may churn.
  • The slow one (minutes): the brain signals the adrenal glands, through a chain called the HPA axis (hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal), to release cortisol. Cortisol makes more energy available and calms the immune system for a while. It then signals back to the brain to switch the response off.

This is a good system. Trouble starts when it switches on too often, stays on, or doesn’t switch off properly. Researchers call the wear and tear from that “allostatic load” (McEwen, 1998 (opens a new tab)). The same stress hormones that protect you in the short term cause harm when they run for months.

Stress or anxiety? Stress is usually a response to something outside you, like a deadline, and fades when it’s over. Anxiety can carry on without a clear threat. If it doesn’t go away and gets in the way of life, it is worth getting help (NIMH: I’m so stressed out! (opens a new tab)).

Fight, flight, freeze and “fawn”

People describe a few common reactions to threat:

  • Fight: getting angry, arguing, pushing back.
  • Flight: leaving, avoiding, keeping busy, scrolling.
  • Freeze: going still, blank or numb, unable to think or speak.
  • “Fawn”: people-pleasing to stay safe: agreeing, apologizing, smoothing things over. The word was coined by therapist Pete Walker in a 2013 book about childhood trauma. It is a popular idea, not a research term, and there isn’t yet much research on it (The Conversation, 2023 (opens a new tab)).

Some researchers also describe “tend and befriend”: under stress, many people, perhaps especially women, reach out to protect others and seek company rather than fight or flee (Taylor and colleagues, 2000 (opens a new tab)).

None of these is a character flaw. They are the body trying to keep you safe. Noticing yours (“I go quiet,” “I start fixing everyone”) is the first step to choosing what to do next.

When the past joins in

The nervous system learns from experience. When a moment resembles an earlier one, the body can react to the earlier one first: the heart jumps, the chest tightens, or you freeze, before the thinking part has caught up. That is one reason a small event can get a big reaction. Hard events are common, and most people recover without lasting trouble. For some, especially after severe or repeated events, the alarm stays easier to set off. None of this is a fault in the person.

The window of tolerance, and the ladder

Psychiatrist Dan Siegel described a “window of tolerance”: a zone where you can feel strong feelings and still think (Siegel, 1999 (opens a new tab)). Above the window, you are wired, flooded or panicky. Below it, you are flat, numb or shut down. Stress, poor sleep and hunger shrink the window. Rest, safety and support widen it.

It is a helpful picture, not something anyone can measure. This site uses a simple three-rung version, the ladder: calm, revved up, running on empty.

Polyvagal theory, honestly

You may have heard of polyvagal theory, proposed by Stephen Porges in the 1990s. It describes three states (safe and social, mobilized, and shut down) and links them to two branches of the vagus nerve. It is very popular with therapists and in self-help, and many people find its language kind and useful.

But many of its specific biological claims are disputed. Other scientists argue that its account of evolution, of how the vagus nerve is wired, and of what heart-rate measures show are not supported. A 2023 review argued that each of its five basic premises is untenable or highly implausible (Grossman, 2023 (opens a new tab); Grossman and Taylor, 2007 (opens a new tab)).

A fair summary: the everyday idea that we move between states, and that feeling safe makes connection easier, fits a lot of research. The specific polyvagal explanation is contested and isn’t settled science. You can use the words if they help. Just don’t let anyone sell you a treatment because of them.

The vagus nerve, and what helps

The vagus nerve is the longest of the nerves that start in the brain. It runs from the brainstem down through the neck to the heart, lungs and gut, carrying messages in both directions (StatPearls: the vagus nerve (opens a new tab)). It is a main part of the “rest and digest” brake: when it is active, your heart slows.

You can see it working in your own pulse: your heart speeds up a little as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out. That is one reason a longer out-breath feels calming. Researchers often use this rhythm to estimate “vagal tone,” though they disagree about how well it measures it (Grossman and Taylor, 2007 (opens a new tab)).

What helps, and how sure we are:

Ways to help your nervous system settle, with how strong the evidence is
WhatHow sureWhat the research shows
Slow breathingSome evidenceSlow breathing (often about six breaths a minute) is linked with calmer body signals and feeling less wound up (Zaccaro and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)). Training with a heart-rate sensor reduced stress and anxiety across 24 studies (Goessl and colleagues, 2017 (opens a new tab)).
MovementGood evidenceHundreds of trials show activity eases low mood, anxiety and distress (Singh and colleagues, 2023 (opens a new tab)).
A safe personEarly evidenceHolding a trusted partner’s hand lowered the brain’s response to a threat in a well-known study (Coan and colleagues, 2006 (opens a new tab)).
SleepSome evidenceAfter a night without sleep, the brain’s alarm center reacted more strongly to upsetting pictures (Yoo and colleagues, 2007 (opens a new tab)). Steadier sleep times went with better next-day mood in a large study (Fang and colleagues, 2021 (opens a new tab)).
Cold on the faceEarly evidenceCold on the neck slowed the heart and raised a sign of the calming branch in 61 healthy adults (Jungmann and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)). Not tested as an anxiety treatment.
Humming and singingEarly evidenceThey slow your out-breath. Group singing shows promise for wellbeing, in studies at risk of bias (Williams and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)).

Be wary of “vagus nerve hacks.” Gadgets, supplements and courses that promise to “reset” or “tone” your vagus nerve go well beyond the research. The free things above are the ones with evidence.

Feeling your body: interoception and alexithymia

Interoception is your sense of what is happening inside your body: your heartbeat, hunger, needing the toilet, a tight chest, tiredness. Signals from inside the body travel to the brain, and they seem to be part of how we feel emotions (Craig, 2002 (opens a new tab)). Researchers think differences in this sense play a part in anxiety and other conditions, though the science is young (Khalsa and colleagues, 2018 (opens a new tab)).

Alexithymia means finding it hard to notice or name your feelings. It is common rather than rare, and in autistic people about half score high, compared with about 1 in 20 others (Kinnaird and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)). If “how do you feel?” is a hard question, try:

  • body words instead of feeling words: “tight chest,” “hot face,” “heavy legs”;
  • a 1 to 5 rating at the same time each day;
  • noticing what you do: snapping, pacing, going quiet, scrolling.

Sensory differences

People notice and react to sound, light, smell, touch and movement very differently. Some notice everything and get overloaded quickly. Others notice less and seek more input (Dunn, 1997 (opens a new tab)). Some people are “highly sensitive,” a trait rather than a disorder (Aron and Aron, 1997 (opens a new tab)). Many autistic people and people with ADHD describe strong sensory differences.

Overload can look like irritability, shutting down or a meltdown. It is the nervous system being overwhelmed, not bad behavior. What usually helps first is turning the input down: noise-reducing headphones lowered a stress measure in a small study of autistic children (Pfeiffer and colleagues, 2019 (opens a new tab)), and breaks, quiet spaces and predictable plans help many people.

Calming together (co-regulation)

Nervous systems affect each other. In close relationships, each person’s feelings and body state nudge the other’s. Researchers call this two-way link co-regulation (Butler and Randall, 2013 (opens a new tab)). Calm is catching, and so is stress. Our brains seem to expect support: with trusted people around, things feel less risky and take less effort (Coan and Sbarra, 2015 (opens a new tab)).

  • One calm person first. If you can, settle yourself before you try to settle someone else.
  • Lower and slower. A quieter voice, slower pace and fewer words help.
  • Side by side. Walking or doing something together can be easier than face to face.
  • Ask. “Do you want help, or company?”

This works for couples, friends, family, roommates and coworkers alike. It needs safety: if someone frightens or controls you, calming them isn’t your job.

How stress shows up between people

When your nervous system is revved up, the thinking part of the brain goes partly offline. People say things they don’t mean, stop listening, go silent, or over-explain. When you’re running on empty, everything feels like too much. Neither person is to blame. Both bodies are doing what bodies do.

A short pause to settle, then coming back to talk, often works better than pushing through. These tools on the site help:

When to see a doctor

Please see a doctor if:

  • you have panic attacks: a doctor can check for other causes of the symptoms and talk through treatment (NHS: panic disorder (opens a new tab));
  • you often feel unreal, detached or outside your body: a doctor can check for other causes and refer you if needed (NHS: dissociative disorders (opens a new tab));
  • stress or anxiety won’t go away, or gets in the way of work, sleep, eating or seeing people (NIMH: I’m so stressed out! (opens a new tab));
  • you have felt low or numb most days for two weeks or more.

Chest pain, fainting or trouble breathing: get urgent medical help. Don’t assume it is “just anxiety.”
