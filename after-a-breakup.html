After a breakup: steady again

Simple versionRead the full guide

This page is for the weeks after a relationship ends, whoever ended it, however long it lasted. It is about getting steady again: the first days, feelings that come in waves, a kinder voice in your head, working out who you are now, their photos and posts, and small steps that count. It’s written for you on your own. For the practical side (money, a home for one, part-time parenting), see On my own after a breakup or divorce.

The first days: getting through

In the first days, the job is small: get through each one. You don’t have to understand what happened yet, decide what it means, or be “over it.”

  • Keep three basics going: some sleep, a proper meal, and daylight every day.
  • Tell one or two people what has happened, and what would help: company, a walk, or just a message each evening.
  • Hold off on big decisions you don’t have to make this week.
  • Go easy on late-night messages to your ex, alcohol, and scrolling their profiles.

Words you could use

To a friend: “We’ve split up. I don’t need advice yet. Could you text me in the evenings this week?”

To yourself: “Today I only need to get through today.”

Feelings come in waves

Sadness, anger, relief, guilt, missing them, not missing them: often in the same afternoon. That mix is normal. It doesn’t mean you made the wrong choice, or that you’ll feel like this for ever.

Feelings after a breakup usually come in waves: a hard hour, then an ordinary one. Over weeks, the hard waves tend to come less often. Many people recover faster than they predicted they would. Most people, after a loss, find their way back to steady.

For example (made up): Priya, 31, cried in the supermarket three weeks after her breakup and felt she was going backwards. Her sister said, “That’s a wave, not a slide.” She started noting the good hours too, and by week six there were clearly more of them.

A kinder inner voice

After a breakup, the voice in your head can get harsh: “I ruined it.” “Nobody will want me.” “I should be over this.” Being hard on yourself feels like it should help. It usually doesn’t.

Self-compassion means talking to yourself the way you’d talk to a good friend: kindly, remembering that heartbreak happens to almost everyone, and noticing the feeling without drowning in it. People who spoke about their separation this way recovered more easily over the following months.

Words you could use

“This really hurts, and it makes sense that it hurts.”

“Lots of people have felt exactly this. I’m not the only one.”

“I can learn from what went wrong without deciding I’m the problem.”

Who am I now?

When two lives were tangled together, part of “me” was “us”: friends, habits, plans, even the music you played. After a breakup, many people feel unsure who they are. That fog is a normal part of it, and it tends to lift.

What helps is small and regular: noticing what is yours. What you enjoy, who you are with friends, what you want next. Thinking about the breakup now and then, in a calm and structured way, seems to help people feel clearer about themselves.

  • Write three things you liked doing before the relationship, or wanted to try.
  • Write one thing you want your next year to have in it.
  • Do one of them this week, however small.

Their posts and photos

Checking an ex’s profile is very human. It can also keep the hurt fresh: people who check more tend to feel more distress and longing. You don’t have to unfriend or block anyone. Muting, archiving photos, or moving them to a folder you don’t open is a kind choice for you, not a statement to them.

When a memory pulls at you, two things help in different ways. Reminding yourself of what didn’t work can loosen the pull, though it may lower your mood for a while. Doing something absorbing lifts your mood without changing the feelings. Using a bit of both is fine.

For example (made up): Tom, 26, kept checking his ex’s stories each night. He muted her and put the phone on the other side of the room after 10 pm. He still thought about her, but the nights were calmer.

Write it out

Writing about what happened and how you feel, for fifteen minutes on a few days, helps some people. It doesn’t need to be good, and nobody needs to read it. If it stirs up too much, stop and do something gentle instead.

Sleep and moving

A broken night makes everything feel lonelier and the world less friendly. And moving your body, even a walk, helps low mood and worry. These aren’t extras. They are part of getting steady.

Small steps that count

Recovery is mostly small things done again and again. A shower. A message to a friend. Cooking one real meal. Each is worth noticing.

When it feels heavy

Some days will be heavy. If the heaviness doesn’t lift over a few weeks, if you can’t sleep or eat, or if you feel hopeless, please tell someone today: a friend, your doctor, or a helpline. Outside help can make a real difference. Help beyond this site lists counsellors and free helplines.

Questions people often ask

How long does it take to get over a breakup?

There’s no set time. Feelings usually come in waves that get smaller over weeks and months, and many people recover faster than they expect. If it isn’t easing at all after a couple of months, talking to someone can help.

Should I stay friends with my ex on social media?

That’s up to you. What seems to matter more is how often you check their profile. Muting or archiving can give you space without a big statement.

I ended it. Why do I feel so bad?

Because it is still a loss: of a person, a routine and a future you had pictured. Relief and grief can come together.

Is it normal not to know who I am any more?

Yes. When a relationship ends, people often feel less sure of themselves for a while. Small, regular reflection and doing things that are yours tends to help it settle.
