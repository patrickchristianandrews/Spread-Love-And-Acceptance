Caring for someone you love

This page is for anyone caring for a parent, a husband, wife or partner, or another person who needs a lot of help. You are doing hard work, much of it unseen. Read the part you need.

When the person can’t share the load back

A lot of this site is about sharing the work of a home fairly between two people. Caregiving can be different. If the person you care for has dementia, or an illness that gets worse over time, they may not be able to take jobs back. That is not their fault, and it is not yours.

So the fair split is not between you and them. The fair split is between you and everyone else: brothers and sisters, grown children, friends, neighbors, a faith community, and paid help. The question to ask is not “Is he doing his part?” It is “Who else can carry some of this?”

It helps to write down every part of the care, including the thinking work: remembering the pills, booking appointments, watching for changes. Then you can see which parts someone else could own.

Grieving someone who is still here

You may miss the person you knew, even while you sit with them every day. Your mother may not know your name some days. Your husband may not be able to talk with you the way he used to. This is a real loss, and it is normal to grieve it.

Some people call this “ambiguous loss”: the person is here in body, but part of who they were is gone. Other people may not notice it. You can still be sad about it. Feeling grief now does not mean you love them less.

The page When you’ve lost someone has more on grief that comes in waves.

Helpers to recruit

People often say “Let me know if you need anything.” It is kind, but hard to use. A specific ask is easier to say yes to. Here are some ideas.

  • Adult children who live far away can still help a lot: a set weekly call with the person you care for (for example, every Sunday at 6), paying bills or handling insurance online, booking appointments, paying for a sitter so you can rest, or coming for a visit of a few days so you can have time off.
  • Friends can do one regular thing: a meal on Wednesdays, a drive to one appointment, sitting with the person for two hours.
  • Neighbors can pick up a prescription, take out the bins, or check in if the lights are off.
  • Your faith community may have visitors, meals or volunteers who sit with people at home.
  • Paid help and respite care. A home aide for a few hours, an adult day program, or a short stay in a care home so you can rest. Using paid help is not giving up. It is how care lasts.

Words you could use

To an adult child: “Could you take over the insurance calls and call Dad every Sunday at 6? That would give me real breathing room.”

To a friend: “Could you sit with Mom on Thursday afternoon for two hours? I need to see my own doctor.”

Resentment and guilt

Many caregivers feel angry or resentful sometimes: at the illness, at family who don’t help, even at the person they care for. Then they feel guilty for feeling that way. These feelings are normal. They are not a failing, and they do not mean you are a bad person. They are usually a sign that you are carrying too much for too long.

What helps:

  • Say it to someone safe. A friend, a support group or a counselor. Saying it out loud often makes it lighter.
  • Take real breaks. Not only sleep. Something that is just for you, even for an hour a week.
  • Ask for one more helper. Resentment often means the load needs to be shared more widely.

Where to get support

  • Alzheimer’s Association 24/7 Helpline (US): call 800-272-3900, any time, day or night. For any kind of dementia, not only Alzheimer’s. More at alz.org.
  • Eldercare Locator (US): call 1-800-677-1116 or visit eldercare.acl.gov to find local services, like meals, transport and respite care.
  • Ask his or her doctor, or a hospital social worker, about adult day programs and respite care near you.
  • Caregiver support groups meet in person and online. The two lines above can help you find one.
  • If it gets too heavy, or you have thoughts of not wanting to be here, call or text 988 (US), any time. In danger right now, call 911.
  • Outside the US, findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country.
