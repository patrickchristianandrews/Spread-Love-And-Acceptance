Caring for a partner (not a parent)

If the person you care for is your husband, wife or partner, things are often different from caring for a parent. Their energy may go up and down from day to day, after a stroke or with illnesses like ME/CFS, MS, Parkinson’s, long COVID or chronic pain. What they can do on good days counts, and so does what they plan and handle from bed: the grocery order, the bills, the calendar. You are still two partners sharing a life, with one of you on less energy.

When one of you is ill or disabled is written for both of you: sharing the load by energy, not hours, good-day and bad-day owners, and something gentle to do together on a hard day.

When the person can’t share the load back

A lot of this site is about sharing the work of a home fairly between two people. Caregiving can be different. If the person you care for has dementia, has had a stroke that changed what they can do, or has an illness that gets worse over time, they may not be able to take jobs back. That is not their fault, and it is not yours.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: When the person can’t share the load back

So the fair split is not between you and them. The fair split is between you and everyone else: brothers and sisters, grown children, friends, neighbors, a faith community, and paid help. The question to ask is not “Is he doing his part?” It is “Who else can carry some of this?”

It helps to write down every part of the care, including the thinking work: remembering the pills, booking appointments, watching for changes. Then you can see which parts someone else could own.

Grieving someone who is still here

You may miss the person you knew, even while you sit with them every day. Your mother may not know your name some days. Your husband may not be able to talk with you the way he used to. This is a real loss, and it is normal to grieve it.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Grieving someone who is still here

Some people call this “ambiguous loss”: the person is here in body, but part of who they were is gone. Other people may not notice it. You can still be sad about it. Feeling grief now does not mean you love them less.

The page When you’ve lost someone has more on grief that comes in waves.

When your partner lives in a nursing home

When a husband, wife or partner moves into a nursing home, often because of dementia or after a stroke, the caring doesn’t stop. It changes shape: visits instead of daily care, and an empty house in the evenings. Many people find this stretch harder than they expected, and very few people talk about it.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: When your partner lives in a nursing home
  • When they don’t know you on a visit. Your visit still counts. Familiar music, a hand to hold, a photo album or a short walk can comfort them even when your name doesn’t come. Short, calm visits are often better than long ones. See moments that aren’t care for small things to do together.
  • When you don’t want to go. Some days the thought of visiting feels too heavy. That is common, and it doesn’t mean you love them less. It’s fine to visit less often, or for less time, and to ask a family member or friend to take a turn. The staff are there every day. You don’t have to be. More in Resentment and guilt.
  • Grieving someone who is still here. You may miss them while they are alive. That loss is real. See Grieving someone who is still here.
  • The long, quiet evenings. Plan one small thing for most evenings: a phone call, a program you look forward to, a walk, a group that meets weekly. Evenings and weekends that are yours has ideas, and the Night Garden is a calm few minutes before bed.

Words you could use

To your children: “I’m finding the visits hard on my own. Could one of you come with me on Sundays?”

To the nursing home staff: “What does a good day look like for her now? I’d like to visit at that time.”

Bonding tips: moments that aren’t care

When every visit is pills, meals and appointments, you can start to feel more like a nurse than a daughter, son or partner. A few moments a day that aren’t care help you both remember who you are to each other.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Bonding tips: moments that aren’t care
  • Play music from their younger years. Songs often reach people even late in dementia, and singing along can be a real moment together.
  • Look through old photos together, and let them tell the stories, even the ones you have heard many times.
  • Let them help with what they can. Folding towels, stirring a pot, choosing the flowers. Being useful matters to everyone.
  • Keep one small ritual from before: tea at four, the crossword, the Sunday call with the grandchildren.
  • Offer gentle touch, if they welcome it: holding hands, brushing their hair, a hand massage with cream.
  • If it’s your partner, keep ten minutes a day that is about the two of you, not the care plan.

Words you could use

“Shall we put on your old records and just sit for a bit?”

Helpers to recruit

People often say “Let me know if you need anything.” It is kind, but hard to use. A specific ask is easier to say yes to. Here are some ideas.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Helpers to recruit
  • Adult children who live far away can still help a lot: a set weekly call with the person you care for (for example, every Sunday at 6), paying bills or handling insurance online, booking appointments, paying for a sitter so you can rest, or coming for a visit of a few days so you can have time off.
  • Friends can do one regular thing: a meal on Wednesdays, a drive to one appointment, sitting with the person for two hours.
  • Neighbors can pick up a prescription, take out the trash, or check in if the lights are off.
  • Your faith community may have visitors, meals or volunteers who sit with people at home.
  • Paid help and respite care. A home aide for a few hours, an adult day program, or a short stay in a nursing home so you can rest. Using paid help is not giving up. It is how care lasts.

Words you could use

To an adult child: “Could you take over the insurance calls and call Dad every Sunday at 6? That would give me real breathing room.”

To a friend: “Could you sit with Mom on Thursday afternoon for two hours? I need a little time to myself.”

Talking about it: with the person you care for, family and doctors

Care brings a lot of hard talks: with someone who may be in pain, confused or proud, with brothers and sisters who see things differently, and with busy doctors. And you are usually tired when they happen.

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Talking about it: with the person you care for, family and doctors

With the person you care for

  • One question at a time, in short sentences, and wait for the answer. With memory loss, offer two choices instead of an open question.
  • Ask before you help. “Would you like a hand with that?” protects their dignity.
  • Answer the feeling, not the facts, if they are confused. “You’re worried about the bus” helps more than “There is no bus.”

With family

  • Share the facts, then ask for one thing. A short weekly update by message keeps everyone looking at the same picture.
  • Listen to the sibling who sees it differently. Staying away can look like not caring when it is really fear or guilt.

With doctors and care staff

  • Bring a written list: your three main questions, and any changes you’ve seen, with dates.
  • Write down the answers, and before you leave, ask: “What should we watch for, and who do we call?”

Words you could use

To a sibling: “Here’s how Mom was this week. I need one thing from you: could you take the pharmacy run on Fridays?”

To a doctor: “I have three questions. The first is about her sleep. Is it all right if I write down your answers?”

Resentment and guilt

Many caregivers feel angry or resentful sometimes: at the illness, at family who don’t help, even at the person they care for. Then they feel guilty for feeling that way. These feelings are normal. They are usually a sign that you are carrying too much for too long.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Resentment and guilt

What helps:

  • Say it to someone safe. A friend or someone in your family. Saying it out loud often makes it lighter.
  • Take real breaks. Not only sleep. Something that is just for you, even for an hour a week.
  • Ask for one more helper. Resentment often means the load needs to be shared more widely.
