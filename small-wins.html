Small wins, and turning the hard stuff into fuel

Big changes are made of small ones. And hard feelings, like anger, worry or regret, often carry useful information. This page is about both: building small wins, and turning the hard stuff into a next step. On your own, or with a partner, friends, kids, housemates or people at work.

In short:

  • Make the next step tiny. Then notice, out loud, when it’s done.
  • Hard feelings are signals, not verdicts. Ask what each one is pointing at.
  • Turn a complaint into a kind, specific request, and a worry into a plan.
  • If it’s too heavy, a new way of looking at it is not the fix. Get support.

Why small wins matter

Some goals are so big there is no first step, like “be closer as a family”. A small win is a piece you can finish, and see finished. Seeing progress keeps people going.

  • Small wins. In 1984 the psychologist Karl Weick wrote that huge problems can freeze people. Cut into small, concrete pieces, they feel doable, and each finished piece makes the next one easier.
  • The progress principle. Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer studied the daily diaries of people at work. What made a workday feel good, more than anything, was progress on work that mattered, even small progress.
  • Starting tiny. In a study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues, new habits took weeks to months to feel automatic, varying a lot between people. Missing a day didn’t undo the progress.
  • Tiny habits. BJ Fogg’s “Tiny Habits” ties a very small step to something you already do, then a little “yes!” It is a coach’s method more than a tested finding.

This site’s idea: a small win counts double when someone else notices it too.

How to use small wins

  • Make the next step tiny and concrete. Not “sort out the garage”, but “fill one box for the charity shop”. Not “talk more”, but “ten minutes on the sofa after dinner, phones down”.
  • Keep a done list, not only a to-do list. A to-do list shows what’s missing. A done list shows how far you’ve come. There is one further down this page.
  • Name wins out loud. “We did the check-in three weeks running.” Saying it makes it real for both of you.
  • Celebrate the effort, not only the result. You can’t control every outcome. You can control showing up.
  • Give each job one owner. With one owner per job, the win and the thank-you have somewhere to land.
  • After a setback, go smaller. If the plan fell apart, shrink the next step until it feels almost too easy. Then do it.
  • Don’t turn wins into a scoreboard. A done list is for encouragement, not proving who does more. If it starts to feel like keeping score, stop counting and say thanks.

Thoughts you could try: “I didn’t do all of it. I did the first part, and that counts.”

Small wins together

  • Partners: try a weekly “what went right” minute. Each says one thing that went well and one thank-you. It is close to Martin Seligman’s “three good things” exercise, where effects were modest but encouraging.
  • Housemates: tick off shared jobs on one list everyone can see. Empty bins are a win for the house, not a point for one person.
  • Friends: “We actually met up twice this month!” is worth saying.
  • Parents and kids: praise the effort and the way they tried, not “you’re so clever”. Studies by Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck pointed this way; later work finds the effect is often modest. Let kids tick their own box.
  • At work: tell someone when their small fix helped. Start a meeting with one minute of “what got done”. More in Appreciation at work.

Words you could use: “Can I just say: we did the check-in three weeks running. I’m proud of us.”

Words you could use: “Thanks for sorting the bins this week. I noticed, and it helped.”

Words you could use, with a child: “You kept trying when it was hard. That’s how people get good at things.”

What hard feelings are for

Anger, worry, sadness, envy, regret. Nobody enjoys them, but they are not mistakes. They point at something that matters to you, or needs care. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict. It doesn’t tell you who is wrong, or what will happen.

  • Bad weighs more than good. Roy Baumeister and colleagues reviewed a large body of research in a paper called “Bad is stronger than good”. Paul Rozin and Edward Royzman described the same pattern as the negativity bias. One harsh comment can outweigh several kind ones. That is why small, frequent good moments matter, and why one bad day can feel like the whole picture.
  • Fighting a feeling often makes it louder. In Daniel Wegner’s studies, people told not to think of a white bear thought of it more. James Gross and others found that hiding feelings tends to cost you, and the people around you.
  • The goal is not toxic positivity. “Just look on the bright side” can feel like being told your feelings are wrong. This page is not about pretending. It is about using what the feeling is telling you.

Thoughts you could try: “I’m angry. That’s information. What is it pointing at?”

Turning it into fuel

Here are some ways to put hard feelings to work. Pick the one that fits today.

Name it to tame it

Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down a little. In brain-scanning studies by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, labelling a feeling went with a calmer response. (The phrase is the psychiatrist Dan Siegel’s.) Try one precise word: not “bad”, but “left out” or “embarrassed”.

Anger: what is it protecting?

Anger often shows up when something you care about gets stepped on: fairness, respect, your time. Ask, “What is this protecting?” Then turn it into a need you can say, once you’ve calmed down.

A complaint is a hidden request

John Gottman’s research separates a complaint (“I’m upset the dishes were left”) from criticism (“You’re so lazy”). Under most complaints is a request, so say the request.

Words you could use: Instead of “You never help after dinner”, try “Could you do the dishes on weeknights? It would help me sit down by eight.”

Worry into a plan

Gabriele Oettingen’s research found that picturing a good outcome works better when you also picture the obstacle and plan for it. Her method is WOOP: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.

Look again, without pretending

James Gross calls this reappraisal: seeing the same moment another way that is also true. “She’s ignoring me” might be “she’s on a deadline”. If the hard version is true, you don’t have to talk yourself out of it.

If you expect the worst, and it helps

Julie Norem studied “defensive pessimism”: some people cope by picturing what could go wrong, then getting ready. Being cheered up can make it worse for them. It is a style, not a flaw. Let the worry write the checklist, then put it down.

Stress as energy

Studies by Alia Crum and colleagues suggest that seeing stress as energy for a challenge can change how it feels. The evidence is still developing, and it is no reason to put up with stress that never stops.

Regret and envy as information

This site’s idea: regret often points at something you value. “I wish I’d called Dad more” can become a call this week. Envy can show what you want: if a friend’s news stings, what would you like more of? Neither makes you a bad person.

Growth after hard times

Some people say a very hard time changed them in ways they value. Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun call this post-traumatic growth. It doesn’t happen for everyone, it isn’t required, and it never means you “had to” go through it. Getting through is enough.

Thoughts you could try: “This hurts because I care about ___. What’s one small thing I can do for that today?”

Turning the hard stuff into something useful, together

  • Turn a fight into an agreement. When calm, ask, “What did we each actually need?” Write one line you both agree on.
  • Turn a frustration that keeps coming back into one small experiment. “Let’s try a list on the fridge for two weeks, then see.” An experiment can fail without anyone failing.
  • Turn criticism you received into one useful thing. Ask, “Is one part of this true and useful?” Keep that part. If it is cruel, vague or about who you are rather than what you did, you can let it go.
  • Notice instead of keeping score. Keeping score asks, “Who’s behind?” Noticing asks, “What’s working?” The Lemonade Stand helps you see the whole load without blame.

Words you could use: “That last argument was rough. Can we each pick one thing we’d do differently next time?”

Words you could use: “Let’s try it for two weeks, then check in. If it doesn’t work, we change the plan, not blame each other.”

Words you could use, at work: “Thanks for the feedback. Could you give me one example, so I know what to change?”

If you are afraid, it is not a reframing problem. If an argument leaves you scared, or one person controls the money, the phone or who you see, please put your safety first. See Not safe at home?

When it’s too much

Sometimes the hard stuff is a weight, not a signal. You may feel low most days for weeks, or worn out in a way rest doesn’t fix. You may be grieving, or frightened. Then a new way of looking at it is not the fix, and nobody should ask you to “stay positive”. You deserve real support.

  • Where am I right now?A one-minute check on how your body is doing, with what tends to help at each step.
  • Not safe at home?Free, private people to talk to, and a quick way to leave this site.

Finding a counsellor: your doctor can suggest one. Many workplaces, colleges and universities offer free, private counselling (at work it may be called an employee assistance programme). Professional bodies in many countries keep a list of qualified counsellors and therapists.

If you are thinking about ending your life or hurting yourself: in the US, call or text 988. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free lines by country. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number.
