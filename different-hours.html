Different hours: nights, shifts, and the one at home

One of you works nights or rotating shifts. Or one of you works from home and seems to be “around” all day. You share a home, but not a clock. This page helps you count the work fairly, protect sleep, and still find time for each other. Not nights, but very long days, with the other one running the home and the kids? Night shifts and recovery sleep are part of the work

A twelve-hour night shift doesn’t end at the front door. Your body needs sleep in the day to do the next one. That sleep is part of the job, not rest or leisure. When one of you is asleep at noon, it can look like a day off from the outside. It isn’t.

Shift work costs in ways nobody sees, too: a body clock out of step with everyone else, missed dinners, birthdays and weekends at work. It is fine to name those.

Words you could use

“My sleep after a night is part of the shift. Can we count it with my work hours, not as rest?”

“I know your nights are real work. I still need us to share the house. Can we look at what fits your days off?”

Being home isn’t the same as being free

If you work from home, people may treat you as always available: the parcel, the dog, the plumber, the call from school. Each one is small. Together they eat your working day, and the house slides onto you by default.

  • Set work hours and say them out loud, as you would in an office.
  • Agree which home jobs fit a break (start the washing, walk the dog) and which don’t (wait in all day for a repair).
  • Write the default jobs down, so they can be shared instead of assumed.

“I’m working nine to five, even though I’m at home. Can we agree what I’ll do in my breaks, and what waits until after?”

Long days, not nights

Sometimes the hours aren’t odd, just long. One of you works fifty or sixty hours a week of days, plus the commute. The other runs the home and the children, often alone from breakfast to bedtime. Both of you end the day empty, and each can feel the other doesn’t see it.

  • Count both people’s hours. Paid work and travel on one side; on the other, the hours of childcare and housework. Looking after small children on your own all day is a full-time job.
  • Solo childcare is work. The hours one of you has the children alone while the other is at work go on the list, the same as paid hours.
  • Fair doesn’t have to mean half and half. Talk about what feels fair for this season of your lives, and look again when it changes.
  • Some jobs can belong to the one who works long days: the evening or weekend ones, like bath and bedtime, the dinner dishes, or the Saturday shop.
  • A real break for each of you, even a short one, on the calendar: not only for the one who was out at work.

Words you could use

From the one working long days: “I know the day with the kids is relentless. What would help most when I get in?”

From the one at home: “I know how hard you work, and I’m grateful. I’m empty by six too. Could bedtime be yours on weeknights?”

Together: “Can we write down all of both our hours this week, paid and unpaid, and see what feels fair?”

To count it without blame, use Who does what?: put the long days under Work & school hours, and the solo childcare beside them. En español: Cuando uno trabaja muchas horas.

Protected sleep hours

  • Agree the hours, for example 9 am to 4 pm after a night shift, and put them on a shared calendar.
  • In those hours: no non-urgent calls, deliveries booked for later, noisy jobs another time. A sign on the bedroom door helps visitors and kids.
  • Agree what is urgent enough to wake someone. A sick child, yes. Where the remote is, no.
  • Make the room work for day sleep: dark curtains, earplugs or steady background noise, and the phone on do-not-disturb with favourites let through.

“Can my sleep be nine to four on the days after nights? If something’s really urgent, call twice and I’ll pick up.”

A check-in at the shift change

The moment one of you comes home and the other heads out, or to bed, is often your only overlap. Make it a short, kind handover, not a list of problems.

  • Two minutes: How was it? What does today need? Anything I should know?
  • Apart? A voice note does the job. Leave one as your shift ends, and listen when you wake.
  • Bigger topics wait for your together time. Not at 7 am after a night shift.

“Rough night, I’m wiped. The bins are out. Can we talk about the weekend at dinner on Thursday?”

The 90-second daily check-in fits well at the start or end of a shift. For a bigger topic, plan a calm check-in at a time that suits both body clocks.

Talking about it: when your clocks don’t match

When you work different hours, you often talk when one of you is wiped out and the other is wide awake. Much of what you say goes by text or voice note. A tired brain hears blame where there isn’t any, and a short text reads cold.

  • Check the state before the topic. Straight after a night shift isn’t the time. Say when would be.
  • Listen first at the handover. Ask how it went, and hear the answer before you add your news.
  • Say your tone in texts. “Short because I’m on a break. All good!” saves hours of worry.
  • Make clear asks. “Can you book the plumber for Thursday?” beats “Someone needs to sort the sink.”
  • Repair fast. A snappy word at 7 a.m. is common. A quick “Sorry, I was wrecked” later fixes most of it.

Words you could use

“I want to talk about the weekend, but you’ve just done a night. When will you be rested enough?”

“Sorry I was short this morning. I was wiped out. It wasn’t about you.”

One fixed together time a week

When your hours never line up, time together won’t happen by chance. Pick one regular slot that fits both clocks: breakfast after a night shift, a late lunch on a shared day off, a walk before the evening shift. Treat it like a shift you can’t swap.

  • Keep it simple: food, a walk, a show. Not the week’s problems.
  • If the rota changes, move it. Don’t drop it. Pick the new time as soon as you see the new rota.

“When the new rota comes out, can we pick our together time first, before anything else goes in?”

Bonding tips when your clocks don’t match

When you rarely share an evening, you can start to feel like housemates passing in the hall. Small touches that fit around the shifts keep you close between your together times.

  • Leave something for the one coming home. A note on the kettle or a plate in the oven says “I thought about you”, even while you sleep.
  • Share a meal, whatever it is called. Your breakfast can be their dinner. Twenty minutes at the table together counts.
  • Send one photo from your day. The sunrise from the car park, the dog asleep. It lets the other person into hours they never see.
  • Keep a running note of things to tell each other, so the good stories don’t get lost before you overlap.
  • Lie down together for ten minutes before one of you sleeps, even if the other gets up again. Being close doesn’t need the same bedtime.

Words you could use

“I left you a note on the kettle. Breakfast together when you’re up? Your breakfast, my lunch.”

Your week, on one page

  1. Write down both schedules for the week, including sleep.
  2. Mark the protected sleep hours.
  3. Mark the overlaps. Those are your check-in moments.
  4. Pick your one together time.
  5. Share the home jobs around the hours each of you is actually awake and home. The one at home in the day might take deliveries; the one on nights might take the big shop or batch cooking on days off.

Look at it again whenever the rota changes. It takes ten minutes and saves a week of crossed wires.

Counting it fairly in Who does what?

Who does what? shows who does which home jobs, as a plain picture with no blame. To make it fair when you work different hours:

  • Put shifts under Work & school hours. They sit beside the other person’s paid work, outside the home-jobs split.
  • Count recovery sleep with the shifts, as part of the work week. Don’t log it as rest or time to yourself.
  • Then look at the home jobs together, around the hours each of you is home and awake, and give each one an owner.

Questions people often ask

My partner sleeps all day after night shifts. Is that fair?

Sleep after a night shift is part of the work, not time off. Count the shifts and the recovery sleep together, then share the home jobs around the hours each of you is awake and home.

How do we find time together when we work opposite shifts?

Pick one fixed slot a week that fits both body clocks, like breakfast after a night shift, and keep it like a shift you can’t swap. Add a two-minute check-in, or a voice note, at each shift change.

I work from home and end up doing everything. What can we do?

Being home isn’t the same as being free. Say your work hours out loud, agree which jobs fit in a break, and write the rest down so each one has an owner.
