When it’s tense with your parents

You’re becoming your own person. You want more say over your time, your space and your choices. That’s supposed to happen.

Your parents are adjusting too, and they’re often a step behind. They’re used to being in charge of a younger you. A lot of the friction lives in that gap. It doesn’t mean anyone is doing it wrong.

Maybe you live in two homes, or there’s a stepparent in the picture. Different houses can have different rules, and that’s allowed. You don’t have to choose between the people you care about. If it helps, show the grown-ups the stepfamilies part of our parents page.

Asking for something so it actually lands

  1. Pick the moment. Not when they just walked in, are driving in traffic or are already annoyed. Later that evening usually beats right now.
  2. Say what you want, and why. Be specific. “Can I go to Maya’s on Saturday until 11? Her mom will be home and I’ll get a ride back.” Specific sounds thought through. Vague sounds like a trap.
  3. Offer something. A check-in text at 9. Chores done first. A trial run, once, so they can see it go fine. It’s easier for them to say yes when they don’t have to take it all on trust.
  4. If it’s no, ask what would make it a yes. Next time, or a smaller version of it. That keeps the door open.

When it blows up

Some talks go sideways. Voices go up and nobody is listening anymore. At that point, more talking usually makes it worse.

  • Step away, but say you’ll be back. Walking off without a word feels like a slammed door to them. Saying when you’ll come back is different.
  • Cool down first. A walk, music, a shower, a few slow breaths. Whatever brings you back to normal.
  • Come back. Even a short “I didn’t like how that went” counts. Coming back is the part that fixes things.

Words you could use

“I’m too heated to talk well right now. Can we try again after dinner?”

“I’m not trying to fight. I just want you to hear my side before you decide.”

Friends, drama and the group chat

Friendships matter a lot right now, so when they go wrong it can feel huge. That’s not you being dramatic. It just hurts.

Group chats and screenshots

  • Assume anything you type can be screenshotted. Even in a chat with people you trust. If you wouldn’t want it shown around, say it in person or not at all.
  • Text loses tone. A short reply can read as cold when they were just busy. If something stings, ask before you react.
  • Got screenshotted? You don’t have to fire back in the chat. Talk to the person one on one, away from the audience.

Being left out

Seeing photos of a hangout you weren’t invited to stings. Sometimes it means something. Often it doesn’t. Plans get made fast and people forget. If it keeps happening, ask one person you trust, privately: “Hey, I noticed I’ve missed the last few things. Is something up?” And it’s okay to put more time into the friends who show up for you.

Saying no without losing friends

You can say no to things and still be a good friend. Keep it short and friendly. You don’t owe a long explanation.

Words you could use

“Nah, I’m going to sit this one out. Have fun though.”

“I’m not comfortable with that. Can we do something else?”

Real friends can handle a no. If someone drops you because you said no once, that tells you something useful about them.

Big feelings, stress and your own wiring

Feelings can hit hard right now. Something small can feel enormous for a while. It passes, even when it doesn’t feel like it will.

When it’s a lot

  • Name it. Angry, embarrassed, left out, overwhelmed. Putting a word on it, even just in your head, can take some of the heat out.
  • Move your body. A walk, a run, stretching, dancing in your room. Stress lives in your body, not just your head.
  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six, a few times. It sounds too simple to work. Try it anyway.
  • Make the stress smaller. If it’s schoolwork, pick just the next small step. Not the whole thing, just the next ten minutes.

Your own wiring

Some people need more quiet. Some people’s brains jump around fast. Some people notice every sound, or worry more than their friends, or need more time to answer. Some of that has names, like ADHD, autism or anxiety. Some of it is just how you are.

You don’t need a label to understand yourself. Knowing what helps you focus, calm down or recharge is useful either way. And it makes it easier to tell other people what you need.

Dating: what healthy looks like

Every relationship has rough days. But some things should always be true, and some things are a warning sign no matter how much you like someone.

Healthy looks like

  • You can say no, to anything, and it’s respected.
  • You still have your own friends and your own stuff going on.
  • Your phone is yours. Your passwords are yours.
  • You can disagree without being scared of what happens next.
  • You feel more like yourself around them, not less.

Warning signs

  • They check your phone, or want your passwords or your location all the time.
  • They tell you who you can see or talk to.
  • They make you scared: yelling, threats, breaking things, hurting you.
  • They pressure you about photos or sex, or guilt you when you say no.
  • They put you down, then say they were joking.

None of this is your fault, and you don’t have to figure it out alone. If something feels off, it’s worth talking to someone, even if you’re not sure.

When it’s more than a bad day

Sometimes it isn’t one bad day. It’s weeks of feeling empty, or hopeless, or like everyone would be better off without you.

Reaching out when it’s like that isn’t being dramatic. It isn’t attention-seeking. It’s the smart move, and the people on these lines talk with people your age every day.

If you’re thinking about hurting yourself or ending your life:

  • Call or text 988 (US), any time, day or night. You can just text if talking feels like too much.
  • Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741 (US).
  • In danger right now: call 911.
  • Outside the US: find a free line near you at findahelpline.com.

Tell an adult you trust. It doesn’t have to be a parent. A teacher, school counselor, aunt, uncle, grandparent, coach, or a friend’s parent all count. If the first person doesn’t get it, try someone else. You deserve someone who listens.

Words you could use

“Can I talk to you about something? I haven’t been okay for a while.”
