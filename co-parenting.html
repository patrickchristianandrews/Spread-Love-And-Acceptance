Disagreements between two homes are normal, and most of them can be handled. After a separation, the goal is calm, brief, child-focused contact. You can’t control the other parent, only your side of the exchange. This page gives you scripts for a cooperative co-parent, and a different route for when cooperating isn’t possible.

Before you start

A court order or parenting plan always comes first.

Four steps for calmer handoffs

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Four steps for calmer handoffs
  1. Keep it short and about the child Say what the other parent needs to know, and nothing else. Leave old arguments out of the doorway.

    “Hi. Homework’s done. She’s had dinner. Pick-up is Sunday at five.”

  2. Never use the kids as messengers Don’t ask a child to carry a message, report on the other home, or choose a side. Send messages to the adult.
  3. Give each task one owner Doctor visits, school forms, activities, pick-ups. Put the list in a shared calendar or a short note, so nobody has to ask twice. The One owner per job list for two homes helps, whether you fill it in together or on your own.
  4. Move the bigger things to a set time If something bigger comes up at a handoff, park it.

    “Can we put that on Thursday’s check-in?”

If pick-ups keep running late: say it once, in writing, about the child, with the time and what you need. A plain statement is fine. It doesn’t have to be a question.

“Pick-up has been after 5:30 three times this month, and Mia waits at the window. If you’re running more than 15 minutes late, text me by 4:30 so I can tell her.”

Talking about it: when it’s tense between two homes

After a separation, old hurts sit under every message. A note about shoes can read like a judgment on your parenting. The goal isn’t to agree about the past. It’s to talk well enough for the kids.

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: Talking about it: when it’s tense between two homes
  • Read it twice, then wait. Unless it’s urgent, reply the next day, not in the heat of the moment.
  • Answer only the question asked. Leave out the history and the digs.
  • Acknowledge before you disagree. “I can see bedtime matters to you” costs nothing and lowers the heat.
  • Make one clear ask: what, when, and by when you need a reply.
  • Talk about the child’s needs, not the other parent’s character.

Words you could use

“I can see why you’re worried about bedtime. At my house it’s 8:30. Can we both aim for that on school nights?”

“I don’t want to argue over text. Can we keep this to the dentist on Tuesday?”

Who decides what

Read moreShow less · about 2 min: Who decides what

Feeling shut out of decisions is one of the most painful parts of co-parenting, on either side. Most of it comes from never saying out loud which decisions are joint. Agree that once, in writing.

  • Name the joint decisions. Usually school, health and anything new and regular, like a club, a sport or a camp. Day-to-day things, like dinner and bedtime, belong to whoever has the kids that day.
  • Agree a reply-by window. One parent asks by message, the other answers within a set number of days. If there is no answer by then, the asking parent can go ahead.
  • Do the hard ones in writing, one topic at a time. Write it through builds a brief, friendly message with one clear ask and a reply-by date, checks the wording, and keeps each reply in order on your own phone. Nothing goes through this site.
  • Keep it to one written channel, so both of you can find what was agreed.
  • Check your parenting plan. Many plans already say who has a say in school and health. If yours doesn’t, or you can’t agree, write down what each of you thinks it should say.

Words you could use

“For school, health and new activities, can we agree to text each other first and reply within 3 days?”

“Before Sam is signed up for anything new, can you text me first so we can decide together?”

“Swimming lessons on Saturdays, starting March 2. Are you okay with that? Can you let me know by Friday?”

Bonding tips: closeness with your child in your home

Moving between two homes is a lot for a child. What helps them feel close to you is a calm, predictable home of your own, not a contest with the other house.

  • Give them time to land after a handoff. A snack, a quiet hour, their own space. Many kids are flat or grumpy for a while after a change. It isn’t about you.
  • Keep a welcome-back ritual. The same dinner on the first night, a walk, a game you always play. Routines make your place feel like home.
  • Give each child some one-on-one time, even fifteen minutes, doing something they choose.
  • Let them talk about the other home freely. Listen without questions or comments. A child who can love both homes out loud feels safer in each.
  • Never use your child as a messenger or a source. Don’t ask them to pass on messages, keep secrets or report back. Closeness can’t grow when a child feels they are working for one side.

Words you could use

“I’m so glad you’re here. Snack first, then cards, or do you just want to chill for a bit?”

Closeness with your child is offered, never demanded. A child who is quiet with you after a handoff isn’t choosing a side, and doesn’t need to prove they missed you.

If this may end up in court, or it’s more than conflict
  • Treat every message as a record. A judge may read it one day. Write as if they will.
  • Use BIFF: Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm. For example:

    “Got it. I’ll be at the school at 5 on Sunday.”

  • Don’t admit fault in writing. Instead of “Sorry, I always mess this up,” say what you will do next: “I’ll be there at 5.”
  • Keep copies of every message, with the date.

If the other parent is controlling, threatening or frightening, this stops being a joint process. Plan exchanges in a public place or through a third person, keep messages short and factual, and keep copies.

Holidays and special days

Holidays are one of the most common sore spots between separated parents. A simple rule that stays the same each year usually works better than arguing it out every time.

  • Check your court order or parenting plan first. It may already say who has which holiday.
  • Alternate the big ones. For example, Thanksgiving with one parent and Christmas with the other this year, then swap next year.
  • Or split the day. Christmas Eve to noon on Christmas Day with one parent, the rest of the day with the other.
  • Put it in writing once, with a reply-by date, and keep it the same each year so the kids can count on it.
  • Teens can say what matters to them, but the adults decide, so no child has to choose between their parents.
  • Tell the kids the plan only once you’ve both agreed it.

Words you could use

“To keep it fair: this year the kids are with you for Thanksgiving and with me for Christmas, and we swap next year. Can you confirm by November 15?”

When the other parent won’t cooperate

Read moreShow less · about 1 min: When the other parent won’t cooperate

Some co-parents can’t talk calmly, and trying harder doesn’t fix that. Parallel parenting means each parent runs their own home, with less direct contact and clear rules for what contact there is.

  • One written channel for logistics, such as email or a co-parenting app. Skip phone calls and texts at night.
  • Business tone. Many family professionals suggest replies that are Brief, Informative, Friendly and Firm. Answer the question asked and skip the rest.
  • Don’t reply to bait. You don’t owe an answer to an insult. A short “I’ll respond to the schedule question” is enough.
  • Keep your own home steady. Calm routines, kind words about the other parent, and your own support matter more than winning any exchange.
  • Look after yourself. A walk, a friend or a night off helps more than another round of messages.

A handoff-bag checklist

Most handoff arguments are about one forgotten thing. A short list, the same in both homes, stops that. Copy it, change it, and keep it by the door or in the bag.

  • Medicines and the inhaler, with a note of the last dose
  • The reading folder, homework and anything to sign
  • School or sports kit for the next few days
  • Glasses, chargers and the comfort toy
  • One line about anything new: sleep, health, or a test coming up
