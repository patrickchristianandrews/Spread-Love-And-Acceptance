Separated co-parents

After a separation, the goal is calm, brief, child-focused contact. You can’t control the other parent, only your side of the exchange. This page gives you scripts for a cooperative co-parent, and a different route for when cooperating isn’t possible.

Read this first

A court order or parenting plan always comes first. This page is not legal advice. For anything about custody, schedules or safety, ask a family-law professional or a local family service.

If the other parent is controlling, threatening or frightening, don’t treat this as a joint process. Plan exchanges in a public place or through a third person, keep your messages short and factual, and keep copies. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233 (or text START to 88788). Outside the US? See Not safe at home? for numbers. In danger now, call your local emergency number.

Four steps for calmer handoffs

  1. Keep it short and about the child Say what the other parent needs to know, and nothing else. Leave old arguments out of the doorway.

    “Hi. Homework’s done. She’s had dinner. Pick-up is Sunday at five.”

  2. Never use the kids as messengers Don’t ask a child to carry a message, report on the other home, or choose a side. Send messages to the adult.
  3. Give each task one owner Doctor visits, school forms, activities, pick-ups. Put the list in a shared calendar or a short note, so nobody has to ask twice. The One owner per job sheet helps, whether you fill it in together or on your own.
  4. Move the bigger things to a set time If something bigger comes up at a handoff, park it.

    “Can we put that on Thursday’s check-in?”

Who decides what

Feeling shut out of decisions is one of the most painful parts of co-parenting, on either side. Most of it comes from never saying out loud which decisions are joint. Agree that once, in writing.

  • Name the joint decisions. Usually school, health and anything new and regular, like a club, a sport or a camp. Day-to-day things, like dinner and bedtime, belong to whoever has the kids that day.
  • Agree a reply-by window. One parent asks by message, the other answers within a set number of days. If there is no answer by then, the asking parent can go ahead.
  • Keep it to one written channel, so both of you can find what was agreed.
  • Check your parenting plan. Many plans already say who has a say in school and health. If yours doesn’t, or you can’t agree, a mediator or a family-law professional can help you write it down.

Words you could use

“For school, health and new activities, can we agree to text each other first and reply within 3 days?”

“Before Sam is signed up for anything new, can you text me first so we can decide together?”

“Swimming lessons on Saturdays, starting March 2. Are you okay with that? Can you let me know by Friday?”

If this may end up in court

  • Treat every message as a record. A judge may read it one day. Write as if they will.
  • Use BIFF: Brief, Informative, Friendly, Firm. For example:

    “Got it. I’ll be at the school at 5 on Sunday.”

  • Don’t admit fault in writing. Instead of “Sorry, I always mess this up,” say what you will do next: “I’ll be there at 5.”
  • Keep copies of every message, with the date.

If the messages feel like more than conflict, see After a separation: when it’s more than conflict.

Holidays and special days

Holidays are one of the most common sore spots between separated parents. A simple rule that stays the same each year usually works better than arguing it out every time.

  • Check your court order or parenting plan first. It may already say who has which holiday.
  • Alternate the big ones. For example, Thanksgiving with one parent and Christmas with the other this year, then swap next year.
  • Or split the day. Christmas Eve to noon on Christmas Day with one parent, the rest of the day with the other.
  • Put it in writing once, with a reply-by date, and keep it the same each year so the kids can count on it.
  • Teens can say what matters to them, but the adults decide, so no child has to choose between their parents.
  • Tell the kids the plan only once you’ve both agreed it.

Words you could use

“To keep it fair: this year the kids are with you for Thanksgiving and with me for Christmas, and we swap next year. Can you confirm by November 15?”

When the other parent won’t cooperate

Some co-parents can’t talk calmly, and trying harder doesn’t fix that. Parallel parenting means each parent runs their own home, with less direct contact and clear rules for what contact there is.

  • One written channel for logistics, such as email or a co-parenting app. Skip phone calls and texts at night.
  • Business tone. Many family professionals suggest replies that are Brief, Informative, Friendly and Firm. Answer the question asked and skip the rest.
  • Don’t reply to bait. You don’t owe an answer to an insult. A short “I’ll respond to the schedule question” is enough.
  • Keep your own home steady. Calm routines, kind words about the other parent, and your own support matter more than winning any exchange.
  • Look after yourself. A counselor or a support group helps more than another round of messages.

Seeing your kids every other weekend, or less often than you’d like? It can feel like being a visitor in their lives. You are still their parent full time. Part-time with your kids has ideas for weekends, the Sunday goodbye and staying close in between.

A handoff-bag checklist

Most handoff arguments are about one forgotten thing. A short list, the same in both homes, stops that. Copy it, change it, and keep it by the door or in the bag.

  • Medicines and the inhaler, with a note of the last dose
  • The reading folder, homework and anything to sign
  • School or sports kit for the next few days
  • Glasses, chargers and the comfort toy
  • One line about anything new: sleep, health, or a test coming up

Words you could use

“Her inhaler wasn’t in the bag on Sunday. Can we both check the list before handoff?”
