Apology languages: saying sorry so it lands

After a hurt, people need different parts of a sorry. Gary Chapman and Jennifer Thomas described five “apology languages.” This guide treats them as a menu of parts of a good apology, so you can ask what would help, not guess.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice which part of a sorry settles you. Between you and others: offer the part that helps, and ask.

Tuesday, 9 p.m.

Jo forgot Sam’s appointment, again. Jo says “I’m so sorry!” three times. Sam still feels nothing has changed. What Sam wanted was to hear “I forgot, that was my job, and here is how I’ll make sure it doesn’t happen again.” Jo was apologising in one language. Sam needed another.

An honest word about the evidence

The five apology languages (Chapman and Thomas, 2006) are popular and have been tested very little. A better-researched finding is about what makes an apology work in general. In one set of studies (Lewicki and colleagues, 2016), the parts people rated most important were acknowledging responsibility and offering to repair, with expressing regret, explaining, promising to change and asking for forgiveness also helping. So use the five as a way to ask “which part do you need?” rather than as a type. More in What makes an apology work.

  1. Expressing regret: “I’m sorry.”

    Saying clearly that you feel bad about the hurt, and naming what it was.

    Healthy

    • “I’m sorry I snapped. That was unkind.”
    • Naming the specific thing.
    • Saying it without a “but.”

    When it goes sideways

    • “Sorry you feel that way.”
    • Repeating “sorry” instead of changing anything.
    • Saying it so the other person has to comfort you.

    Try: “I’m sorry I raised my voice. It wasn’t fair to you.”

  2. Accepting responsibility: “It was my fault.”

    Owning your part plainly, without excuses or shifting blame. Research finds this is one of the parts that matters most.

    Healthy

    • “I forgot. That was my job.”
    • Owning the effect, not just the intent.
    • Explaining only if asked, and briefly.

    When it goes sideways

    • “I only did it because you…”
    • A long explanation that becomes a defence.
    • Taking all the blame to end the talk.

    Try: “That was on me. I didn’t do what I said I would.”

    A caution: if you are in a legal dispute (for example, with an ex in family court), apologize in person, or follow your lawyer’s advice, rather than in writing. In messages, say what you’ll do next instead: “I’ll be there at 5 on Sunday.”

  3. Making it right: “What can I do to fix it?”

    Offering to repair what the hurt cost: time, a task, trust, something broken. Also among the parts researchers rate most important.

    Healthy

    • Doing the repair, and checking it fits what they need.
    • Offering a specific step, not a vague “I’ll make it up to you.”
    • Letting them say what would help.

    When it goes sideways

    • A gift in place of a changed behaviour.
    • Making a repair that suits you more than them.
    • Offering again and again so the hurt can’t be spoken.

    Try: “I’ll book it tonight and put it in both calendars. Does that help?”

  4. Genuinely changing: “I’ll try not to let it happen again.”

    A plan for the next time, small and real. This is where the setup matters: change the arrangement, not just the promise.

    Healthy

    • A specific change: an alarm, a swap, a pause rule.
    • Telling them what to look for.
    • Letting them check in without it being nagging.

    When it goes sideways

    • A big promise with no plan.
    • “I’ll never do it again” when you might.
    • A promise that depends on the other person reminding you.

    Try: “I’m putting an alarm on my phone for the school form. If I miss it, tell me and I’ll fix it that day.”

  5. Asking for forgiveness: “Can you forgive me?”

    Giving the other person the choice about when and whether. Some people need to be asked; others find it pressuring.

    Healthy

    • Asking, and accepting “not yet.”
    • Giving time and space.
    • Saying thank you if they do forgive.

    When it goes sideways

    • Demanding forgiveness to feel better.
    • Treating “not yet” as an insult.
    • Asking before the hurt has been heard.

    Try: “When you’re ready, I’d like to be forgiven. There’s no rush.”

  6. How to practice: ask before you need it.

    1. Ask on a calm day. “When I’ve upset you, what helps most: hearing sorry, hearing that I understand, or seeing me fix it?”
    2. Tell them yours. What would help you most after a hurt?
    3. Put the parts in order. Most good apologies include regret, responsibility and repair. Add the others as they fit.
    4. Check, don’t assume. After you apologise: “Did that cover it, or is there a part I missed?”
    5. Review. In your weekly review: “Is anything I did still sitting wrong?”
