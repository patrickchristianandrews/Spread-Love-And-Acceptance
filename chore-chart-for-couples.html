A fair chore chart for couples (free, printable)

A fair chore chart for couples lists every job at home, including the invisible ones like planning meals and remembering appointments. It gives each job one owner who handles it from start to finish, and you both look at it for ten minutes once a week. Fair doesn’t have to mean fifty-fifty. It means you both agree the split works. The chart below is free: type on it or print it.

How to use this chore chart in four steps

  1. List the jobs together. Start with the rows below, cross out what doesn’t fit, and add your own. Include the thinking jobs, not just the doing jobs.
  2. Pick one owner for each job. The owner notices when it needs doing, plans it and does it. No reminders needed. If you can’t agree, try one owner for a month and swap after. WP-03, One owner per job, has more help with this.
  3. Tick the days it got done. A quick tick is enough. This is not a scorecard. It just shows what is really happening, so you don’t have to argue from memory.
  4. Hold a ten-minute review each week. What went well? What slipped? Is one person carrying too much this week? Change one thing, then stop. Keep it short and kind.

What makes a chore split fair?

A fair chore split is one you both agree works, for this season of your lives. It is not always equal. If one of you works longer hours, has a long commute, or is caring for a parent, a fair split might look uneven on paper and still feel right to both of you.

Three questions help:

  • Is all the work on the chart? Most unfair splits hide in the thinking jobs. Our guide to invisible labor and the mental load has a list of the ones people forget.
  • Does every job have one owner? Shared jobs tend to slip, because each person waits for the other. One owner fixes that.
  • Could you both keep this up for a year? If one person is running on empty, the split isn’t working yet, even if the ticks look even.

A chore chart for roommates and families

This chart works the same way for roommates and families. For roommates, add rows for shared supplies, like paper towels and dish soap, and for the bills you split. For families, give kids small jobs they can fully own, like feeding the cat or setting the table. Owning one job, start to finish, teaches more than helping with many.

Questions people often ask

How do you split chores fairly as a couple?

List every job, including planning and remembering. Give each one a single owner. Then check together whether the total feels fair to both of you, given your work hours and energy. Adjust once a week until it does.

Should chores be split 50/50?

Not always. Equal and fair are different. A split that matches your real schedules and strengths can be fairer than an exact half. What matters is that you both agree, and that the thinking work is counted too.

What if my partner won’t use a chore chart?

Start with your own side. Write down one week of what you do and share it as information, not a complaint. Ask for one small step, like owning one job fully. Many people warm up once they see it isn’t a scorecard.

How often should we change the chart?

Look at it for ten minutes each week, and make bigger changes once a month or when life changes, like a new job, a new baby or a move.
