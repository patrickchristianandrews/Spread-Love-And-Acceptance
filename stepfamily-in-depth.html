  • Stepfamilies start with a different shape from first families. That shape, not anyone’s character, causes many of the early struggles.
  • Connection before correction. The parent leads on discipline; the stepparent builds trust.

It takes time: why stepfamilies start differently

In a first family, the couple usually comes first, and children arrive into it. In a stepfamily, it’s the other way round. A parent and child already share years of history, habits and jokes. A new adult arrives into that. Often there is another home, another parent, and sometimes a former partner who died.

What research suggests

The psychologist Patricia Papernow (2013) calls this “stepfamily architecture.” Drawing on research and years of clinical work, she describes five common challenges that come from this shape: insider and outsider positions, which can be intense and get stuck; children’s struggles with loss, loyalty binds and too much change; parenting tasks that pull the adults apart; building a new family culture; and a former partner who remains part of the family. Lawrence Ganong and Marilyn Coleman, in their research overview Stepfamily Relationships (Ganong and Coleman, 2017), bring together decades of studies on how stepfamilies form, change and get help.

A theme across this work is that closeness in a stepfamily builds slowly, often over years. Families who expect that tend to cope better than families who expect an instant bond and then blame themselves when it doesn’t come.

In everyday life

Lower the bar for “success” in the first years. A calm meal, a shared joke, a child who says “night” to the stepparent: these are real progress. Build small, predictable routines. Keep some of each family’s old ways, and slowly add a few new ones.

Words you could use

“Let’s not expect a family by Christmas. Let’s aim for a good Saturday.”

“It’s not that we’re failing. It’s that this takes a long time for everyone.”

The stepparent role: connection before correction

Many new stepparents feel they should step in as an equal parent straight away, especially if the house feels chaotic. It usually backfires.

What research suggests

Papernow sums up the stepparent’s early job as connection rather than correction. In her view, the parent should keep the lead on discipline until the stepparent and child have a trusting relationship, and harsh, rule-heavy stepparenting is very likely to hurt that relationship. Research on how stepparents build relationships points the same way. In one study, Ganong, Coleman and colleagues interviewed 53 stepparents, along with their partners and stepchildren. Stepparents who made friends with the children before moving in, and kept doing it afterwards, felt closer and reported less conflict. Those who stopped once they’d moved in had more conflict. Those who never tried were the most distant. Stepchildren also tended to see the first efforts as the stepparent’s job.

In everyday life

  • The parent leads: rules, consequences and the hard conversations.
  • The stepparent supports: keeps the house rules, backs the parent up in private, and stays calm in public.
  • The stepparent connects: shows interest, keeps promises, finds one small shared thing.
  • The stepparent can still say what’s okay in their own home, politely and as an adult, not as a parent: “Please don’t take my phone charger without asking.”
  • When the parent is away, the stepparent is the adult in charge, like a trusted babysitter: “Your mum said bed at nine.”
  • Over time, and only as trust grows, the stepparent can take on more of a parenting role. With teenagers, that may never become a full parent role, and that is fine.

For example (made up): Nia moved in with Tom and his daughter Ellie, 11. Nia was tidy; Tom and Ellie were not. Nia started reminding Ellie about her shoes, her plates, her coat. Ellie stopped speaking to her. Nia and Tom talked. Tom took over the reminders, and Nia asked Ellie to teach her the dance game Ellie loved. It was months before the shoes improved. But Ellie started saying goodnight.

Words you could use

To a stepchild: “I’m not trying to replace your dad. I’d just like us to get along.”

For the couple: “Can you take the lead on rules with your kids, and I’ll back you up?”

From the parent: “Jo isn’t in charge of rules. But Jo is an adult in this house, and we’re polite to each other.”

Stepparent in charge for an evening: “Your mum said screens off at eight. I’m just passing it on.”

Loyalty binds, loss and too much change

Children in stepfamilies are often coping with several things at once. They may have lost the family they knew, through separation or a death. They may have moved home or school. And they may feel that warming to a stepparent is a betrayal of their other parent.

What research suggests

Papernow (2013) names children’s struggles with loss, loyalty binds and too much change as one of the five core stepfamily challenges. Long-term studies led by Mavis Hetherington followed about 1,400 families; Hetherington and Kelly (2002) report that most children of divorce and remarriage turn out reasonably well, though outcomes vary widely from child to child. Amato (2001), pooling many studies, found that differences in wellbeing between children of divorced and continuously married parents were small on average. Both are reassuring, and both leave room for the things families can do to help.

In everyday life

  • Name the bind, and release them from it. “You don’t have to choose” needs saying more than once.
  • Never criticise the other parent where children can hear. Children often hear it as criticism of half of themselves.
  • Keep one-to-one time between each parent and their own children. It is one of the most protective things you can do.
  • Limit change where you can. If you can avoid a new home, new school and new siblings all in the same year, do.
  • If a parent has died, make room for their memory. A photo, their birthday, stories. A stepparent who welcomes this is easier to accept.

Words you could use

“You can love your mum and still like hanging out with me. You don’t have to pick.”

From a parent: “It’s okay to miss how things were. I miss some of it too.”

After a parent has died: “Your dad’s photo stays right where it is. Will you tell me about him sometime?”

The other household and the ex

A former partner is part of a stepfamily’s life, through the children, for years. The adults don’t need to be friends. Calm, predictable contact between homes is what helps children most.

In everyday life

  • The parents run parent-to-parent contact, especially at first. A stepparent stepping into those messages can make things tense fast.
  • Keep messages short, factual and about the children. A message that would be fine read aloud to a judge, or to the children when they’re grown, is usually a good message.
  • Handoffs: polite, brief, and not the moment for any discussion.
  • Children are never messengers or spies. Don’t ask them what happens at the other home.
  • A new partner at the other home is a stepparent too. Assume good intent unless you see otherwise.
  • If the other parent frightens, controls or threatens, co-operation isn’t the goal. Keep contact minimal and in writing, and see After a separation: when it’s more than conflict.

Words you could use

Parent to parent: “Mia has a school trip on the 12th. Can you sign the form at yours, or shall I?”

Stepparent to the other parent: “Hi, I’m Jo. They’ve been telling me all about the football. Nice to meet you.”

To a child who’s been asked to pass on a message: “Thanks for telling me. That’s a grown-up thing, so I’ll sort it out with your dad directly.”

Different rules in two homes

Different rules in two homes are normal. Children adapt to different rules at school, at grandparents’ houses and at friends’ homes. What’s hard is rules that change without warning, or adults who criticise each other’s rules.

In everyday life

  • As a couple, agree a few clear house rules, in private, before you announce them.
  • The parent explains them, calmly, once. The stepparent is there and agrees.
  • Write them down. It takes the heat out of “you never said.”
  • Talk to the other home about the few things that really need to match: medicine, bedtimes for little ones, and big decisions about school or health.
  • Handoff days are hard. Children often come back tired, cross or quiet. Keep the first hour easy.

Words you could use

“At Dad’s you can stay up later. Here, it’s lights out at nine. Both are okay.”

To the other parent: “Could we keep bedtime at about the same time on school nights? It would help Leo a lot.”

Step-siblings and half-siblings

Children who didn’t choose each other are suddenly sharing a home, a bathroom and their parents’ attention. Some become close. Many get along politely. Both are fine.

  • Space that stays theirs. A child who lives with you part of the time still needs a bed, a drawer or a shelf that is theirs, untouched while they’re away.
  • Bedrooms: if children must share, talk about it with them first, and give each a private corner.
  • Same rules for fights, whoever’s child started it. Children watch closely for favouritism.
  • A new baby belongs to both of you, which can make older children feel even more like outsiders. Keep one-to-one time with each, and don’t make them the default babysitter.
  • Different ages and stages may mean different privileges. Explain why: “Leo is older, so his bedtime is later.”

Words you could use

“You two don’t have to be best friends. You do have to be kind.”

“The baby needs a lot of me right now. You still matter just as much. Saturday morning is ours.”

“This drawer is yours. Nobody touches it while you’re at your mum’s.”

Money and fairness between the kids

Money in a stepfamily carries feelings: about the old family, about fairness, and about the future. It helps to talk about it before it becomes a fight.

  • Child support and costs. How do money paid to or from the other home, and each child’s costs, fit into your household budget? Decide together, and stick to facts.
  • Everyday fairness. Pocket money, presents and treats: roughly equal for children who live with you, even part-time, adjusted for age.
  • Different means at the two homes. One home may be able to give more. Don’t compete, and don’t criticise.
  • Accounts. Many stepcouples keep some money separate and some shared. Whatever you choose, agree it openly. See Money together.
  • Wills and property. Without a clear will, laws in many places may not provide for stepchildren, or may leave a partner in a hard position. Talk it through as a couple and get proper legal advice.

Words you could use

“Can we agree pocket money and birthday budgets for all the kids, so nobody feels left out?”

“I want to make sure both your kids and mine are looked after if something happens to us. Can we see someone about our wills this year?”

The Lemonade Stand can split shared bills by income, and give a child who lives with you part of the time a smaller share of the jobs.

Holidays and special days

Holidays bring out old traditions, big expectations and the logistics of two or more homes.

  • Plan early, between the adults. Children should never have to negotiate where they go.
  • Alternate, split, or celebrate twice. A second Christmas, Diwali or Eid on another day is still the real thing to a child.
  • Blend traditions slowly. Ask each child which tradition matters most to them, and keep it.
  • Make one new tradition that belongs only to this family.
  • Birthdays and school events: agree who goes, and keep it calm in shared spaces.

Words you could use

“You’re with your dad on Christmas Day this year, so we’ll have our Christmas on the 27th. Same presents, same pancakes.”

“What’s the one thing about holidays you never want to change?”

Adult stepchildren and later-life stepfamilies

When parents re-partner later in life, the stepchildren are often adults with their own homes and children. The relationships are different, and so are the worries.

  • Common worries: that a parent who died will be forgotten, that a parent will be taken advantage of, that inheritance will change, or that family traditions will be lost.
  • Each partner leads with their own children, especially on money, health and big news.
  • Aim for friendly, respectful adults, not a parent–child bond.
  • Be open about plans: wills, the family home, care if one of you becomes ill. Get legal advice.
  • Grandchildren: let the adult children set the pace for how involved a step-grandparent becomes.

For example (made up): When Ruth, 68, married George, his daughter Clare was cool towards her for a year. Ruth didn’t push. George talked with Clare about her mother, and showed her that the family home was still left to her. At Clare’s son’s birthday, Ruth brought a present and left early. The next year, Clare asked her to stay for cake.

Words you could use

From a parent: “Marrying Pat doesn’t change what I’ve planned for you. I’ll show you, if you’d like.”

From a stepparent: “I’m not trying to be your mum. I’d like us to be friendly, and I know she matters to you.”

From an adult stepchild: “I’m still getting used to this. It’s not about you. Can we take it slowly?”

Step-grandparents

A step-grandchild may already have two, four or more grandparents. A step-grandparent doesn’t need to compete for a place. Being another warm, steady adult is a gift.

  • Follow the parents’ rules, and ask what they’d like you to be called.
  • Treat step- and biological grandchildren alike when they’re together: presents, attention, photos on the fridge.
  • Start small. A card on their birthday, a game at family gatherings.

Words you could use

“You can call me Bev. I’ve got a biscuit tin with your name on it.”

To the parents: “I’d love to be part of their lives in whatever way suits you. What would help?”

Insiders and outsiders: a stepparent who feels outside

What research suggests

Papernow (2013) describes insider and outsider positions as built into stepfamily structure. The parent is an insider with their children and with their partner. The stepparent is often an outsider to the parent–child bond. The children may feel outside the new couple. The parent may feel torn in two. These positions can become intense and get stuck, but they are about where people stand, not who they are.

In everyday life

  • Name it together, without blame. “You feel left out; I feel pulled in two” is a shared problem, not a fight.
  • Protect couple time, even an hour a week.
  • Protect parent–child time too. It helps the children, and, over time, the stepparent.
  • Give the stepparent space and a voice: somewhere in the home that is theirs, and a say in how it runs.
  • The stepparent finds support outside the family, too: friends, other stepparents, a group.
  • Check in regularly, at a calm time, not after a bad evening. The family plan card gives the talk a shape.

Words you could use

Stepparent: “I love you, and I love that you’re close to your kids. Sometimes I feel like a lodger. Can we talk about one small thing that would help?”

Parent: “I hear you. I’m not choosing them over you. Let’s find one evening that’s just ours.”

Parent: “I feel stuck in the middle. I need you both, and I need us to work on this together.”

Every kind of stepfamily

Stepfamilies include couples who marry and couples who don’t, same-sex couples, a partner with no children of their own, and families where both adults bring children. The core ideas on this page apply to all of them.

Things some families face

  • Recognition. Schools, doctors, family members and laws may not recognise an unmarried or same-sex stepparent. Agree what the stepparent can do, and put it in writing where you can: pickup lists, emergency contacts, consent forms. Check what your local rules allow.
  • Family acceptance. Some relatives may struggle with a same-sex stepfamily. The parent leads with their own family. Children need to hear that their family is a real family.
  • A stepparent with no children of their own is learning parenting and stepparenting at once. Be generous with each other.
  • A bonus family that doesn’t live together. Some couples keep two homes for years. The same ideas apply, at a gentler pace.

Words you could use

To a school: “This is Alex, my partner. Alex is on the pickup list and can be contacted in an emergency.”

To a child: “Some families have a mum and a dad, some have two mums, some have a stepdad. Ours has all of us.”

When it’s going wrong, and getting help

Hard years are common in stepfamilies, and they don’t mean the family has failed. But some signs suggest it needs more than time:

  • The couple argues about the children most weeks, and it isn’t getting better.
  • A child seems very unhappy, angry or withdrawn for months, or their schoolwork or friendships drop off sharply.
  • A stepparent and child can’t be in a room together.
  • One adult has started to dread coming home.

What helps

  • Go back to the basics: the parent leads, the stepparent connects, small regular time together, and one-to-one time for parents and their own children.
  • Talk at a calm time. Check-ins give a hard talk a kind shape.
  • See someone who knows stepfamilies. Advice made for first families can make things worse, for example by pushing a stepparent to discipline. Ask any counsellor directly about their stepfamily experience. Help beyond this site explains how to find one.
  • For a child who is struggling, talk with their other parent, their school, and their doctor.

Words you could use

“We keep having the same fight about the kids. I don’t think either of us is wrong. Could we see someone who knows stepfamilies?”

To a child: “Things have been hard at home. That isn’t your fault. We’re getting some help to make it better.”

If anyone in the home is frightened, hurt or controlled, safety comes first. See Not safe at home?

Exercises

1. Who leads on what

As a couple, list the everyday parenting jobs: bedtime, homework, screens, consequences, school contact. Mark who leads on each for now. Look again in six months.

2. One small thing

Stepparent: choose one small, regular thing to share with each stepchild that has no rules attached. Keep it going for a month.

3. Our three house rules

Agree three house rules in private. The parent explains them. Write them in House rules for us.

4. Insider, outsider

Each adult finishes the sentence “In this family, I sometimes feel…” and “What would help me is…”. Swap. Listen without fixing.

5. The holiday map

List the year’s holidays and birthdays. For each, note which home, which traditions to keep, and one new one to try.

Questions people often ask

Should a stepparent ever discipline?

At first, the parent leads and the stepparent backs them up. When the parent is out, the stepparent passes on the parent’s rules, like a trusted babysitter. As trust grows, often over years, the stepparent can take on more.

How long does it take for a stepfamily to feel settled?

Usually years, not months, and it varies a lot. Families who expect it to be slow tend to cope better than families who expect an instant bond.

My partner’s ex is hostile. What should I do as the stepparent?

Stay out of parent-to-parent messages, be polite at handoffs, and never criticise the other parent in front of the children. If the ex threatens or frightens anyone, see the safety page and keep contact in writing.

Is it unfair to treat my own children and stepchildren differently?

Fair doesn’t always mean the same, because ages and needs differ. But presents, rules and attention in your home should be roughly even, and you should be able to explain any difference.

My stepchild says “You’re not my dad.” How do I answer?

Calmly, and agree: “That’s true. I’m not trying to be. I’m an adult who lives here and cares about you.” Then leave the rule to their parent.

We’re a same-sex couple. Does any of this apply to us?

Yes. The structure of a stepfamily is the same. You may also need to make the stepparent’s role clear to schools, doctors and family, in writing where you can.

The limits of this page

This page gives general guidance. It isn’t therapy, and it isn’t legal or financial advice: rules on stepparent rights, child support and inheritance differ a lot between countries and states. Much of the research it draws on comes from a few countries and from married, mostly heterosexual stepfamilies, so treat it as a guide to common patterns. If anyone in the home is unsafe, go straight to Not safe at home?.
