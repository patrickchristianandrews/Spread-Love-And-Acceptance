One owner per job Also called the RACI Treaty Ownership · Changes · Agreeing on It

A living agreement that gives every regular household job one owner, and, if you like, a helper.

This worksheet puts Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person to work, with a nod to Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. In you: a job that keeps slipping is a setup problem, not a character flaw. Between you and others: every job gets one clear owner, so unclaimed work stops drifting to one person.

The Treaty Changing the Treaty Agreeing on It Does every job have an owner?

Purpose: a living agreement that gives every regular household job one owner, so you stop re-deciding who owns what every week. (The technical name comes from RACI, a planning term from the workplace: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed. Here the owner is the Responsible name, and the optional helper stands in for Accountable.)

Owner: the one person who does the job and sees it through.
Helper (optional): someone who pitches in, covers, or notices if it slips.

Part A

The Treaty

Before you start, run WP-02. This is the longest session in the suite: 45 to 60 minutes of working out who owns what. Don't try it at the end of a hard day. If either of you has a high load score, pick another time.

The list below is a starting point, not the whole list. Add every regular task your household actually has before you assign anything. The score at the bottom is only as honest as the list behind it, and the tasks most worth writing down are the ones nobody has ever said out loud.

Why the second block is there. The Preface opens on someone noticing the milk is low, and someone remembering a dentist appointment three weeks out. That kind of work is the whole reason this program exists. It is also the easiest to leave off a chore list, because it has no start time, no finish, and nothing to point to when it's done.

An invisible-column row often has an owner and no helper, and that's fine. Naming it is the point. A household where one person holds every row in that block has found something a tidy kitchen was hiding.

Part B

Changing the Treaty

This treaty isn't set in stone. It's meant to be reworked when life changes (a new job, a health issue, a new baby), not quietly shifted when one person just starts doing more. To change it:

  1. Either of you can ask to revisit it at the weekly closing, your short end-of-week review (Chapter V).
  2. Write each change into the table above. Don't just agree out loud.
  3. Log it below, with the date and who agreed to it.

A treaty with no history of changes is almost always a treaty nobody is using. The log also answers the question WP-04 asks each month: did last month's fix actually hold? You need a date to answer that.

On removals. A task only comes off this treaty through a WP-04 Part B decision. That means it was sorted as a "standard in question," you talked it through, and you both agreed the household doesn't need it. Log it as Removed, with the date.

Quietly deleting a row because nobody wants it is not a removal. It's the same unowned task, now unwritten.

Feeds Into

Does every job have an owner?

How many jobs have an owner? Count the jobs with an owner written in, then divide by the total number of jobs listed. For example, 8 of 10 jobs is 0.80 out of 1.00. This is the ownership clarity score, the ownership number in CALC-01 (Chapter II).

Don't raise the score by shortening the list. Deleting the three tasks nobody owns makes the math look better and changes nothing at home. If a task is real and has no owner, keep it here as a row with no owner. Bringing that row to light is the whole point of this score.

The math also works the other way, so expect it: adding tasks you'd never written down before will lower the score, because they arrive as new rows with no owner yet. A month where the number drops because five invisible tasks finally got named is a better month than one where it held steady on an incomplete list.

What this score cannot see. A list where one person owns all twenty rows scores a perfect 1.00. Ownership is completely clear, but the arrangement may still be badly unfair.

Clarity and fairness are measured separately, and this score only measures clarity. WP-01 measures how the work is split, and the two are meant to be read together. A 1.00 here next to an 80/20 split there doesn't mean anything is solved. It means the household has written down exactly how lopsided things are. That is a real and useful first step, but not the last one.
