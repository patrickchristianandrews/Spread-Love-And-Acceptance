Wired Differently: neurodiversity and communication

Simple versionRead the full guide

The same words can land very differently, depending on how each person is wired. This guide helps you speak so the message you meant is the message that arrives.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, part of the Five Pillars. In you: know your own wiring, pace and how you hear things. Between you and others: translate across different wiring and tone. A mismatch is a tuning problem, not a moral failing.

A text, read twice

“Fine.” One of you meant it literally: that is fine, go ahead. The other saw the period and heard a door closing. Same four letters, two entirely different messages.

  1. A mix-up usually runs both ways.

    When two differently wired people misread each other, it is not one person’s fault. Each of you is decoding with a receiver built for your own kind of signal. Compare notes instead of looking for who is to blame.

    Dig deeper: the double empathy problem

  2. For some people, too much gets through.

    The full guide walks through 14 wirings, one by one. Highly sensitive people, for example, pick up tone and mood others miss, so a slightly clipped “Sure.” can sting for hours. A soft start, a quiet room and one topic help.

    Dig deeper: the highly sensitive profile

  3. Some people feel plenty but can’t find the word.

    For them, “How does that make you feel?” can feel like a quiz with no right answer. Ask about the body or actions instead: “More like tired, or more like annoyed? Space or company?”

    Dig deeper: the alexithymia profile

  4. The relationship changes how words land.

    The same sentence can be a loose plan between friends and a broken agreement between partners. A hint between friends risks a little awkwardness. Between co-parents, it can feel like someone is keeping notes.

    Dig deeper: seven kinds of relationship

  5. The setting matters, and so do both people’s needs.

    A calm kitchen can hold a hard talk. A group chat or a 1 a.m. text cannot. If one of you needs closeness and the other needs space, say you are not leaving, name a clock time, and come back.

    Dig deeper: situation, room and caring for both needs

  6. Tell each other how you’re built, before a hard talk.

    A Wiring Card says how you take words in, what your silence means, and how a request should be phrased. Swap cards on an ordinary day, not in the middle of an argument.

    Dig deeper: make a wiring card

  7. If you’re the one with ADHD, these are for you.

    Not about you: for you. Small tools that make everyday life easier for both of you.

    • If you zone out mid-conversation, say so. “Sorry, I lost the last part. Can you say it again?” is a fine thing to say. Asking for a repeat is good listening, and there is nothing to be ashamed of.
    • Work with time blindness, not against it. Use a timer for tasks and for leaving the house. When you agree on a plan, put it in the calendar right then, while you are still talking.
    • Set your own reminders. Put them in your own phone, instead of relying on your partner to remember for you. Then nobody has to be the reminder.
    • Agree on a signal for hyperfocus. Pick a word, a tap on the shoulder or a timer that means “come up for air.” Agree on it on a calm day, so it feels like help, not an interruption.
    • Step out of the parent-and-child pattern. When one of you keeps reminding and the other keeps forgetting, it starts to feel like a parent and a child. Give each job one owner, keep the list in writing, and check in once a week instead of reminding every day.

  8. If you’re the autistic one, these are for you.

    Not about you: for you. Small things to adjust on your side, just as your partner adjusts on theirs.

    • Say it before you hit the wall. “I’ve got about an hour of people left in me” is easier to work with than a sudden shutdown.
    • Name your silence. “I’m processing, not upset. Give me till after dinner.” Without a few words, quiet can read as anger.
    • Give a return time when you need recovery. “I need an hour alone, then I’m back.”
    • Check the meaning before you react. “Did you mean that literally?” Your partner may have meant something softer.
    • Say how you show care. “Fixing your bike was me saying I love you” helps a partner who listens for words.
    More for you in the neurodivergent relationships guide

  9. If your wiring is different from your partner’s, these are for you.

    That includes being the one who isn’t neurodivergent. The adjusting runs both ways, so you have a list too.

    • Say the request, not the hint. “Could you take the bins out by eight?” instead of “The bins are full.”
    • Put plans where you can both see them. A plan that only lives in your head isn’t shared yet.
    • Give a heads-up before changes, even ten minutes ahead.
    • Check before you read meaning into tone or silence. “Are you thinking, or are you upset?”
    • Stop being the reminder, by agreement. Hand jobs over whole, then let the owner own them, even if they do it differently.
    • Name your own wiring as a need. “I settle faster when we talk soon. Could we set a time tonight?”
