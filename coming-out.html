Coming out as an adult

You might be 25, 40 or 65. You might be telling your parents, your grown children, your coworkers or your faith community. You might be gay, lesbian, bi, pan, trans, nonbinary, asexual, or still finding the words. Coming out as an adult is common, and it is yours to do in your own way and your own time.

In short

  • You choose when, who and how. There’s no deadline.
  • If your family might cut you off, sort out safety and money first.
  • Tell someone likely to be kind first.
  • Short, warm words work best. There are some below.
  • A partner can support you, but the timing is still yours.
  • Free, confidential support in the US: the LGBT National Help Center, 1-888-843-4564.

Gay, lesbian, bi or trans couples: everything here works for you. The tools on this site use your names, not “he” and “she,” and nothing assumes a man and a woman. Start in 10 minutes or share the load like any couple.

You choose when, who and how

  • It’s your information. You can tell one person, everyone, or nobody yet.
  • It isn’t one big moment. Most people come out many times, to different people.
  • Start with someone likely to be kind: a friend, a sibling, a cousin. One person on your side makes the next talk easier.
  • Pick the way that suits you: in person, a call, or a letter. A letter lets them have their first reaction in private, and lets you say it all without being interrupted.
  • Pick the moment: not a holiday dinner, not in a car you can’t get out of, not when anyone has been drinking.

Safety and money first

Many families come round in time, even when the first reaction is hard. But if you rely on your family for a home, money, a job or a visa, or you think they might cut you off or hurt you, plan first.

  • Your own money: a bank account and a phone in your own name, and some savings if you can.
  • Somewhere to go if a visit goes badly: a friend’s sofa, a way home.
  • Your documents (ID, passport, visa papers) somewhere safe that you control.
  • Your devices: whoever runs the Wi-Fi can usually see which sites you visit, though not what you type or read. Mobile data or a private window helps. Clearing what this site keeps shows the rest.

If you are afraid of violence, or of being forced into anything, including a marriage, see Not safe at home? In danger now, call your local emergency number.

Telling your parents or family

  • Lead with love, and why you’re telling them.
  • Say it plainly, once. You don’t have to explain or justify who you are.
  • Give them time. First reactions are often not final ones. Silence, tears or “Are you sure?” can come from shock, not rejection.
  • Say what you’d like from them. Often it’s just “keep loving me.”
  • Leave when you planned to. You don’t have to stay for the whole reaction.

Words you could use

Telling a parent

“I’m telling you because I love you and I want you in my life. I’m bisexual, and Dani is my partner. We’ve been together two years.”

“This isn’t easy to say, and I’ve thought about it for a long time. I’m gay. I’m still the same person you’ve always known.”

Asking for what you need

“You don’t have to understand it all today. I’d just like you to keep loving me, and to meet Dani when you’re ready.”

Setting a boundary

“I want you in my life, and I need you to treat Dani with respect. If that can’t happen today, I’ll head home, and I’ll call you next week.”

That is a caring boundary, not a threat. You are saying what you will do, not ordering them to change.

A question you don’t want to answer

“That’s a fair question, but I’m not ready to talk about that part yet.”

What if they cut me off?

It’s one of the most common fears, and for some people it happens. If it does:

  • Stay safe first: a place to stay, your own money, people you can call.
  • Leave the door open if you want to, without begging. Many families come back over months or years.
  • Plan the holidays early with friends or chosen family. They are often the hardest days.
  • Get support for yourself: a counsellor who works with LGBTQ+ people, a local LGBTQ+ centre, or a support group. Grieving a family that steps away is normal, even while they’re alive.
  • You didn’t cause this by being honest.

“I know this is hard for you. I’m not going anywhere. When you’re ready to talk, I’m here.”

At work

  • You don’t have to come out at work. If you want to, you can start with one coworker you trust.
  • Keep it ordinary, the way others mention their partners: “My wife and I went hiking at the weekend.”
  • Check your employer’s equality policy, and whether there is an LGBTQ+ staff network.
  • If you’re treated badly because of who you are, write down what happened and when, and talk to HR, a union or an employment adviser. In many places it is against the law.

When a partner wants you to come out sooner

It’s hard when your partner is out and you aren’t yet. They may feel hidden. You may feel pushed. Both feelings are fair, and neither of you is the bad guy.

  • Talk about what each of you needs, not about who is braver.
  • Agree small steps: who knows now, who’s next, roughly when.
  • Agree what you’ll each say if someone asks, so nobody is caught off guard.
  • It is still your decision. A partner who threatens to out you, or uses it to control you, is crossing a line. See Not safe at home?

“I want to be out with you. I’m scared about my parents, and I need to do it in my own time. Can we agree a first step together, like telling my sister by the summer?”

For the partner who’s out: “Sometimes I feel hidden, and it hurts. I’m not trying to rush you. Can we make a plan together?”

Bi, pan, trans or nonbinary later in life

Many people come out in their thirties, forties, sixties or later, sometimes after a long marriage or after having children. It doesn’t make your past a lie.

  • If you’re married or in a long relationship, your partner may need time and support too. A counsellor can help you both.
  • Telling your children: keep it simple and right for their age, and give older kids time too. “I’m still your dad. I’m also bi. That means I can love people of more than one gender.”
  • Being bi in a relationship with someone of a different gender is still being bi. You don’t have to prove anything.

Where to get support

  • US: LGBT National Help Center, 1-888-843-4564. Free and confidential.
  • Under 25: The Trevor Project, 1-866-488-7386, or text START to 678-678, any time.
  • Trans and questioning: Trans Lifeline, 877-565-8860.
  • UK: Switchboard LGBT+ helpline, 0800 0119 100.
  • Thinking about ending your life: call or text 988 (US), or find your local line at findahelpline.com.

Questions people often ask

Is it too late to come out as an adult?

No. Many people come out in their thirties, forties or later. You choose when, who and how, and there is no deadline.

What if my family cuts me off?

Plan your safety and money first, so you have a place to stay and your own account. If it happens, leave the door open if you want to, plan the holidays with friends, and get support for yourself. Many families come back in time.

Is there a support line for LGBTQ+ adults?

In the US, the LGBT National Help Center is free and confidential on 1-888-843-4564. In the UK, Switchboard is on 0800 0119 100. The Trevor Project is for people under 25.
