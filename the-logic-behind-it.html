Some reactions look odd, rude or over the top from the outside. Most of them have a logic behind them that makes sense once you can see it. This page lays that logic out, one step at a time, so the reaction can be understood by other people, and so the other person gets a fair way to respond too.

It works in both directions. Neurodivergent logic is explained for people who don’t share it, and everyday social habits like small talk, “I’m fine” and hints are explained in plain logic for anyone who finds them baffling. Plenty of reactions belong to everyone: snapping when tired, going quiet in a row, or driving the way you learned at home.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, part of the Five Pillars. In you: find the reasons behind your own reactions, and say them kindly. Between you and others: see the reasons behind theirs. Wiring is information, not a flaw, and a mismatch is a tuning problem, not a moral failing.

  1. What it looks like from outside. One line, in camera words: what a camera would see or hear. No “rude”, no “weird”.
  2. The logic chain. Three or four short steps, each following from the one before. For example: Energy is a budget → the party spent it faster than expected → staying past zero risks a shutdown → leaving quietly protects the evening and the friendship.
  3. An analogy, or two. Something familiar that works the same way inside, such as a phone at 3% switching off everything but the call.
  4. Why the reaction fits. What makes it reasonable, said fairly.
  5. The other side. What the other person may have felt or needed. Care is not control, and nobody has to be the bad guy.
  6. One sentence to explain it, and one small step that helps both people.

Logic explains a reaction. It doesn’t excuse harm. If a reaction hurts someone, the kind and logical next move is to say so, repair it, and find a better step for next time. Each situation below says so where it matters.

Three examples, written out in full

A tip the founder of this site often shares: when another driver tailgates you, or drives in a way that feels aggressive, remember that driving habits are learned locally. What counts as a normal gap, a fair merge or a friendly horn differs a lot between places. Taking it personally turns a different rulebook into an insult, and ruins your drive. If a driver is truly dangerous, keep your distance and let them go. Don’t engage.

Something anyone can do · Different rulebooks

A driver who tailgates or feels aggressive

1What it looks like from outside

A car sits close behind you, merges tightly, or uses the horn more than you would.

2The logic behind it

  1. Driving habits are learned locally
  2. Normal following distance, merging and horn use differ a lot between places
  3. Someone from out of town may simply be driving the way everyone does where they learned
  4. It isn’t aimed at you: it’s a different rulebook, not an insult

3It works like this

  • An accent: it’s how they learned to speak the road.
  • A different dialect for the same instruction: in one town a horn means “hello, I’m here”, in another it means “get out of my way”.
  • Someone who stands closer when they talk because that’s normal where they grew up.

4Why the reaction fits

What counts as a safe gap, a polite merge or a friendly toot really does vary from place to place, and people drive by the rules they learned without thinking. Taking it personally turns a different rulebook into an insult, and ruins your drive.

5The other side

They may not know they feel close to you. On their roads, this may be ordinary, careful driving.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“They’re probably driving the way people do where they learned. It isn’t about me.”

One small step: Give space, let them pass, and stay safe. Never retaliate.

With care: If a driver is truly dangerous, keep your distance and let them go. Don’t engage.

The second example is one many people do, and many autistic people know well: leaving a party early.

Something anyone can do · Sensory and energy

Leaving a party early, without a long goodbye

1What it looks like from outside

Partway through the party, someone slips out with a quick wave, or with no goodbye at all.

2The logic behind it

  1. Energy is a budget
  2. The party spent it faster than expected
  3. Staying past zero risks a shutdown or a sharp word
  4. Leaving quietly protects the evening and the friendship

3It works like this

  • A phone at 3% switching off everything but the call.
  • A car pulling into the services before the fuel light turns red, not after.
  • Leaving a swimming pool when your legs start to tire, while you can still climb out easily.

4Why the reaction fits

A long goodbye costs energy too: a round of hugs and small talk at the door. For people who find social time draining, including many autistic people, the quiet exit is the version that still leaves them able to get home safely.

5The other side

The host may feel unappreciated or worry something went wrong. Friends may not know whether the person was upset.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I had a good time. When my battery is gone I need to go quickly, before it runs out completely.”

One small step: Send a short text after: “Thanks for tonight, I had to go when my battery ran out.” Hosts can say up front: “Slip out whenever you need to.”

The third runs the other way: a habit many people find natural, explained for anyone who finds it baffling.

A common social habit, explained · Social rules

Small talk about the weather

1What it looks like from outside

Two people who barely know each other spend five minutes on the weather and the traffic.

2The logic behind it

  1. Strangers need a safe way to check each other out
  2. The weather is a topic with no risk in it
  3. Swapping easy lines shows “I’m friendly, you can relax”
  4. Once both feel safe, real talk can start

3It works like this

  • Musicians tuning up before the concert: no tune yet, but it makes the tune possible.
  • Dogs sniffing hello before they play.
  • A handshake made of words.

4Why the reaction fits

Small talk isn’t really about information. It’s a way to show goodwill and build a little trust. Studies suggest brief, friendly chats, even with people we hardly know, are linked with feeling more connected and with better days.

5The other side

For people who find it pointless or tiring, including many autistic people, it costs energy and gives no new information. They may wonder why everyone pretends to care about rain.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Small talk is how I say hello and check we’re friendly. It doesn’t have to be about the weather for long.”

One small step: Have two easy lines ready and one real question (“What’s keeping you busy at the moment?”). It’s fine to steer to a topic you care about after a minute.

Plans and change (8)

Last-minute changes, surprises, lateness, and what “sometime” means.

Getting upset when a plan changes at the last minuteNeurodivergent logic, explained

1What it looks like from outside

The plan changes an hour before, and the person goes quiet, tense or upset, even though the new plan sounds fine.

2The logic behind it

  1. A plan is a map the mind has already walked through
  2. A late change means redrawing the map with no warning
  3. Redrawing uses the energy that was meant for the day itself
  4. The upset is the cost of the redraw, not a verdict on the new plan

3It works like this

  • A sat nav recalculating while you’re already on the motorway: for a moment it has no route at all.
  • A chef told mid-service that the menu has changed: every dish already prepped is now the wrong dish.
  • Someone moving the furniture in a room you walk through in the dark.

4Why the reaction fits

For many autistic people, and for many anxious people, not knowing what comes next is costly in itself. A plan lowers that cost. Losing it at short notice is a real loss of something that was holding the day together.

5The other side

The other person may have seen the change as small, or even as a nice surprise. They may feel their good intention was missed, or worry they can never change anything.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I’m not against the new plan. I just need a few minutes to catch up with the change.”

One small step: Give changes as early as you can, with the reason. The person who needs time says “Give me ten minutes and I’ll be on board,” and then comes back on board.

Asking lots of questions before saying yes to a planSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

Someone is invited out and asks who is going, how long it lasts, where to park and what to wear before giving an answer.

2The logic behind it

  1. A plan with gaps is a plan with surprises in it
  2. Questions fill the gaps before the day
  3. Filled gaps mean less to manage on the day
  4. The questions are a way of saying yes properly

3It works like this

  • A pilot running the pre-flight checklist: nobody thinks the pilot doesn’t want to fly.
  • Reading the whole recipe before you start cooking, so you don’t find out halfway through that the oven needed heating.
  • Checking the weather and the route before a long walk.

4Why the reaction fits

Knowing the details lets a person decide honestly and turn up ready. For someone who finds the unknown tiring, the questions do the tiring part early, when there is time to handle it.

5The other side

The person asking may feel cross-examined, or hear the questions as doubt about their plan or as reluctance to come.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I’m asking because I want to come, and I want to get it right.”

One small step: Send the key details in one message: time, place, who, roughly how long, and how to leave early. The person asking then gives a clear answer by an agreed time.

Not enjoying a surprise, even a kind oneSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A surprise party or treat is revealed, and the person looks stunned or flat instead of delighted.

2The logic behind it

  1. A surprise takes away the chance to get ready
  2. A big moment with no warning has to be handled on the spot
  3. Some bodies read unplanned big moments as alarm before joy
  4. The flat face is the alarm settling, not a lack of thanks

3It works like this

  • Being pushed on stage without a script, in front of people you love.
  • A pop quiz instead of a test you studied for: same questions, much harder.
  • Being handed a heavy box with no warning: you might love what’s inside, but first you have to stop it falling.

4Why the reaction fits

Delight usually needs a moment of safety first. People who like time to prepare, including many autistic and many anxious people, can feel the shock long before the kindness reaches them. The thanks often arrives later.

5The other side

The planner put thought, time and love into it. A flat reaction can feel like the gift was rejected, and they may feel embarrassed in front of others.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I love that you thought of me. I enjoy things more when I know they’re coming.”

One small step: Swap a full surprise for “a surprise with a date”: tell them the day and the dress code, and keep the rest secret. Say thank you later, when it has sunk in.

Leaving exactly at the agreed timeNeurodivergent logic, explained

1What it looks like from outside

At 9 o’clock, as agreed, the person stands up and leaves, even though everyone else is staying and having fun.

2The logic behind it

  1. The finish time was part of what they agreed to
  2. Their energy was planned to last until that time
  3. Staying later spends energy that was set aside for tomorrow
  4. Leaving on time keeps the deal they made with themselves and with you

3It works like this

  • A parking meter: when the time is up, it’s up, however nice the street is.
  • A shop that closes at six. It isn’t rude to its customers; it’s open when it said it would be.
  • Packing exactly enough food for a day’s walk: going on for another hour means walking hungry.

4Why the reaction fits

Agreeing a time and keeping it is honest and reliable. For many people who budget their energy carefully, an end time is what made it possible to say yes in the first place.

5The other side

The host may wonder whether the person was enjoying themselves, or feel the evening was cut short. Friends may feel a little left behind.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I had a good time. Leaving at nine is what lets me say yes next time too.”

One small step: Say your leaving time when you arrive, so it isn’t a surprise. Hosts can make it easy: “Nine is great. Thanks for coming.”

Running late again, despite really caringSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

Someone who clearly cares about you arrives late, again, with an apology.

2The logic behind it

  1. For some minds time has two settings: “now” and “not now”
  2. “Not now” turns into “now” all at once
  3. Getting ready takes longer than the mind guessed
  4. Lateness is a clock problem, not a caring problem

3It works like this

  • A watch with only two settings: “plenty of time” and “too late”.
  • Guessing a journey without a map: you’re sure it’s twenty minutes until you’re on the road.
  • A kettle you forgot to switch on: you were waiting, but nothing was heating.

4Why the reaction fits

For many people with ADHD, sensing time passing is genuinely harder. Researchers describe it as part of how attention and self-control work, not as a sign of how much someone cares.

5The other side

Waiting can feel like being less important, and it costs the other person real time and sometimes money. Repeated lateness can wear down trust.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I’m late because I misjudge time, not because you matter less. I’m working on it.”

One small step: Set an alarm for when to leave, not when to arrive, and text “Leaving now” when you go. The waiting person can agree a time to start without them.

With care: Logic explains lateness; it doesn’t make the other person’s time free. A real fix (alarms, leaving earlier, a text) shows the care that the lateness hides.

Saying no to a last-minute invitationSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A friend texts “Pub in an hour?” and gets a no, though the person had nothing on.

2The logic behind it

  1. The evening already had a plan, even if the plan was rest
  2. A new plan means cancelling the old one
  3. Rest that gets cancelled rarely comes back later in the week
  4. Saying no to the invite is saying yes to the plan they had

3It works like this

  • A reserved seat: it looks empty, but it’s taken.
  • A phone left charging for tomorrow’s long day.
  • A savings pot: it looks like spare money until you remember what it’s for.

4Why the reaction fits

Rest and quiet are real plans, even when nothing is written in a calendar. Protecting them keeps a person able to show up well for the plans they do make.

5The other side

The friend may feel turned down, or wonder if they’re wanted. If it happens often, they may stop asking.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Tonight’s my quiet night. I’d really like to see you. Can we pick a day?”

One small step: When you say no, offer a real day in the same message. Friends can send two options: “Tonight, or Saturday?”

“Let’s get coffee sometime” with no dateA common social habit, explained

1What it looks like from outside

Someone says “We should get coffee sometime!” and then never sets a day.

2The logic behind it

  1. The phrase is a friendly signal, not a booking
  2. It says “I like you and I’m open to more”
  3. A fixed date would turn it into a commitment neither of you has checked
  4. Leaving it vague keeps it warm and low-pressure for both

3It works like this

  • A wave across the street: it means “hello, I see you”, not “come over”.
  • “See you around”: nobody checks a calendar for it.
  • A shop’s “Do come back!” as you leave: kind, but not an appointment.

4Why the reaction fits

Many people use loose invitations to show warmth without putting anyone on the spot. Either person can turn it into a real plan later, or let it rest without anyone losing face.

5The other side

Someone who takes words literally hears a plan, waits for it, and then feels let down when nothing happens. It can feel like a broken promise.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“When I say ‘sometime’, I mean I’d like to. If you want a real date, ask me for one and I’ll say yes or no.”

One small step: If you want it to happen, ask: “Shall we pick a day?” If you said “sometime” and don’t mean it yet, don’t worry: the other person can ask too.

“Maybe” that means noA common social habit, explained

1What it looks like from outside

Someone answers an invitation with “Maybe, I’ll see,” and everyone else seems to know they won’t come.

2The logic behind it

  1. A flat “no” can feel like a door shut in someone’s face
  2. “Maybe” lets the other person down slowly
  3. Both people keep their dignity: nobody was refused out loud
  4. Many listeners hear the no inside the maybe

3It works like this

  • A dimmer switch instead of a light switch: the light still goes off, just gently.
  • Easing off the accelerator instead of slamming the brakes.
  • A shop’s “We’ll keep your details on file”.

4Why the reaction fits

Indirect answers are a common way to say something awkward while keeping the relationship safe. Researchers describe this kind of speech as a way for both people to avoid an open refusal.

5The other side

For someone who takes words at face value, a “maybe” leaves a real question open. They plan around it, wait for an answer, and later feel misled.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I say ‘maybe’ when I don’t want to hurt your feelings. I’ll try to give you a clear yes or no.”

One small step: It’s fair to ask, kindly: “Is that a maybe-yes or a maybe-no?” And a clear “No, but thank you” is kinder than it sounds.

Wearing headphones or earplugs around other peopleNeurodivergent logic, explained

1What it looks like from outside

At a family meal or in the office, someone has headphones or earplugs in.

2The logic behind it

  1. Sound arrives whether you want it or not
  2. A brain that filters less hears all of it at once
  3. Filtering by hand uses up attention
  4. Headphones do the filtering, so attention is left for people

3It works like this

  • Sunglasses on a bright day: you still see everything, just without squinting.
  • A spam filter: the important emails get through because the rest don’t.
  • Turning the radio down to read a road sign.

4Why the reaction fits

Many autistic people, and many people with ADHD or a sensitive nervous system, take in more of the background. Some researchers think this is partly about how much the brain predicts and smooths out what it hears. Turning the volume down can be what makes staying possible.

5The other side

The people around may feel shut out, or think the person doesn’t want to talk to them.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“These help me stay, not leave. Tap my arm and I’m all yours.”

One small step: Agree a signal (a tap, a wave) that means “I’m talking to you.” Where you can, lower the noise for everyone: music down, one conversation at a time.

Pulling away from a hug or a touchSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

Someone reaches for a hug and the other person stiffens, steps back or turns it into a quick pat.

2The logic behind it

  1. Touch is input, and unexpected touch is loud input
  2. Some bodies feel light touch as sharp or itchy
  3. Bracing for it takes energy
  4. Stepping back protects the body; it doesn’t reject the person

3It works like this

  • A cold hand on the back of your neck: you flinch even if you love the person.
  • Being tapped on the shoulder while you’re concentrating hard.
  • Someone turning the volume up to full on your favourite song.

4Why the reaction fits

People differ a lot in how touch feels. For some, including many autistic people, light or surprise touch is uncomfortable, while firm, expected pressure feels fine. The body reacts before kindness can be weighed.

5The other side

A hug may be how the other person says “I love you” or “I’m sorry”. A pull-back can feel like that love was refused.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“It’s not you. Surprise touch is loud for me. Ask, and I’ll often say yes.”

One small step: Ask first: “Hug, high five or wave?” Find the version that works for both: a firm hug, a hand squeeze, or sitting close.

Snapping when hungry or tiredSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A small question at the end of a long day gets a sharp, short answer.

2The logic behind it

  1. Patience runs on fuel
  2. Hunger and tiredness drain the tank
  3. With an empty tank, small things feel big
  4. The snap is the fuel gauge talking, not a verdict on you

3It works like this

  • A phone on 3% that suddenly starts closing apps.
  • A car running on fumes: it still drives, badly.
  • A rubber band that has been stretched all day: one more pull and it snaps.

4Why the reaction fits

Studies link poor sleep with more conflict between partners the next day, and suggest hunger can colour how harshly we judge things, especially when we don’t notice we’re hungry. The snap is real information about the body’s state.

5The other side

The person who asked did nothing wrong. A sharp tone still lands on them, and it can make them feel like they have to tiptoe.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I’m running on empty. It’s not about you. Can we talk after I’ve eaten?”

One small step: Name the state before you answer (“I’m wiped”), eat or rest, then come back and repair. Keep big topics away from the hungry, tired times of day.

With care: Explaining the snap doesn’t undo it. A quick “Sorry I snapped, I was starving” after the snack is part of the logic working properly.

Going quiet or irritable in a busy shopSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

In a crowded supermarket, the person goes quiet, walks fast, or gets short with you.

2The logic behind it

  1. Lights, music, people and choices all arrive at once
  2. Each one needs a little processing
  3. The processor fills up
  4. Shutting down extras, like chat, keeps them able to finish

3It works like this

  • Too many tabs open in a browser: everything slows and the fan gets loud.
  • A juggler handed one more ball.
  • A crowded inbox: the more that arrives, the shorter each reply.

4Why the reaction fits

Busy places put a lot of input into the senses at once. People who take in more of it, including many autistic people and many people with ADHD, can reach their limit quickly. Short answers save what is left.

5The other side

The person with them may feel snapped at or ignored, and may not understand why a simple trip got tense.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“It’s the shop, not you. I’ll talk properly once we’re back in the car.”

One small step: Shop with a list at quieter times, split the list, or agree a word that means “I’m near my limit, let’s finish fast.”

Cancelling plans after a long weekSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

On Friday afternoon, a text arrives: “So sorry, I can’t make tonight.”

2The logic behind it

  1. Energy recharges with rest
  2. This week spent all of it
  3. Going out at zero means being there in body but not in mind
  4. Cancelling spares the friendship a flat, tired evening

3It works like this

  • Not driving when you’re too tired to drive safely.
  • A phone that needs a full charge, not one more call.
  • A field left fallow for a season so it can grow again.

4Why the reaction fits

Rest is what makes good company possible. Sometimes the kindest thing a tired person can offer is a better evening later, not a worse one now.

5The other side

The friend may have cleared their own evening, booked a table or been looking forward to it. Last-minute cancelling can feel like they come last.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“I’m cancelling because I’m empty, not because you don’t matter. Can we pick a new day now?”

One small step: Cancel as early as you can and offer a new date in the same message. If it keeps happening, plan lighter evenings, like a walk or a takeaway at home.

With care: Cancelling often, or at the last minute, does cost the other person. Saying so and offering a real alternative keeps it fair.

Wearing the same clothes again and againNeurodivergent logic, explained

1What it looks like from outside

Someone wears the same soft jumper or the same few outfits nearly every day, even to events.

2The logic behind it

  1. Every fabric is a feeling on the skin all day long
  2. A scratchy label can feel like a pebble in your shoe
  3. A known outfit removes one thing to cope with
  4. That frees attention for the day itself

3It works like this

  • A pebble in your shoe: small, but you think about nothing else.
  • A favourite pen: any pen writes, but this one doesn’t get in the way.
  • A uniform: one less decision every morning.

4Why the reaction fits

For many people with sensory differences, comfort isn’t a luxury: it decides how much attention is left for everything else. Fewer choices in the morning also leaves more energy for later.

5The other side

Family may feel the person isn’t making an effort for an occasion, or worry what others will think.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Comfy clothes let me give the day my full attention.”

One small step: Find a comfortable version of “smart” together: soft fabrics, labels cut out, the same cut in a dressier colour.

Needing time alone after seeing peopleSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

After visitors leave, the person heads straight to another room and shuts the door.

2The logic behind it

  1. Time with people can be lovely and still costly
  2. The cost has to be paid back
  3. Quiet time is how it gets paid back
  4. After it, the person comes back recharged

3It works like this

  • Wringing out a sponge so it can soak up more.
  • Cooling down after a run.
  • A rechargeable torch going back on its stand.

4Why the reaction fits

People recover from social time at different speeds. For many introverted people, many autistic people and many people who mask, quiet after company is how they recover, not a sign the company was bad.

5The other side

A partner or host may feel shut out just when they wanted to chat about how it went, or wonder if they did something wrong.

A driver who tailgates or feels aggressiveSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A car sits close behind you, merges tightly, or uses the horn more than you would.

2The logic behind it

  1. Driving habits are learned locally
  2. Normal following distance, merging and horn use differ a lot between places
  3. Someone from out of town may simply be driving the way everyone does where they learned
  4. It isn’t aimed at you: it’s a different rulebook, not an insult

3It works like this

  • An accent: it’s how they learned to speak the road.
  • A different dialect for the same instruction: in one town a horn means “hello, I’m here”, in another it means “get out of my way”.
  • Someone who stands closer when they talk because that’s normal where they grew up.

4Why the reaction fits

What counts as a safe gap, a polite merge or a friendly toot really does vary from place to place, and people drive by the rules they learned without thinking. Taking it personally turns a different rulebook into an insult, and ruins your drive.

5The other side

They may not know they feel close to you. On their roads, this may be ordinary, careful driving.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“They’re probably driving the way people do where they learned. It isn’t about me.”

One small step: Give space, let them pass, and stay safe. Never retaliate.

With care: If a driver is truly dangerous, keep your distance and let them go. Don’t engage.

Tipping a different amount from youSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

At the end of a meal, one person leaves a big tip and the other leaves little or none, and each is quietly surprised.

2The logic behind it

  1. Tipping rules are learned where you grew up or work
  2. In some places a tip is part of the wage; in others it’s a small extra, or not expected at all
  3. Each person is following the rule that feels normal to them
  4. The amount says “this is how it’s done”, not “I’m mean” or “I’m showing off”

3It works like this

  • Driving on the left or the right: neither is wrong, but it matters which road you’re on.
  • Shaking hands, kissing once, or kissing twice when you say hello.
  • Different plugs for the same electricity.

4Why the reaction fits

Tipping customs vary a great deal between countries, regions and kinds of work. People who have worked in service jobs often tip differently too, because they know what the tip means to the person on the other end.

5The other side

The other person may feel embarrassed, judged, or worried the server was treated unfairly.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Where I learned, this is what people tip. What’s normal here?”

One small step: Check the local custom before you pay, or agree in advance how you’ll split it. When in doubt, ask the place itself.

Different ideas of “on time”Something anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

One person arrives at 7 sharp for a 7 o’clock dinner; the other arrives at 7.40 and thinks that’s on time.

2The logic behind it

  1. “Seven o’clock” is read through a local rule
  2. In some families and places it means seven exactly; in others it means “from seven”
  3. Each person turns up at the time their rule says is polite
  4. Neither is being careless; they’re reading the same words differently

3It works like this

  • A recipe that says “a pinch”: everyone’s pinch is different.
  • A “quick drink” that means twenty minutes to one person and three hours to another.
  • Two clocks set to different time zones in the same room.

4Why the reaction fits

How strictly a time is kept varies between cultures, families, workplaces and kinds of event. A party, a dinner and a train each have different unwritten rules.

5The other side

The person who arrived “on time” may have waited, felt unimportant, or had food go cold.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Where I grew up, seven meant come from seven. I’ll check what you mean next time.”

One small step: Say which kind of time you mean: “Seven sharp, the table is booked” or “Any time after seven.”

A family that talks loudly and over each otherSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

At a partner’s family dinner, everyone talks at once, loudly, and it sounds like arguing.

2The logic behind it

  1. Every family has its own volume and turn-taking rules
  2. In some homes talking over each other means everyone is engaged
  3. Loud can mean warm and lively, not angry
  4. The noise is their normal, not a fight

3It works like this

  • A busy market where the shouting is how business gets done.
  • Supporters singing at a match: loud, together, happy.
  • Jazz musicians playing over each other on purpose.

4Why the reaction fits

Families and cultures differ in how loud and overlapping conversation is. In some, jumping in shows you’re interested; in others, waiting your turn shows respect.

5The other side

Someone from a quieter home may feel overwhelmed, unable to get a word in, or think everyone is cross.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“We’re not arguing. This is just how my family talks.”

One small step: The quieter person can take short breaks or ask a direct question to get a turn. The family can pause to ask them in by name.

Feedback at work that sounds harsh, or too softSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A new colleague says “This is wrong” in a meeting. Another says “Maybe we could think about it” and means “This is wrong.”

2The logic behind it

  1. How directly people give feedback is learned at home, at school and at work
  2. In some workplaces plain words show respect and trust
  3. In others, softer words protect the relationship
  4. Each person is being polite by the rules they learned

3It works like this

  • Two people using different units, miles and kilometres, for the same distance.
  • A thermostat set to a different temperature: what feels warm to one feels cold to the other.
  • A volume knob: one person’s “normal” is another’s “too loud”.

4Why the reaction fits

Workplaces and cultures differ in how bluntly disagreement is expressed. Neither style is wrong, but mixing them without knowing causes a lot of hurt.

5The other side

The softer speaker may feel attacked by plain words; the plainer speaker may feel misled when soft words hid a real problem.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Where I trained, we say it straight so it’s quick to fix. Tell me if you’d like it gentler.”

One small step: Ask early: “How do you like feedback: straight, or with some cushioning?” Agree a style for the team.

Texting habits that seem cold or oddSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A parent replies “OK.” with a full stop; a teenager reads anger. Or an older relative phones when a text would do.

2The logic behind it

  1. Texting habits are learned in a particular time and group
  2. For some, a full stop is just how a sentence ends; for others it signals annoyance
  3. Phoning can mean care for one person and intrusion for another
  4. Each person is writing in the style they learned

3It works like this

  • Handwriting: neat or messy, it isn’t about how they feel about you.
  • A letter that starts “Dear”: formal, but not cold.
  • An accent in writing.

4Why the reaction fits

Different age groups and friendship groups use punctuation, emojis and calls differently. The same message can carry different tones to different readers.

5The other side

The reader may feel snubbed, scolded or interrupted, and worry about what was meant.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“My full stop isn’t cross. It’s just how I write.”

One small step: Talk about it once, on a calm day: what does “OK.” mean to each of you? Agree when to call and when to text.

Saying no to food the first time it’s offeredSomething anyone can do

1What it looks like from outside

A guest says “No, thank you” to food, and the host keeps offering, or the guest seems to expect it to be offered again.

2The logic behind it

  1. Hospitality rules are learned at home
  2. In some families a first “no” is just politeness, and the host offers again
  3. In others, a no means no straight away
  4. Both host and guest are being polite by their own rules

3It works like this

  • A dance where each partner knows different steps.
  • Holding a door: “After you”, “No, after you”.
  • A handshake where one person goes for a hug.

4Why the reaction fits

In many cultures and families, refusing once before accepting is good manners, and offering more than once is generous. Elsewhere, it feels like pressure.

5The other side

The host may feel their food is unwanted; the guest may feel pushed or end up hungry.

6One sentence to explain it, and one small step

“Where I grew up, we say no once to be polite. If I say no twice, I mean it.”

One small step: Hosts can make it easy: “There’s plenty, help yourself whenever.” Guests can say “Yes please” or “I’m really full, thank you” clearly.
