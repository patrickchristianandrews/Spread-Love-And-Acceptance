Friends: when a friendship drifts

Most friendships don’t end in a fight. They drift. One friend keeps reaching out, the other goes quiet, and nobody says anything. Usually both still care. This page is for both of you: the friend who always texts first, and the friend who has been underwater with a new baby, a new job, an illness or a loss.

In short:

  • Reaching out? Say it once, kindly, with one small ask.
  • Gone quiet? A ten-second message is enough. You don’t need a good time or a long reply.
  • For both: no ledger of who texted first. Name what’s been missing, then move on to the next nice thing.

If you’re always the one reaching out

It hurts to feel like the only one holding a friendship up. You plan, you remember the birthdays, you text first. After a while it can feel like you care more than they do. That feeling is real, and it is worth saying out loud.

It is rarely the whole story, though. The quiet friend may be more tired, busier or more stuck than you can see from the outside.

  1. Check what’s going on for them. A new baby, a new job, an illness, grief, a move? Going quiet is usually about how much someone has left, not about you.
  2. Say it once, as a feeling and a small ask. Not a list of every time you texted first.
  3. Make the ask tiny. Ten minutes, a voice note, a walk. Easy to say yes to.
  4. Then give it a few days. One kind message works better than three.

Words you could use

“I miss you and I’ve been feeling a bit left out. Could we find ten minutes this week?”

“I know life is full right now. No pressure to reply properly. I’d love a voice note when you can.”

“I’ve noticed I’m usually the one who plans. I don’t mind, I’d just love to hear from you first sometimes.”

When you are hurt, sarcasm slips in easily: “I guess I’m only your friend when it suits you.” It comes from hurt, but it lands as an accusation, and it is hard to answer. Say the hurt instead: “I miss you.” Not sure how a message sounds? Check it first in the Signal Translator.

If you’re the one who went quiet

Some seasons take everything you have. A new baby, a new job, an illness, caring for someone, a loss. Going quiet in those times is very common, and it doesn’t mean you don’t care. The guilt can make replying feel even harder, so the gap grows.

Your friend doesn’t need a long catch-up. They need to know they still matter to you.

  1. Send something short today. Ten seconds is enough. A small message now beats a perfect one next month.
  2. Say why, in a few words, so they don’t fill the silence with “it’s me.”
  3. Offer one easy next thing. A voice note, a call while you walk, or them coming over while you’re in pajamas.
  4. Thank them for reaching out. They have been holding the thread for both of you.

Words you could use (ready to send)

“I’ve been underwater with the baby. I miss you and I’m not ignoring you. Voice note this weekend?”

“The new job is eating me alive. You’re still one of my favorite people. Can I call you Sunday while I walk?”

“I’ve been having a hard time and went quiet with everyone. It’s not you. Thank you for still texting.”

“Thank you for always being the one who reaches out. I notice, even when I’m slow to reply.”

Friendships after a baby

A baby changes every friendship for a while. Sleep, time and attention all go to one small person. A friend without kids can feel left behind. A friend with kids may be just as tired. Both are normal, and most friendships come through it.

If you’re the new parent

  • Voice notes beat texts. You can send one during a feed, one-handed, and they carry your tone better than text.
  • Invite them into the mess. “Come over, I’ll be in pajamas, bring snacks” is a real invitation.
  • Ask about their life too, even briefly: “Tell me something from outside the baby bubble.”

If you’re the friend

  • Go to them. Offer a walk with the stroller, or drop off food and stay twenty minutes.
  • Lower the bar for replies. “No need to reply” takes the pressure off.
  • Keep inviting, even if they say no for a while. Being asked still feels good.

For both of you: a light touch

  • No ledger. Counting who texted first turns a friendship into a scoreboard. Name what’s been missing once, kindly, then move on.
  • Different rhythms, not different caring. Some people text every day. Others go quiet for weeks and pick up right where they left off. Neither is wrong.
  • Agree one small ritual, so nobody has to plan it every time: first-Saturday coffee, a monthly call, a shared playlist.
  • It’s okay if it changes. Some friendships get smaller for a season and grow again later.

Bonding tips for friends

Nothing holds a friendship in place except the two of you choosing it. It runs on small, repeated moments more than big catch-ups, so a few easy habits do a lot.

  • Do something side by side. A walk, a market, a class, a match. Many friends talk more easily when they aren’t sitting face to face.
  • Remember one thing and ask about it. The job interview, the sore knee, the sister’s wedding. “How did Thursday go?” shows you were listening.
  • Keep a shared thread going. A song you both love, a show you text about, a photo of every terrible menu you find.
  • Ask for a small favour now and then. Help choosing a sofa, a lift to the station. Being needed draws people closer, and it lets them give back.
  • Say what they mean to you, once in a while. One plain line is enough: “I’m really glad you’re my friend.”
