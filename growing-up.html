Where your lens came from: how growing up shapes the way you see yourself and others

Simple versionRead the full guide

Going gently. This page looks back at growing up. You can read one card and stop, and come back another day. Nothing here needs finishing.

Not every home felt calm or kind. If “they were doing their best” doesn’t fit your story, that’s okay. Take only what is useful to you and leave the rest.

Every stage of growing up asks a big question: Is the world safe? Am I capable? Where do I belong? The answers we land on can become understandings many people keep using as adults, usually without noticing. Together they make up your lens. Most of these understandings serve us well. Some were right for the child we were and are out of date for the adult we are now. They can change.

Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed and the kitchen is a mess. Before you’ve had a single thought, you feel it: “Of course. It’s always me.” Nobody said that. But in the house you grew up in, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it, and anyone resting while there was work to do was lazy. That rule comes from your childhood, and it just made a decision for you.

  1. You see the world through a lens you didn’t choose.

    Family, school, friends, culture, money at home, what you watched and what’s on your phone all taught you what to expect: of yourself, of other people, and of how a “good” partner, parent or friend behaves. By adulthood your lens doesn’t feel like a lens. It feels like simply seeing.

    Dig deeper: the lens idea

  2. Each stage of growing up asks a different question.

    Researchers call these developmental stages. The ages are rough, and people move at their own pace:

    • First years (about 0 to 1) “Is the world safe? Will someone come?”
    • Toddler years (about 2 to 3) “Can I do it myself?”
    • Early childhood (about 4 to 5) “Is it okay to start things and take up space?”
    • School years (about 6 to 11) “Am I capable?”
    • Teen years (about 12 to 17) “Who am I, and where do I belong?”
    • Young adulthood (about 18 to 39) “Can I be close without losing myself?”

    The answers can become understandings many people keep using as adults. We often come back to these questions at big changes, and each time we can answer a little differently.

    Dig deeper: the stages, with a table

  3. When something happened matters.

    An experience that touches the question you were answering at the time tends to go deeper. Researchers call the times when a growing brain is especially ready to learn certain things sensitive periods. A move at 4 is mostly about routines; a move at 14, in the middle of “Where do I belong?”, can leave a longer feeling of being the outsider. Repetition, what you decided it meant about you, and whether someone helped you make sense of it matter too. Deeper isn’t permanent: people keep learning all their lives.

    Dig deeper: why timing matters

  4. Some of what you believe about yourself was learned when you were a child and didn’t have all the facts.

    For example: “I’m only okay when I’m useful.” “My needs are a burden.” “Mistakes mean I’m bad.” A child often can’t know what was going on for the grown-ups; they only know how it felt. As an adult, you’re allowed to look again with the full picture. Looking again doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine. Some things that hurt really were not okay.

    Dig deeper: old stories and fairer readings

  5. The lens colors how you read the people you love.

    If silence at home meant anger, a partner’s quiet evening can feel like trouble is on the way. We also tend to blame other people’s slips on who they are (“lazy”) and our own on our day (“I was swamped”). Ask before you conclude: “When you went quiet, what was going on for you?”

    Dig deeper: misreading others

  6. An expectation nobody said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only miss it.

    “If you loved me, you’d just know.” “A good partner plans the birthdays.” “Rest is earned.” “Whoever notices, does it.” Each is somebody’s family rule, not a law of nature. When two people follow two different rulebooks, each sure theirs is normal, resentment grows without anyone doing wrong.

    Dig deeper: the expectations we place on others

  7. Write down the rulebook from home.

    Everyone grew up with an unwritten rulebook from home. Finish these six lines the way your childhood home would have: “In my family, love looked like…” “When someone was upset, we…” “The jobs were split like this…” “Resting was okay when…” “Asking for help meant…” “The unforgivable thing was…” Then ask of each line: do I still want this rule?   8. A one-minute lens check.

    When a reaction feels bigger than the moment: Where did I learn this? How old does this feel? Is it true here, with this person, now? What would the adult me choose? Do it once you’ve settled, not in the heat of it.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, with words to say

  9. Two rulebooks, one home: write a new one together.

    On a calm day, swap rulebooks with curiosity, not correction. Then choose your own rules on purpose: who owns which job, how upset looks in each of you, and what helps. It isn’t about deciding whose family did it right.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Ask whether a feeling is the size of this moment or older (Pillar III), and notice what you were praised or scolded for as a child still pulling on you (Pillar V). Holding an old rule in check is real, hidden effort (Pillar I).
  • Between you and others: A learned rule is part of the setup, not a flaw in anyone (Pillar II). Say your rules out loud, check what the other person heard, and choose shared ones (Pillar IV).

Try this: Think of one “always” or “never” you felt this week. Ask: whose voice does this sound like, and how old did I feel? Then write one sentence the adult you would rather live by.
