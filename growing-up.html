Where your lens came from: how growing up shapes the way you see yourself and others

Simple versionRead the full guide

Every stage of growing up asks a big question: Is the world safe? Am I capable? Where do I belong? The answers we land on become understandings we carry forward and keep using as adults, usually without noticing. Most serve us well. Some were right for the child we were and are out of date for the adult we are now.

Where this fits: This page mainly uses Pillar III, Read your state first, and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, Pillar II and Pillar IV. They are all part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice when an old understanding is doing the thinking. Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed and the kitchen is a mess. Before you’ve had a single thought, you feel it: “Of course. It’s always me.” Nobody said that. But in the house you grew up in, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it, and anyone resting while there was work to do was lazy. That rule is thirty years old, and it just made a decision for you.

  1. You see the world through a lens you didn’t choose.

    Family, school, friends, culture, money at home, what you watched and what’s on your phone all taught you what to expect: of yourself, of other people, and of how a “good” partner, parent or friend behaves. By adulthood the lens doesn’t feel like a lens. It feels like simply seeing.

    Dig deeper: the lens idea

  2. Each stage of growing up asks a different question.

    Roughly: first years, “Is the world safe?” Toddler years, “Can I do it myself?” Early childhood, “Is it okay to take up space?” School years, “Am I capable?” Teen years, “Who am I, and where do I belong?” Young adulthood, “Can I be close without losing myself?” The answers become the understandings we use for the rest of life, and we revisit them at every big change.

    Dig deeper: the stages, with a table

  3. When something happened matters.

    An experience that touches the question you were answering at the time tends to go deeper. A move at 4 is mostly about routines; a move at 14, in the middle of “Where do I belong?”, can leave a longer feeling of being the outsider. Repetition, what you decided it meant about you, and whether someone helped you make sense of it matter too. Deeper isn’t permanent: people keep learning all their lives.

    Dig deeper: why timing matters

  4. Some of what you believe about yourself was decided by a child with half the facts.

    “I’m only okay when I’m useful.” “My needs are a burden.” “I’m too much.” “Mistakes mean I’m bad.” A child can’t know the grown-ups were tired or worried; they only know how it felt. As an adult, you’re allowed to look again with the full picture.

    Dig deeper: old stories and fairer readings

  5. The lens colors how you read the people you love.

    If silence at home meant anger, a partner’s quiet evening can feel like a storm coming. We also tend to blame other people’s slips on who they are (“lazy”) and our own on our day (“I was swamped”). Ask before you conclude: “When you went quiet, what was going on for you?”

    Dig deeper: misreading others

  6. Unspoken expectations can only be broken.

    “If you loved me, you’d just know.” “A good partner plans the birthdays.” “Rest is earned.” “Whoever notices, does it.” Each is somebody’s family rule, not a law of nature. When two people follow two different rulebooks, each sure theirs is normal, resentment grows without anyone doing wrong.

    Dig deeper: the expectations we place on others

  7. Write down the rulebook from home.

    Finish these the way your childhood home would have: “Love looked like…” “When someone was upset, we…” “The jobs were split like this…” “Resting was okay when…” “Asking for help meant…” Then ask of each line: do I still want this rule?

    Dig deeper: the six rulebook lines

  8. A one-minute lens check.

    When a reaction feels bigger than the moment: Where did I learn this? How old does this feel? Is it true here, with this person, now? What would the adult me choose? Do it once you’ve settled, not in the heat of it.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, with words to say

  9. Two rulebooks, one home: write a new one together.

    On a calm day, swap rulebooks with curiosity, not correction. Then choose your own rules on purpose: who owns which job, how upset looks in each of you, and what helps. It isn’t about deciding whose family did it right.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Ask whether a feeling is the size of this moment or older (Pillar III), and notice what you were praised or scolded for as a child still pulling on you (Pillar V). Holding an old rule in check is real, hidden effort (Pillar I).
  • Between you and others: A learned rule is part of the setup, not a flaw in anyone (Pillar II). Say your rules out loud, check what the other person heard, and choose shared ones (Pillar IV).

Try this: Think of one “always” or “never” you felt this week. Ask: whose voice does this sound like, and how old did I feel? Then write one sentence the adult you would rather live by.
