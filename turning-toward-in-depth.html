Turning Toward

Full version← Back to the simple version

A Tuesday, nothing wrong

“Look at this,” they say, holding up their phone. You could glance up, say “mm”, and go back to what you were doing. Or you could lean over, look, and ask about it. It feels like too small a choice to matter.

But most days together are ordinary days like this one, not big occasions. Those tiny moments add up to most of your time together, so how you answer them quietly decides how close you feel.

Researchers who followed newlywed couples for six years saw this clearly. The couples who were still together had turned toward these small “look at this” moments most of the time. The couples who split up had done it only about a third of the time.

Most of this program is about what to do when something has gone wrong: the log, the treaty, the audit, the check-in. This page is about the other side, the part that makes the hard conversations easier when they come. Research on couples, friends and families keeps finding the same thing: connection is built less in big conversations than in small, ordinary moments, answered warmly, many times over. These practices are for every kind of relationship on this site, not only partners.

None of this replaces fairness. Warmth doesn’t make an unfair split of work acceptable, and a fair split doesn’t make up for coldness. The two support each other.

1. Notice bids, and turn toward them

A bid is any small reach for attention, interest or support: “look at this,” a sigh, a joke, a question about your day, a hand on the shoulder, a link sent with no comment. The researcher John Gottman and colleagues observed that people answer bids in one of three ways:

ResponseWhat it looks likeHow it tends to land
Turning towardLooking up, answering, asking one question, a small “oh nice”“You’re here with me.”
Turning awayNot noticing, staying on the phone, changing the subject“I don’t matter much right now.”
Turning againstIrritation: “Can’t you see I’m busy?”“Reaching for you costs me.”

In their studies, couples who more often turned toward each other’s everyday bids were warmer and more playful even when they later argued. Turning away is rarely cruel. It is usually tiredness or distraction, which is exactly why it matters to notice. Nobody catches every bid, and you don’t have to: what counts is the overall pattern.

If you can’t give attention right now, a short turn toward still counts: “I want to hear this. Give me ten minutes?” Then come back.

2. Say thank you, specifically

Much of the work of a shared life is invisible, which is where this program begins (the Preface). Thanks is how invisible work becomes seen. The psychologist Sara Algoe describes gratitude in close relationships as working three ways: it helps you find people who are good for you, reminds you of them, and binds you closer.

Specific thanks lands far better than general thanks, because it proves you noticed:

Instead of “thanks for everything,” try: “Thank you for sorting the school forms. I know that took your whole lunch break.”

Thank the thing, the effort, or the quality behind it (“you’re always the one who remembers birthdays”). Thanking someone for their share of the work doesn’t cancel a conversation about fairness. It makes that conversation easier.

3. Celebrate good news

How you respond when someone shares good news may matter as much as how you respond to bad news. Shelly Gable and colleagues found four common responses:

ResponseExample, to “I got the job!”
Active and warm“That’s brilliant! What did they say? When do you start?”
Quiet and warm“Oh, nice.” (then back to what you were doing)
Active and deflating“Does it pay more? Isn’t the commute awful?”
Quiet and deflating“Did you remember to pick up milk?”

Only the first, active and warm, was linked to closer, more satisfying relationships. The other three are common and usually meant kindly, especially the practical worry in the third. Save the practical questions for tomorrow. Tonight, ask one question that lets them tell the story again.

The Conversation Reader watches for good news met with a flat reply, because it is one of the most common ways people drift apart without meaning to.

4. The stress-reducing conversation

This practice, described by Gottman, is a short daily or near-daily talk about stress from outside the relationship: work, family, health, the world. It is not about each other.

  1. Take turns. Each person gets about 10 to 15 minutes to talk about what’s weighing on them.
  2. The listener is on the speaker’s side. “That sounds exhausting.” “No wonder you’re fed up.” This is not the time to defend the boss or suggest the other side has a point.
  3. Don’t problem-solve unless you are asked. Ask instead: “Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?”
  4. Show you understand before anything else. A hand, a nod, “that makes sense.”

Stress from outside that nobody talks about tends to leak into the relationship as irritability. This conversation gives it somewhere to go. It pairs naturally with WP-13’s daily check-in and with Today’s Weather.

5. Small rituals of connection

A ritual is a small thing you do together on purpose, the same way each time, so it doesn’t depend on anyone’s mood. Examples:

  • How you say hello and goodbye: looking up, a real greeting, a hug that lasts a breath longer than usual.
  • A weekly walk, coffee, or phone call with nothing to sort out.
  • A question at dinner: “What was the best part of your day?”
  • For roommates or coworkers: a Friday “what went well this week” in five minutes.
  • For families: a weekly meal where devices go in a basket.

Keep rituals small enough to survive a bad week. A ritual that only happens when things are good isn’t a ritual yet.

6. Know their world

Gottman calls it a “love map”: a detailed picture of the other person’s inner world, what they’re worried about, looking forward to, and who matters to them right now. People change, so the map needs updating. Try one of these every so often, and actually listen to the answer:

  1. What’s on your mind this week that I don’t know about?
  2. Who at work (or school) do you enjoy right now? Who’s hard?
  3. What are you looking forward to this month?
  4. What’s something you’ve been wanting to try?
  5. What’s been stressing you that you haven’t mentioned?
  6. What did you love doing as a kid?
  7. What would make next weekend feel good for you?
  8. Is there something you wish I asked you about more?
  9. What’s a small thing I do that you like?
  10. What’s one thing you’re proud of lately?

7. Remember what you like about them

In a hard stretch, attention narrows to what’s going wrong. Deliberately remembering what you like, respect and admire about someone widens it again. It also guards against contempt, which Gottman’s research singles out as the most damaging of the conflict patterns he studied.

  • Once a week, name one quality you admire in them, and one moment when you saw it.
  • Tell someone else something good about them, and let it get back to them.
  • Remember how you met, or a hard time you got through together.

Across every kind of relationship

  • Partners: all seven apply. Start with bids and good news.
  • Family: specific thanks and knowing their world, especially across generations, where people often know old versions of each other.
  • Co-parents: keep it about the children and the effort: “Thanks for handling the dentist. She said you made it fun.” Warmth between co-parents helps children, even when the relationship itself is over.
  • Friends: good news and rituals. A standing call or walk keeps a friendship alive through busy years.
  • Roommates and coworkers: specific thanks for shared chores or work, and a short weekly “what went well”.
  • Caregivers: thanks and the stress-reducing conversation, in both directions. Caregivers are often thanked least.

If you’re wired differently

Bids and warmth don’t look the same for everyone. An autistic person may show care through information, practical help or shared interests rather than small talk. An ADHD person may miss bids during focus and turn toward warmly a minute later. Someone with alexithymia may find “how do you feel about my news?” hard, and “tell me what happened” easy. None of this is turning away.

The fix is to ask, and to write it down: how does each of you like to be greeted, thanked, and celebrated? What counts as a bid for you? The Wiring Card is a good place to record the answers, and Wired Differently explains why the same words reach people differently.

When these aren’t the right tool

These practices assume both people are safe with each other and both want things to be good. They are not a way to earn better treatment, and warmth isn’t owed to someone who threatens, controls or frightens you. If that’s where you are, please reach out for support: in the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233; for thoughts of suicide, call or text 988; elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free, confidential helplines.
