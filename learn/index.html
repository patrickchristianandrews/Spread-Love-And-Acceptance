Stories from Philosophy

Simple versionRead all twelve stories

Twelve old stories from philosophy, retold in plain words. They help you know yourself, see the other person more clearly, and keep a ledger without letting it replace the person.

  1. Start with yourself.

    The Buddha’s second arrow: the event hurts once, and the story you add about it hurts again. Many arguments are mostly second arrows. Epictetus adds a second lesson: your side is yours, and their answer is theirs.

    Dig deeper: part one, knowing yourself

  2. You can both be right about different parts.

    In the old story, each blind man touches one part of the elephant and reports it honestly. Two people in a relationship are always touching different parts. Don’t fill in their side for them. Ask and listen.

    Dig deeper: part two, seeing the other person

  3. Closeness is a distance you can talk about.

    Porcupines huddle for warmth, then pull apart from the quills, until they find a middle distance. Needing warmth isn’t clingy. Needing room isn’t cold.

    Dig deeper: part three, living together

  4. The ledger serves the person, never the other way round.

    Would you use these tools the same way if the other person could see everything you wrote? And the moment the numbers become the person, the ledger has failed.

    Dig deeper: part four, the ethics of keeping a ledger

Try this: Next time something stings, write two lines: “What happened” and “What I’m telling myself it means.” Only the first line belongs in the ledger.
