Brain Map

How wiring differs

Brains are wired in different ways, and research can show where those differences sit on average. Tap a part of the brain, or pick a way of being wired, to see where research finds differences, what it can feel like, and what helps.

Before you read the map

This is a map of group averages from research. People who share a label differ a lot from each other, and they overlap a great deal with people who do not have it. A scan cannot diagnose anyone, and nothing here is a diagnosis.

Brains change with experience, sleep, support and treatment, so a difference is a snapshot, not a verdict. A difference is not damage, and it is not anyone’s fault.

Some of these are lifelong neurodevelopmental differences: autistic, ADHD and dyslexic wiring. Others are health conditions that can improve: anxiety, depression, trauma and PTSD, and OCD. Both deserve understanding and support, and many people have more than one.

Where this comes from: Maguire et al., 2000; Månsson et al., 2016; Holiga et al., 2019.

Sources: where this comes from

Every finding on the map comes from one of the studies below, and each one is also listed, with a plain-words summary, on the research page. The links open the paper or its record in a new tab. The labels mean: Strong, replicated in large pooled studies; Moderate, pooled analyses or several consistent studies that are smaller, older or depend on the task; Early, a few small studies, one data set, or mostly expert reviews.

See them on the research page

  • Ben-Sasson, A., Hen, L., Fluss, R., et al. (2009)A meta-analysis of sensory modulation symptoms in individuals with autism spectrum disordersJournal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 39(1), 1–11

    Pooling 14 studies, autistic people reported or showed more sensory differences than comparison groups. The gap was largest for under-responding to sensations, then over-responding, then seeking sensations. The differences were largest in children aged 6 to 9.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A pooled analysis, backed by an update and by later reviews. Most studies relied on parent or self report.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1007/s10803-008-0593-3

  • Boedhoe, P. S. W., Schmaal, L., Abe, Y., et al. (2017)Distinct subcortical volume alterations in pediatric and adult OCD: a worldwide meta- and mega-analysisAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 174(1), 60–69

    Pooling scans from 1,830 people with OCD and 1,759 comparison people, adults with OCD had slightly smaller hippocampi and slightly larger pallidums on average (about 0.13 and 0.16 of a standard deviation), while children with OCD who were not taking medicine had larger thalamus volumes (about 0.38). The adult and child patterns differed.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A large pooled analysis. The effects are small to moderate and the groups overlap a great deal; medicine use affected the results.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.16020201

  • Boedhoe, P. S. W., Schmaal, L., Abe, Y., et al. (2018)Cortical abnormalities associated with pediatric and adult obsessive-compulsive disorder: findings from the ENIGMA Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder working groupAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 175(5), 453–462

    In scans of 1,905 people with OCD and 1,760 comparison people from 27 sites, the inferior parietal cortex was slightly thinner in both adults and children with OCD. Adults also showed a smaller surface area in one part of the temporal cortex. Effect sizes ranged from about 0.1 to 0.3 of a standard deviation.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A large pooled analysis. The effects are small and medicine use affected some results.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.17050485

  • Cerrillo-Urbina, A. J., García-Hermoso, A., Sánchez-López, M., Pardo-Guijarro, M. J., Santos Gómez, J. L., & Martínez-Vizcaíno, V. (2015)The effects of physical exercise in children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized control trialsChild: Care, Health and Development, 41(6), 779–788

    Across eight small trials (249 children with ADHD), exercise, especially aerobic exercise, improved attention, hyperactivity and related symptoms compared with control groups.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A meta-analysis of small randomized trials.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1111/cch.12255

  • Cortese, S., Kelly, C., Chabernaud, C., et al. (2012)Toward systems neuroscience of ADHD: a meta-analysis of 55 fMRI studiesAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 169(10), 1038–1055

    Pooling 55 brain-activity studies, people with ADHD showed on average less activation in the frontoparietal (control) and ventral attention networks, and more activation in the default network and some sensory and motor areas, than comparison people. The differences were seen even without other conditions or past stimulant use.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of 55 mostly small studies. Task choice matters, and the groups overlap a great deal.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.11101521

  • Cortese, S., Adamo, N., Del Giovane, C., et al. (2018)Comparative efficacy and tolerability of medications for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder in children, adolescents, and adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysisThe Lancet Psychiatry, 5(9), 727–738

    Pooling 133 double-blind trials, medicines for ADHD reduced core symptoms in the short term. Taking both benefit and side effects into account, methylphenidate (for children and teenagers) and amphetamines (for adults) came out as the preferred first choices. The authors called for studies of long-term effects.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A large analysis of 133 randomized trials, but short-term, and the long-term evidence is thin.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30269-4

  • Craske, M. G., Treanor, M., Conway, C. C., Zbozinek, T., & Vervliet, B. (2014)Maximizing exposure therapy: An inhibitory learning approachBehaviour Research and Therapy, 58, 10–23

    Describes exposure therapy for anxiety as building new learning that competes with old fear, and sets out ways to strengthen it, such as testing specific expectations, dropping safety behaviors and practicing in varied settings.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Theory-led clinical review drawing on lab and treatment research

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2014.04.006

  • Crompton, C. J., Ropar, D., Evans-Williams, C. V. M., Flynn, E. G., & Fletcher-Watson, S. (2020)Autistic peer-to-peer information transfer is highly effectiveAutism, 24(7), 1704–1712

    In "diffusion chains" where a story was passed along eight people (72 adults), all-autistic chains passed on as much detail as all-non-autistic chains, while mixed chains lost detail faster and reported lower rapport.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A careful experiment that supports the double empathy idea. Modest sample and a single task.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1177/1362361320919286

  • Cruwys, T., Dingle, G. A., Haslam, C., Haslam, S. A., Jetten, J., & Morton, T. A. (2013)Social group memberships protect against future depression, alleviate depression symptoms and prevent depression relapseSocial Science & Medicine, 98, 179–186

    In population data on adults aged 50 and over followed for two to four years, belonging to fewer groups predicted more depression later. Among people already depressed, joining one group was linked to a 24% lower risk of relapse and joining three to a 63% lower risk.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Large longitudinal samples, but observational, so links rather than proof

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2013.09.013

  • Demetriou, E. A., Lampit, A., Quintana, D. S., et al. (2018)Autism spectrum disorders: A meta-analysis of executive functionMolecular Psychiatry, 23, 1198–1204

    Pooling 235 studies with over 14,000 participants, found that autistic people on average scored lower than non-autistic people across executive function tasks, with wide variation and mixed findings across ages.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Large meta-analysis of lab tasks, which capture only part of daily life

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/mp.2017.75

  • Ekers, D., Webster, L., Van Straten, A., Cuijpers, P., Richards, D., & Gilbody, S. (2014)Behavioural activation for depression; an update of meta-analysis of effectiveness and sub group analysisPLoS ONE, 9(6), e100100

    Across 26 randomized trials (1,524 people), behavioral activation, planning and doing small valued activities, reduced depression more than control conditions, and at least as much as medication in the four trials that compared them.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Consistent effects, but most studies were small or low quality with short follow-up

    Read more → doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100

  • Etkin, A., & Wager, T. D. (2007)Functional neuroimaging of anxiety: a meta-analysis of emotional processing in PTSD, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobiaAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 164(10), 1476–1488

    Pooling brain-activity studies, people with PTSD, social anxiety disorder or a specific phobia showed, on average, more activity in the amygdala and the insula than comparison people while handling emotional material, similar to what happens in healthy people during fear learning. Only in PTSD were the front-of-brain areas that help settle feelings (parts of the cingulate and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex) less active.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of many small studies from before 2007. Later work generally agrees, but individual studies vary and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2007.07030504

  • Freeman, D., Sheaves, B., et al. (2017)The effects of improving sleep on mental health (OASIS): A randomised controlled trial with mediation analysisThe Lancet Psychiatry, 4(10), 749–758

    In 3,755 university students with insomnia, an online CBT course for sleep greatly reduced insomnia and also reduced paranoid thoughts and hallucinatory experiences compared with usual care, with better sleep explaining much of the change.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong Very large randomized trial. Students only, so results may not apply to everyone

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30328-0

  • Gabrieli, J. D. E. (2009)Dyslexia: a new synergy between education and cognitive neuroscienceScience, 325(5938), 280–283

    A review explaining that dyslexia often comes from difficulty linking the sounds of speech to letters, that brain studies of children with dyslexia show less engagement of the left temporoparietal cortex during reading, and that good teaching changes both reading and the brain's response. It argues that early, evidence-based teaching could prevent most reading failure.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An influential expert review. The brain findings are averages with large overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1126/science.1171999

  • Galuschka, K., Ise, E., Krick, K., & Schulte-Körne, G. (2014)Effectiveness of treatment approaches for children and adolescents with reading disabilities: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trialsPLoS ONE, 9(2), e89900

    Pooling 22 randomized trials, teaching that builds explicit links between sounds and letters (phonics) was the one approach with a clearly confirmed benefit for reading and spelling in children and adolescents with reading difficulties. The authors concluded that even severe reading and spelling difficulties can be improved with the right teaching.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate 22 randomized trials, but many were small, and other approaches had fewer studies, so they are not proven not to work.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0089900

  • Grove, R., Hoekstra, R. A., Wierda, M., & Begeer, S. (2018)Special interests and subjective wellbeing in autistic adultsAutism Research, 11(5), 766–775

    In a survey of autistic adults in the Netherlands Autism Register, about two-thirds reported a special interest. Greater motivation for the interest was linked to higher wellbeing, while very high intensity of engagement was linked to lower wellbeing.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Large survey, cross-sectional and self-report

    Read more → research.vu.nl

  • Hamilton, J. P., Etkin, A., Furman, D. J., et al. (2012)Functional neuroimaging of major depressive disorder: a meta-analysis and new integration of baseline activation and neural response dataAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 169(7), 693–703

    In response to negative material, people with depression showed on average more activity in the amygdala, insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and less in the dorsal striatum and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, than comparison people.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of many small studies; results depend on the task, and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2012.11071105

  • Hayes, J. P., Hayes, S. M., & Mikedis, A. M. (2012)Quantitative meta-analysis of neural activity in posttraumatic stress disorderBiology of Mood & Anxiety Disorders, 2, 9

    Pooling 26 brain-activity studies, people with PTSD showed more activity on average in the middle and dorsal anterior cingulate and (when region-of-interest studies were included) the amygdala, and less activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and the inferior frontal gyrus than comparison people.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of 26 studies. Results depend on the task and on which comparison group was used, and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1186/2045-5380-2-9

  • Hofmann, S. G., Asnaani, A., Vonk, I. J. J., Sawyer, A. T., & Fang, A. (2012)The efficacy of cognitive behavioral therapy: A review of meta-analysesCognitive Therapy and Research, 36(5), 427–440

    Reviewing 106 meta-analyses of cognitive behavioral therapy, found the strongest support for anxiety problems, bulimia, anger and general stress, with support across many other problems. Few studies looked at groups such as ethnic minorities or people on low incomes.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A review of many meta-analyses. Quality of the underlying trials varies

    Read more → doi.org/10.1007/s10608-012-9476-1

  • Holiga, Š., Hipp, J. F., Chatham, C. H., et al. (2019)Patients with autism spectrum disorders display reproducible functional connectivity alterationsScience Translational Medicine, 11(481), eaat9223

    In several large data sets, autistic people showed a reproducible pattern of differences in how brain regions work together. The pattern was only moderately linked to day-to-day traits and overlapped a lot with comparison people, so the authors say it is not usable for diagnosis.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Replicated across several data sets, with a large overlap between groups.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.aat9223

  • Hollocks, M. J., Lerh, J. W., Magiati, I., Meiser-Stedman, R., & Brugha, T. S. (2019)Anxiety and depression in adults with autism spectrum disorder: A systematic review and meta-analysisPsychological Medicine, 49(4), 559–572

    Pooling 35 studies, estimated that about 27% of autistic adults had an anxiety disorder at the time (42% over their lifetime) and about 23% had depression (37% lifetime), with wide variation between studies.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Meta-analysis. Very varied methods and many clinical samples

    Read more → doi.org/10.1017/S0033291718002283

  • Hoogman, M., Bralten, J., Hibar, D. P., et al. (2017)Subcortical brain volume differences in participants with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder in children and adults: a cross-sectional mega-analysisThe Lancet Psychiatry, 4(4), 310–319

    In 1,713 people with ADHD and 1,529 comparison people at 23 sites (ages 4 to 63), the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, caudate, hippocampus and putamen were slightly smaller on average in the ADHD group (very small effects, about 0.1 to 0.2 of a standard deviation). The pallidum and thalamus did not differ.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A very large pooled scan study. The effects are very small and the groups overlap almost completely.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(17)30049-4

  • Hoogman, M., Muetzel, R., Guimaraes, J. P., et al. (2019)Brain imaging of the cortex in ADHD: a coordinated analysis of large-scale clinical and population-based samplesAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 176(7), 531–542

    Comparing the cortex of 2,246 people with ADHD and 1,934 comparison people from 36 centers, the authors found small average differences in cortical thickness and surface area. Measures of the cortex also related, more weakly, to ADHD symptoms in people in the general population, which fits the view that ADHD sits at one end of a continuum.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A large coordinated analysis. The differences are small and not found in every age group.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2019.18091033

  • Hull, L., Petrides, K. V., Allison, C., Smith, P., Baron-Cohen, S., Lai, M.-C., & Mandy, W. (2017)"Putting on my best normal": Social camouflaging in adults with autism spectrum conditionsJournal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 47(8), 2519–2534

    Interviews with 92 autistic adults described camouflaging as motivated by wanting to fit in and connect, done through masking and compensating techniques, and often followed by exhaustion and threats to self-perception.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A qualitative study that shaped later measurement work.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1007/s10803-017-3166-5

  • Hull, J. V., Jacokes, Z. J., Torgerson, C. M., Irimia, A., & Van Horn, J. D. (2017)Resting-state functional connectivity in autism spectrum disorders: a reviewFrontiers in Psychiatry, 7, 205

    A review of brain-connectivity studies in autism. Studies have reported more connectivity, less connectivity, or a mix, and the authors link the differences between studies to age, sex, scan design and the wide variation among autistic people.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A review whose main message is that results are mixed.

    Read more → doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2016.00205

  • Jenkinson, R., Milne, E., & Thompson, A. (2020)The relationship between intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety in autism: A systematic literature review and meta-analysisAutism, 24(8), 1933–1944

    Across 10 studies pooled in a meta-analysis (12 reviewed), finding uncertainty hard to bear was strongly linked with anxiety in autistic people (r = 0.62), a link of similar size to that found in non-autistic people.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A meta-analysis, but of a small number of studies with methodological concerns. Correlational

    Read more → doi.org/10.1177/1362361320932437

  • Kaiser, R. H., Andrews-Hanna, J. R., Wager, T. D., & Pizzagalli, D. A. (2015)Large-scale network dysfunction in major depressive disorder: a meta-analysis of resting-state functional connectivityJAMA Psychiatry, 72(6), 603–611

    Pooling 27 data sets (556 people with depression and 518 comparison people), depression was linked on average to less connectivity within the frontoparietal control network and between it and a network that directs attention to the outside world.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of mostly small studies; the differences are modest and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2015.0071

  • Kuyken, W., et al. (2016)Efficacy of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy in prevention of depressive relapse: an individual patient data meta-analysis from randomized trialsJAMA Psychiatry, 73(6), 565–574

    Across nine trials (1,258 people with recurrent depression), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy cut the risk of relapse over 60 weeks by about 31% (38% relapsed versus 49%).

    ●●●Evidence: Strong An individual-patient meta-analysis of randomized trials.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0076

  • Lieberman, M. D., Eisenberger, N. I., Crockett, M. J., Tom, S. M., Pfeifer, J. H., & Way, B. M. (2007)Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuliPsychological Science, 18(5), 421–428

    In a brain-imaging study, labeling the emotion in a picture reduced amygdala and other limbic responses compared with other ways of processing the image.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Replicated lab finding. Effects are modest and mostly measured with pictures in the lab.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x

  • Logue, M. W., van Rooij, S. J. H., Dennis, E. L., et al. (2018)Smaller hippocampal volume in posttraumatic stress disorder: a multisite ENIGMA-PGC studyBiological Psychiatry, 83(3), 244–253

    Across 16 groups of researchers and 1,868 people, those with current PTSD had slightly smaller hippocampi on average than trauma-exposed people without PTSD (a small effect, about 0.17 of a standard deviation). The amygdala difference was smaller still and did not hold up after correcting for the number of regions tested.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A large pooled analysis with a fair comparison group (people who had also been through trauma). The effect is small and a scan study cannot say whether it was there before the trauma.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2017.09.006

  • Maguire, E. A., Gadian, D. G., Johnsrude, I. S., et al. (2000)Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi driversProceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 97(8), 4398–4403

    The back part of the hippocampus was larger on average in London taxi drivers than in comparison people, and it was larger the longer someone had driven a taxi, which suggests the adult brain changes with long experience.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A classic study with many follow-ups on brain change. It links experience and size and cannot prove one caused the other.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1073/pnas.070039597

  • Månsson, K. N. T., Salami, A., Frick, A., et al. (2016)Neuroplasticity in response to cognitive behavior therapy for social anxiety disorderTranslational Psychiatry, 6, e727

    In 26 people with social anxiety disorder who had nine weeks of internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy, the amygdala's volume and its response to criticism both fell on average, and the drop in volume tracked the drop in anxiety. A comparison treatment did not show the same changes.

    ●○○Evidence: Early One small trial, though it shows that the brain responds to effective treatment.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/tp.2015.218

  • Menon, V. (2011)Large-scale brain networks and psychopathology: a unifying triple network modelTrends in Cognitive Sciences, 15(10), 483–506

    Proposed that problems in how three large networks switch on and off, the salience, frontoparietal and default networks, show up across many conditions, with the salience network acting as a switch between the other two.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An influential model that has guided much research. It is a framework to test, not a settled finding.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.08.003

  • Menzies, L., Chamberlain, S. R., Laird, A. R., et al. (2008)Integrating evidence from neuroimaging and neuropsychological studies of obsessive-compulsive disorder: the orbitofronto-striatal model revisitedNeuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 32(3), 525–549

    A review and pooled analysis of brain-imaging studies of OCD. It tests the long-standing idea of an orbitofrontal-striatal loop and proposes a wider picture that includes other areas, including parts of the parietal cortex.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An expert review and pooled analysis from before the large scan studies.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2007.09.005

  • Milad, M. R., & Rauch, S. L. (2012)Obsessive-compulsive disorder: beyond segregated cortico-striatal pathwaysTrends in Cognitive Sciences, 16(1), 43–51

    A review arguing that OCD involves more than the loops linking the front of the brain, the striatum and the thalamus: the orbitofrontal cortex, the dorsal anterior cingulate and circuits through the amygdala also matter, and learning that a fear is no longer needed (extinction) may work differently.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An expert review of imaging and treatment studies. It describes circuits that appear involved, and cannot say what causes what.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2011.11.003

  • Milton, D. E. M. (2012)On the ontological status of autism: The “double empathy problem”Disability & Society, 27(6), 883–887

    A short conceptual paper arguing that misunderstanding between autistic and non-autistic people runs both ways: each side finds the other hard to read, so the problem lies between them, not only in the autistic person.

    ●○○Evidence: Early A conceptual paper, not a test of one. no data in the paper itself. Later experiments (e.g. Crompton and colleagues, 2020) support parts of it

    Read more → autism.org.uk

  • Murray, D., Lesser, M., & Lawson, W. (2005)Attention, monotropism and the diagnostic criteria for autismAutism, 9(2), 139–156

    Proposed "monotropism": autistic attention tends to be pulled strongly into a few interests at a time, and the authors argued this can explain the other diagnostic features of autism.

    ●○○Evidence: Early A conceptual paper, not a test of one. an influential, autistic-led account. Empirical testing is still limited.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1177/1362361305051398

  • National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (2011)Generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder in adults: management (CG113)NICE guideline (updated 2020)

    Recommends stepped care: self-help first, then CBT or applied relaxation (or medicine) for generalized anxiety, and CBT for panic disorder.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A national guideline based on reviews of trials.

    Read more → nice.org.uk

  • National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (2013)Social anxiety disorder: recognition, assessment and treatment (CG159)NICE guideline

    Recommends individual CBT developed specifically for social anxiety for adults with social anxiety disorder.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A national guideline based on reviews of trials.

    Read more → nice.org.uk

  • National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (2018)Post-traumatic stress disorder (NG116)NICE guideline

    Recommends trauma-focused CBT for adults with PTSD, and EMDR for adults with PTSD more than three months after a non-combat trauma.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A national guideline based on reviews of trials.

    Read more → nice.org.uk

  • National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) (2022)Depression in adults: treatment and management (NG222)NICE guideline NG222, London

    The national health guideline for England on depression: recommends a choice of treatments made together with the person, including guided self-help, group or individual CBT and behavioral activation, group exercise, other talking therapies and antidepressants.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong An official guideline based on systematic reviews. Some methods have been criticized

    Read more → nice.org.uk

  • Nolen-Hoeksema, S. (1991)Responses to depression and their effects on the duration of depressive episodesJournal of Abnormal Psychology, 100(4), 569–582

    Proposed the response styles theory: people who ruminate on their low mood tend to have longer depressions than people who distract themselves.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong Extensive, consistent later research.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.100.4.569

  • Olatunji, B. O., Davis, M. L., Powers, M. B., & Smits, J. A. J. (2013)Cognitive-behavioral therapy for obsessive-compulsive disorder: a meta-analysis of treatment outcome and moderatorsJournal of Psychiatric Research, 47(1), 33–41

    Pooling 16 randomized trials (756 people), cognitive behavioral therapy, centered on exposure and response prevention, reduced OCD symptoms much more than control conditions right after treatment, with a smaller but lasting benefit at follow-up.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong Pooled randomized trials with a large effect. Follow-up results are smaller and the trials vary in quality.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2012.08.020

  • Panagiotidi, M., Overton, P. G., & Stafford, T. (2017)The relationship between ADHD traits and sensory sensitivity in the general populationComprehensive Psychiatry, 80, 179–185

    In an online survey of 234 adults, more ADHD traits went with more reported sensory difficulties in every sense, suggesting sensory differences may be part of the ADHD picture.

    ●○○Evidence: Early A single survey of adults from the general population, with self-report only.

    Read more → eprints.whiterose.ac.uk

  • Pfeiffer, B., Stein Duker, L., Murphy, A., & Shui, C. (2019)Effectiveness of noise-attenuating headphones on physiological responses for children with autism spectrum disordersFrontiers in Integrative Neuroscience, 13, 65

    In six autistic children, wearing noise-attenuating headphones during the day changed skin-conductance measures of stress compared with baseline.

    ●○○Evidence: Early A single-subject design with six children.

    Read more → doi.org/10.3389/fnint.2019.00065

  • Pole, N. (2007)The psychophysiology of posttraumatic stress disorder: a meta-analysisPsychological Bulletin, 133(5), 725–746

    Pooling studies of the body's responses, adults with PTSD had a faster heart rate than adults without PTSD at rest, during startle tests and when reminded of the trauma. Heart rate was among the most consistent differences. Most studies were of male veterans.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of many studies, but one limited to the groups that were studied most.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.725

  • Raymaker, D. M., Teo, A. R., Steckler, N. A., et al. (2020)"Having all of your internal resources exhausted beyond measure and being left with no clean-up crew": Defining autistic burnoutAutism in Adulthood, 2(2), 132–143

    From 19 interviews and 19 public online accounts, analyzed with autistic co-researchers, autistic burnout was defined as long-term exhaustion, loss of function and reduced tolerance to stimulus, arising from chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities without enough support.

    ●○○Evidence: Early The first formal definition, from a qualitative community-based study. Distinct from occupational burnout and depression in participants' accounts.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0079

  • Richlan, F., Kronbichler, M., & Wimmer, H. (2009)Functional abnormalities in the dyslexic brain: a quantitative meta-analysis of neuroimaging studiesHuman Brain Mapping, 30(10), 3299–3308

    Pooling 17 brain-activity studies of reading, people with dyslexia showed on average less activity in the left inferior parietal, superior temporal, middle and inferior temporal, and fusiform areas, and in the left inferior frontal gyrus, together with more activity in the primary motor cortex and anterior insula, which may be a way of working around the difficulty.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A pooled analysis of 17 studies, with later reviews pointing the same way. Study sizes were small and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1002/hbm.20752

  • Robertson, C. E., & Baron-Cohen, S. (2017)Sensory perception in autismNature Reviews Neuroscience, 18(11), 671–684

    A review arguing that sensory experience is a central part of autism, noting that unusual sensory experience may affect as many as nine in ten autistic people, across all the senses, and discussing what is known about how sensory processing works differently.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An expert review. The explanations for why are still being tested.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/nrn.2017.112

  • Sasson, N. J., Faso, D. J., Nugent, J., Lovell, S., Kennedy, D. P., & Grossman, R. B. (2017)Neurotypical peers are less willing to interact with those with autism based on thin slice judgmentsScientific Reports, 7, 40700

    Across three studies, non-autistic observers formed less favorable first impressions of autistic children and adults from a few seconds of video, and were less willing to interact with them. The bias disappeared when they judged only transcripts of what was said.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate Three studies with consistent results. Suggests style, not substance, drives the bias.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/srep40700

  • Schmaal, L., Veltman, D. J., van Erp, T. G. M., et al. (2016)Subcortical brain alterations in major depressive disorder: findings from the ENIGMA Major Depressive Disorder working groupMolecular Psychiatry, 21(6), 806–812

    Pooling scans from 1,728 people with major depression and 7,199 comparison people at 15 sites, the hippocampus was smaller on average in the depression group (a very small effect, about 1 percent). The difference was clearest in people with repeated episodes and in those whose depression began before age 21, and not seen in people in their first episode.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong One of the largest pooled scan studies of depression. The effect is small, the groups overlap almost completely, and a scan study cannot say which came first.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/mp.2015.69

  • Schmaal, L., Hibar, D. P., Sämann, P. G., et al. (2017)Cortical abnormalities in adults and adolescents with major depression based on brain scans from 20 cohorts worldwide in the ENIGMA Major Depressive Disorder working groupMolecular Psychiatry, 22(6), 900–909

    In scans of 2,148 people with major depression and 7,957 comparison people, adults with depression had slightly thinner cortex on average in the orbitofrontal cortex, the anterior and posterior cingulate, the insula and the temporal lobes (small effects, about 0.1 of a standard deviation). Adolescents showed a smaller total surface area instead.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A very large pooled scan study. The effects are small and the groups overlap almost completely.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/mp.2016.60

  • Shaw, P., Stringaris, A., Nigg, J., & Leibenluft, E. (2014)Emotion dysregulation in attention deficit hyperactivity disorderAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 171(3), 276-293

    A literature review found that trouble regulating emotions (quick, strong feelings that are hard to bring back down) is common in ADHD across the lifespan, with clinic studies of young people putting it at roughly 25 to 50 percent, and that it adds a lot to the day-to-day difficulty ADHD brings.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A widely cited review. The estimates come mostly from clinic samples, and how emotion dysregulation fits into ADHD is still debated.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13070966

  • Shin, L. M., & Liberzon, I. (2010)The neurocircuitry of fear, stress, and anxiety disordersNeuropsychopharmacology, 35(1), 169–191

    A review of animal and human work on fear circuits. In PTSD, social anxiety and specific phobia the amygdala tends to respond more strongly to cues tied to the fear, and the insula is often more active across several anxiety disorders. In PTSD the rostral anterior cingulate and nearby ventromedial prefrontal cortex tend to respond less. The authors note it is not yet clear which differences come first.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate An expert review that agrees with the pooled analyses. It cannot say what causes what.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1038/npp.2009.83

  • Singh, B., et al. (2023)Effectiveness of physical activity interventions for improving depression, anxiety and distress: an overview of systematic reviewsBritish Journal of Sports Medicine

    An overview of 97 reviews (1,039 trials, about 128,000 people) found physical activity reduced symptoms of depression, anxiety and distress compared with usual care, with medium-sized effects.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong A very large body of trials. The trials vary a lot in quality and type of exercise.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-106195

  • Stoodley, C. J. (2014)Distinct regions of the cerebellum show gray matter decreases in autism, ADHD, and developmental dyslexiaFrontiers in Systems Neuroscience, 8, 92

    Pooling scan studies of autism (17), ADHD (10) and dyslexia (10), no cerebellar area was larger in any group, and each condition had smaller gray matter in a different part of the cerebellum, with no overlap between conditions.

    ●○○Evidence: Early A pooled analysis of mostly small scan studies; the sizes are modest and the groups overlap.

    Read more → doi.org/10.3389/fnsys.2014.00092

  • Temple, E., Deutsch, G. K., Poldrack, R. A., et al. (2003)Neural deficits in children with dyslexia ameliorated by behavioral remediation: evidence from functional MRIProceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 100(5), 2860–2865

    In 20 children with dyslexia aged 8 to 12, a language and listening training program improved reading and language scores, and brain activity during sound-based tasks rose in the left temporoparietal cortex and left inferior frontal gyrus, moving closer to the pattern seen in typical readers.

    ●○○Evidence: Early One small study with no matched untreated group of children with dyslexia. It shows the brain responds to teaching but cannot say how much.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0030098100

  • van Rooij, D., Anagnostou, E., Arango, C., et al. (2018)Cortical and subcortical brain morphometry differences between patients with autism spectrum disorder and healthy individuals across the lifespan: results from the ENIGMA ASD working groupAmerican Journal of Psychiatry, 175(4), 359–369

    Scans of 1,571 autistic people and 1,651 comparison people (ages 2 to 64, 49 sites) showed small average differences: slightly smaller pallidum, putamen, amygdala and nucleus accumbens, slightly thicker frontal cortex and slightly thinner temporal cortex (effect sizes about 0.1 to 0.2 of a standard deviation). The differences in cortical thickness were largest around adolescence.

    ●●●Evidence: Strong The largest scan study of autism to date. The effects are very small, the groups overlap almost completely, and autistic people differ widely from each other.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.17010100

  • Woodward, N. D., Giraldo-Chica, M., Rogers, B., & Cascio, C. J. (2017)Thalamocortical dysconnectivity in autism spectrum disorder: an analysis of the Autism Brain Imaging Data ExchangeBiological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging, 2(1), 76–84

    Comparing 228 autistic people with 228 matched comparison people, the thalamus was more strongly connected on average with prefrontal, temporal and sensory-motor cortex in the autism group, most clearly in adolescents. Other studies have found less connectivity in some thalamus pathways, so the direction is not settled.

    ●○○Evidence: Early One data set, and other studies point in mixed directions.

    Read more → pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

  • Yoo, S.-S., Gujar, N., Hu, P., Jolesz, F. A., & Walker, M. P. (2007)The human emotional brain without sleep—a prefrontal amygdala disconnectCurrent Biology, 17(20), R877–R878

    After a night without sleep, the amygdala reacted more strongly to negative pictures and was less connected to the prefrontal cortex.

    ●●○Evidence: Moderate A small lab study, consistent with later sleep research.

    Read more → doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007
