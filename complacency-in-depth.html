Complacency

Full version← Back to the simple version

A Sunday, nothing wrong

Sam and Jo have been together eight years. Dinner is easy. “Good day?” “Fine. You?” “Fine.” They watch something, they scroll, they go to bed. Nobody is upset, and nothing is broken.

That is exactly why neither of them has noticed that nobody has asked a real question in months. Nothing went wrong on the day the asking stopped. It just stopped.

Most of this program is about what to do when something has gone wrong: the log, the job list, the look-back, the check-in. This page is about the opposite case, the long stretch where nothing seems wrong, and what quietly happens in it. It is for every kind of relationship on this site, and for the one you have with yourself.

The idea is not new here. The creator of this site worked as an auditor of systems, and the cause that came up most often was not fraud or laziness. It was complacency: something had worked for years, so people stopped checking it. The About page tells that story, and its short version is “quiet is not the same as working.” People are not so different from systems on this point: what is never re-checked slowly drifts.

None of this is a charge against anyone. Complacency is what a normal, busy mind does with anything steady. The answer is a better setup, not a worse opinion of yourself or the people you love.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives and Pillar I, See the whole load, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice what you’ve stopped checking, from your habits and health to your own “I’m fine.” Between you and others: build a small, regular re-check into the setup, so what’s good stays seen and thanked.

1. Why good things fade into the background

Minds are built to notice change. Whatever stays the same, even something wonderful, slides out of attention. Psychologists call the general effect adaptation: we get used to new circumstances, good and bad, and they stop registering as much as they did at first. A new home thrills you for a few weeks. A steady partner’s kindness is a delight in year one and furniture by year ten.

That is not a defect. It is how you can function without being amazed by every chair. The cost is that the things you most depend on are the ones you stop seeing. The unpaid, unseen work this program starts with (the Preface) is partly this: the work that always gets done has long since stopped being noticed.

Three ordinary things combine to make it worse:

  • Nothing breaks on the day it starts. A missed question, a skipped thank-you, a step left out once because the day was busy: none of it causes a visible problem, so the shortcut becomes the habit.
  • Checking feels like distrust. “Are we okay?” can sound like doubt, so people don’t ask while things are fine, which is when it is easiest to ask.
  • “It’s fine” is the easiest answer. It ends the conversation without anyone having to look.

Complacency, in short, is what adaptation does when nobody puts anything in its way. A small, regular re-check is that something.

2. The quiet signs

These are signals about the setup, not verdicts on a person. Each one has a usual hidden cause, and a small first move.

One or two of these is an ordinary week. When several have been true for months, that is a pattern worth a gentle, curious look. It is not an emergency, and it is rarely about anyone’s character.

3. How we get complacent: with ourselves and with others

Nobody sets out to do any of this. These are ordinary habits, and most people have a few of each.

With ourselves

  • Letting “I’m fine” stand without checking. The answer comes out before the question has been asked of yourself.
  • Keeping an old picture of yourself. “I’m just not a person who…” was true once, and nobody renewed it.
  • Treating a label as a closed question. “It’s just how I’m wired” can be true of wiring and untrue of a pattern.
  • Skipping the small checks because nothing hurts. Appointments, budgets, sleep, a call to a friend.
  • Letting a good routine run with no review. It worked, so it never gets asked whether it still does.

With others

  • Keeping an old picture of them. You know who they were. People keep changing.
  • Letting someone else do the noticing. The person who always remembers, plans or covers becomes the system, and nobody checks the system.
  • Assuming silence means satisfied. “They’d say if something were wrong” is the most common unspoken rule, and it is often untrue.
  • Letting thanks go generic. Or letting it stop, because the work is reliable.
  • Dropping the small questions. “Fine” ends the conversation, so the real answer never gets said.
  • Not offering your own context. The other person can’t see what you’re carrying, so they fill the gap with a guess, usually a worse one than the truth.

The last item runs the other way too. When neither of you shares what is going on inside, each assumes the other is fine, and both of you drift. That is why the fix is not a stricter routine. It is a regular chance for each of you to give context and receive it, so that you understand each other instead of guessing (more below).

4. In yourself

You are the relationship you have the longest. Complacency with yourself looks like “I’m fine,” “it’s probably nothing,” and “I’ve always been like this,” said so often that you stop hearing them. The question isn’t whether those are wrong. It’s when did I last actually check?

AreaHow it driftsA gentle re-check
Your bodyA niggle explained away for months; routine check-ups skipped because you feel okayBook the appointment you’ve been postponing. This guide isn’t medical advice, and a check-up is not a diagnosis.
Rest and energyRunning on empty becomes “just how I am”Take a reading with Today’s Weather or WP-02, and see what it says over a week
MoneyAutopilot spending and subscriptions; “we’re okay” without looking at the numbersOne quiet hour a month to look at the actual figures, with a partner if you share them
Skills and workCoasting on what you learned years agoAsk what you could do now that you couldn’t a year ago. If nothing, that’s information.
FriendshipsSlowly letting everyone drift because everyone is busyPick the one person you’d miss most and make a small, fixed plan
Your story about yourself“I’m just not a person who…”, a sentence from years ago still runningAsk whose rule that was, and whether it still fits (see below)
Your own patternsSnapping, avoiding or pleasing on repeat, filed under “that’s me”Sort it into wiring, learned pattern or today’s conditions

The last two deserve a note. Know your own wiring separates three layers: wiring, which stays; patterns life taught you, many of which can soften; and today’s conditions, which pass. “I’ve always been this way” is sometimes true of wiring and often untrue of a pattern. Treating a pattern as wiring is a quiet form of complacency, because it closes the question. Where your lens came from is a gentle way to reopen it.

Self-compassion matters here. A common worry is that being kind to yourself will make you complacent. Research on self-compassion suggests the opposite, that people who treat their mistakes kindly tend to be more willing to look at them (see the Library entry). Looking is the whole point. You can only do it from somewhere reasonably safe.

A monthly self re-check, in three lines: What am I assuming is fine? When did I last actually check? What would I say to a friend who told me this about their life?

5. With a partner

Early in a relationship, everything is information. Years in, most of it is assumption, and some assumptions are right. The risk is the long, easy stretch where neither person is giving or asking for anything new.

“Taking for granted” usually begins as a compliment: I trust you. I don’t have to worry about you. You’ve always got it handled. It turns into a problem when the trust goes unthanked and the handling goes unseen. The person doing the reliable work hears silence and concludes it doesn’t matter. The other person hears nothing wrong and concludes all is well. Both are being reasonable, and both are drifting.

Four places it tends to show up between partners:

  • The unseen work. The jobs that always get done stop being counted. Walk back through invisible labor and the mental load, and look at who has been carrying what without a word. “It’s always worked” may mean “one of us always did it.”
  • Curiosity. You stop asking because you think you know. Gottman calls the detailed picture of a partner’s inner world a “love map,” and says the map needs updating because people change. Ten questions to start.
  • Appreciation. Thanks goes generic (“thanks, hon”) or disappears. Specific thanks is how the work gets seen again. Why it works.
  • Shared newness. Researchers who study “self-expansion” found that couples who did new, mildly challenging activities together reported better relationship quality than couples who did pleasant, familiar ones. The effect was modest and the research is still developing, but the idea fits ordinary life: the novelty doesn’t have to be dramatic. A new route, a new café, a class, a project.

None of this means the relationship was in trouble. Most long, happy relationships have all four drifting a little at any given time. The difference is whether anyone looks.

“I realised I haven’t asked you anything I didn’t already know the answer to in a while. What’s on your mind that I don’t know about?”

6. With family and friends

Parents and older relatives

The bond feels permanent, which is the very reason it is easy to put off. Calls get shorter, then rarer, then become the thing you do at holidays. A standing call, even ten minutes at the same time each week, beats an intention to “call more.” Ask about the part of their life you don’t know: a story from before you were born, how they’re really finding things now. If you share care of someone, the guide on caregivers covers keeping that fair.

Children and teenagers

Children change faster than anyone, so the child you’re parenting may be last year’s. Complacency here is “I know my kid,” said about someone who has since changed a lot. Children also give little signals easily lost in a busy day. Turning toward them (the “look at this” moments) is the habit that keeps the picture current. Ask what’s good and what’s hard, and let a teenager not answer. They noticed that you asked.

Friends

Friendships fade by neglect far more often than by falling-out. “We’ll catch up soon” is a wish. A monthly walk, a regular call, or a shared standing thing (a show, a game, a recipe) is a plan. The point isn’t a ledger of who reached out last. It’s that someone keeps the door open, kindly, more than once.

In-laws, siblings and the extended family

Old roles stay frozen: the responsible one, the funny one, the difficult one. Years after everyone changed, the family still treats each person as who they were at fifteen. Ask a sibling or in-law something unrelated to the old role. You may meet someone new.

Grandparents

The page for grandparents covers how to keep a close, current connection with grown children and grandchildren, a place where “I know them” can quietly go out of date across a generation.

7. Homes, teams and the quarterly re-check

Shared setups, such as the chore list, the rent split, the weekly meeting, the way a project is handed over, tend to be agreed once and then run on autopilot. They worked when they were made. People’s hours, energy and circumstances have since changed. And because the setup “works,” no one asks who is quietly making it work.

  • Housemates and roommates. The chore list from last year; the bills split the way they were when one person earned less; one person who always notices the fridge. Re-check on a date, not when someone snaps.
  • Co-parents. The handoff routine that was right for a toddler may be wrong for a teenager. Review it when the child’s age or the schedule changes, not only when there’s a fight.
  • Coworkers and teams. “We’ve always done it this way” and “no news is good news” are the classic signs. The unseen work (follow-ups, reminders, covering) keeps teams running and rarely gets noticed. Use these tools among peers who agree to them, never as a way to rate anyone’s performance.

The quarterly question: every few months, put twenty minutes on the calendar. Ask “Does this still work for everyone?” Start from the facts, using WP-03 to see who owns what and WP-04 to see what keeps coming back. Change one thing. Thank whoever has been covering. A setup you have to re-agree to every so often is a setup that keeps working.

8. Contentment, acceptance and complacency

This site is called Spread Love & Acceptance, so the difference matters. They can look the same from outside, and they feel very different inside.

What it isWhat it sounds like
AcceptanceSeeing someone (or yourself) as they really are, and being kind about it“That’s how you’re wired. I’ll plan around it.”
ContentmentBeing at peace with what’s good, while still paying attention to it“I really like how our mornings go. Let’s keep them.”
ComplacencyNot looking, because it has been fine for a while“It’s fine.” (Said before the question is finished.)

A quick test: contentment can answer “how are you, really?” and enjoys being asked. Complacency can’t, because it hasn’t looked lately. A second test: contentment notices small changes and welcomes them. Complacency is mildly surprised, then defensive, when something shifts.

Acceptance of a person is not a reason to stop caring for the relationship, and it is certainly not a reason to stop saying thank you. You can accept someone completely and still ask them a new question this week.

When “it’s fine” is really keeping the peace

Telling yourself everything is fine can also be a way to stay safe in a relationship where you feel afraid, belittled or controlled, or where speaking up has been punished. That isn’t complacency, and no re-check habit is the answer to it. If this sounds like you, please talk with someone you trust or a qualified professional. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

9. Bringing it up: context on both sides

Raising complacency is delicate, because the word sounds like a charge, and it is easy to hear “you’ve stopped caring.” Two things help. First, you can’t see inside the other person’s week, and they can’t see inside yours, so a good conversation gives context and receives it, on both sides. Second, you are describing a setup that has gone quiet, never a flaw in a person. This is the same ground that Check-ins sets out: you are on the same side.

Before you say anything

  • Check your own part first. Where have you stopped asking, thanking or looking? Starting with your own side lets the other person relax.
  • Check your state. If you are tired, hurt or keeping score, wait. A look at Today’s Weather helps.
  • Pick a time, not a moment. Not in the middle of dinner or the middle of a mood. Ask for ten or twenty minutes at a set time.
  • Choose one thing. One quiet pattern, not a list.

The steps

  1. Name what you noticed, as a fact. “We haven’t had a real talk in a few weeks.” Not “you never ask me anything.”
  2. Give your context. What has been going on for you, including your part. “I’ve been heads-down at work, and I’ve let our evenings slide.”
  3. Ask for theirs. “What’s it been like for you?” Then listen to all of it.
  4. Say it back. “What I heard is that you’ve been feeling like the planning is all yours. Did I get that?” Understanding comes before any answer.
  5. Agree one small change and a date. “Let’s try one real question each, once a week, and look again in a month.”
  6. Thank them for talking. A conversation like this is a good moment to say a specific thank-you.

“Nothing’s wrong. I’ve noticed we haven’t asked each other much lately, and I think part of that is me. Here’s what’s been going on for me. What’s it been like for you?”

Words for different relationships

  • Partner: “I miss being curious about you. I’ve been on autopilot, and I’d like to ask you more. What’s on your mind that I don’t know about?”
  • Parent or older relative: “I realized we mostly talk about logistics. I’d like to hear more about how you’re really doing, and I’ll share more of my own life too.”
  • Adult child or teenager: “I might be picturing you as you were a year ago. I’d like to catch up, and you’re welcome to tell me what I’m missing.”
  • Friend: “It’s been a while, and I realize I’ve let it slide. I’d love to catch up, and I want to hear what’s been going on.”
  • Housemate or coworker: “The way we do this hasn’t changed in a while. Could we check whether it still works for everyone? Here’s how it looks from my side, and I’d like to hear yours.”

If someone raises it with you

It can sting to hear that you have been taken for granted, or have been taking someone for granted. A few things make it easier:

  • Thank them for saying it. It took some courage, and it is a sign they still care about this.
  • Don’t defend yet. Ask for their context first: “Can you tell me what it’s been like?” Defending before you have heard them is how a quiet problem becomes a loud one.
  • Say back what you heard, then share your own context, briefly and without using it as an excuse. “That’s true. I’ve been stretched thin, and I stopped checking in. I’d like to fix that.”
  • Offer one small change, not a promise to be a different person. Small and kept beats large and dropped.

When you each see it differently

It is possible that one of you sees a lot of drift and the other sees very little. That is information, not a stalemate. Each view comes with its own context: how much one person is carrying, what the other is used to, what each takes “fine” to mean. Spend a few minutes on that before trying to settle who is right. The Signal Translator can test your opening sentence, and the Wiring Card helps if you take in words differently. If the conversation heats up, use the pause from Check-ins.

A caution: raising complacency is for relationships that are basically safe. If speaking up has been met with anger, punishment or fear, see the note under contentment, and talk with someone you trust first.

10. The re-check: small, regular, and kind

Where audits are done well, nothing is trusted just because it has always worked. Each control is tested again on a schedule, whether or not anyone suspects a problem, and the test is routine rather than an accusation. The same idea works for people, with one important difference: a re-check between people should be warm, light and short.

A re-check is a look, not a trial. It is a question and a thank-you, not a case against anyone. If it starts to feel like surveillance, or you need reassurance over and over, it has stopped doing its job. Go back to something smaller and kinder.

How oftenWhat it isWhere it already lives
DailyNinety seconds: how are you, really, and one thing you appreciatedWP-13, the 90-second daily check-in
WeeklyOne specific thank-you and one question you don’t know the answer toTurning Toward
MonthlyA calm look back for what the weekly check-ins missChapter V, the monthly look-back, and WP-04
Quarterly“Does this still work for everyone?” for the shared setupWP-03, one owner per job
YearlyA longer “where are we, and what do we want next year?”The Workpaper Suite

Three rules keep it working:

  1. Small enough to survive a bad week. A re-check that needs a good mood won’t happen.
  2. Fixed to a day or an event. “Sunday walk” survives. “Sometime this month” doesn’t.
  3. One change at a time. A re-check that produces a list of fifteen improvements gets abandoned. Pick one.

11. Touchstones: the words that carry context

A touchstone is a small piece of shared language or meaning that reminds two people who they are together. It might be an inside joke, a pet name, a catchphrase, the way you say hello and goodbye, a line from a film, a song, a place, or a story you retell. Researchers who study couples call some of these personal idioms: private words and phrases that outsiders wouldn’t understand. In several studies, couples who use more of them report being more satisfied. That is a link, not proof of cause, but it fits what most people feel: shared words are a quick way of saying “you and I have a history.”

Touchstones matter for this page for two reasons. They are among the first things to go stale when attention fades, and they are also one of the quickest ways to bring it back.

Kinds of touchstone

KindExamplesHow it goes stale
Words and namesPet names, nicknames, catchphrases, inside jokes, a family word for somethingThe name stops being used, or the joke is repeated by rote without a smile
Hello and goodbye“Love you,” “safe travels,” “text me when you’re there”Said on autopilot, from another room, without looking up
StoriesHow you met, “remember when…,” the family story everyone tellsNobody has told it, or asked for it, in years
Songs, shows, places“Our” song, a café, a trail, a standing showYou stop going, or go on your own
ObjectsA photo, a mug, a note on the fridgeIt becomes furniture and nobody sees it
RitualsA Sunday walk, a Friday call (see Turning Toward)They depended on mood or free time, not on a plan
Words for hard moments“Weather check?”, “pause, back at eight,” a safe word for a breakNever agreed in the first place, so it’s missing when needed

How to refresh one

  • Retell the story. Ask how you met, or how a joke began, and let each person tell it from their side. You will often learn something. That is context on both sides.
  • Say the old words on purpose. Use a pet name or phrase you’ve dropped, and see if it still lands. If it doesn’t, ask what has changed.
  • Change a hello or goodbye. Look up, stop what you’re doing, and say it as if you mean it. Small, and it works the same day.
  • Make a new one. A phrase from last month’s trip or a joke from this week is a touchstone in the making. New ones count as much as old ones.
  • Ask what a phrase means now. “What does ‘fine’ mean when you say it?” can open a better conversation than a direct question.

Agree words for the hard moments

The most useful touchstones are plain words you agree on in advance, so neither of you has to invent them mid-argument. They let you share a little context fast, and they keep a hard moment from being read as a verdict:

  • A weather word. “My sky is clear, gusty or fogged in.” The Check-ins card uses this. One word tells the other person what you’re carrying without a speech.
  • A pause word. “Pause, back at eight.” Either person can use it, and it always includes a time to return (see the pause).
  • A check-in word. “Weather check?” as a light way to ask how the other person is really doing, without the weight of “can we talk?”
  • A thanks word. A short phrase you only say when you really mean a thank-you.

With yourself

You can have touchstones with yourself too: a phrase, a song, an object or a place that reminds you who you want to be and checks you in. “I’m fine” as a touchstone is a trap. “What do I actually need?” is a better one.

Cautions

  • Retire the ones that hurt. A nickname someone dislikes, a joke at their expense, an old “you always…” line. A touchstone only works if both people feel warm about it. Ask, and be ready to drop it.
  • Don’t assume the words are shared. People take words differently. Someone who hears words literally may find an inside joke confusing or may want the plain version. Families and cultures have different words for closeness. The Wiring Card and Wired Differently help you agree what words should mean.
  • Don’t use them as a shortcut around a real talk. A pet name doesn’t replace a conversation that needs to happen. Warm words and a real answer belong together.

“Remember how we always said ___? I realized we haven’t said it in ages. What did that mean to you?”

12. Questions that wake things up

Use a few, now and then, not all at once. For more, see the ten love-map questions.

For yourself

  1. What am I assuming is fine that I haven’t looked at lately?
  2. What did I stop doing that I used to enjoy, and did I decide to?
  3. Where am I coasting?
  4. Which rule about myself is older than I am willing to admit?
  5. Who would I be sorry to lose touch with?
  6. What would I change if I knew nobody would mind?

For a partner

  1. What’s something I do that you’d miss if I stopped?
  2. What’s something you’d like more of that I haven’t noticed?
  3. What are you looking forward to this year?
  4. What’s something new you’d like us to try, even a small one?
  5. Is there anything you’ve stopped mentioning because it didn’t seem worth it?
  6. What did we used to do that you miss?
  7. Is there a phrase, joke or name of ours you’d like to bring back?
  8. What’s a word you’d like us to use when one of us needs a pause?

For family and friends

  1. What’s a part of your life I don’t know much about?
  2. What’s been the best and the hardest part of this year?
  3. What’s something you wish people asked you more?
  4. Is there a way I could be more in touch that would actually suit you?

For a household or team

  1. Does this still work for everyone?
  2. Who is quietly covering for a gap?
  3. What are we doing only because we’ve always done it?
  4. What’s one thing that would make next month easier?

13. Words you could use

Re-checking only lands if it doesn’t sound like a charge. These are starting points. Say them your own way.

To a partner: “Nothing’s wrong. I just realised I haven’t asked you anything real in a while, and I miss it. What’s on your mind?”

To a friend or relative: “I keep saying we should catch up, so let’s pick a day. Does the first Sunday of the month work for a call?”

To a household: “Let’s do a quick check on the chore list. It’s been a year. What still works and what doesn’t?”

To a teenager: “I know I’m still picturing you as you were last year. What’s something about your life now that I should know?”

To yourself: “I’ve been saying ‘I’m fine’ for a while. Am I?”

The Signal Translator can test any of these before you send them, and the Wiring Card helps if one of you takes in words differently.

14. If you’re the one being taken for granted

If you do much of the unseen work and feel it isn’t noticed, three things are worth knowing.

  • It’s probably not malice. Reliable work becomes invisible, which is the most ordinary thing in the world. That doesn’t make it okay. It makes it fixable.
  • Be specific, once, kindly. Hints and sighs are easy to miss. “When the forms get done, I’d like a thank-you now and then. It would help” is easier to act on than months of quiet resentment. A check-in gives you a calm place to say it.
  • Make the work visible. The log in WP-01 records who did what for a week. The numbers say what the mood can’t.

It isn’t your job to carry the whole re-check alone. If you raise it clearly and kindly, more than once, and nothing changes, that is a different conversation, worth having with a counselor or someone you trust.

15. When it’s more than complacency

Sometimes “we’ve drifted” covers something heavier. Flatness, withdrawal, short temper and “nothing feels like anything” can be signs of burnout, depression, grief, resentment or a relationship that has really run its course. A re-check habit won’t fix those, and shouldn’t be asked to.

  • If you’ve felt flat or hopeless for more than a couple of weeks, please speak to a doctor or a qualified mental health professional.
  • If one of you has stopped caring about the relationship, or the distance is wide, a couples or family counselor can help far more than a guide.
  • If you feel unsafe, see the note under contentment above.

This page can’t diagnose anything, and it doesn’t try to. It names a common, quiet pattern, and offers small habits that help most when things are basically okay.

16. Where this comes from

  • Adaptation. Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell described the “hedonic treadmill” in 1971, the tendency to return to a baseline as circumstances become familiar. Sonja Lyubomirsky’s 2011 review, “Hedonic adaptation to positive and negative experiences,” covers the evidence that people adapt to good things more readily than to bad ones.
  • Everyday bids, thanks and love maps. John Gottman and colleagues on turning toward, and on updating a partner’s inner world. Sara Algoe’s “find, remind and bind” account of gratitude. Both are covered in Turning Toward in depth.
  • Shared novelty. Arthur Aron and colleagues’ work on self-expansion, including a 2000 study of couples’ shared participation in novel and arousing activities. Effects are modest, and the field is still developing.
  • Shared language. Hopper, Knapp and Scott (1981) on couples’ personal idioms, and Bruess and Pearson (1993) on idiom use and marital satisfaction over the life cycle. Findings link more shared idioms with higher satisfaction, but these studies are correlational. Fiese’s work on family rituals and symbolic meaning (see the Library entry) points the same way.
  • Self-compassion and motivation. Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012). See the Library entry.
  • Re-testing what works. From the creator’s audit work: a control that isn’t re-tested drifts. See About.

Research on relationships describes averages, not your relationship. Use it as a prompt for a conversation, not as a verdict.

What this page can’t do

It can’t tell you whether a relationship is working. It can’t replace a doctor, a counselor or a mediator, and it scores no one. It names a quiet pattern and gives you a few small, kind habits to push back on it.

Try this: Pick one relationship, or yourself. Ask one question from the list above that you don’t know the answer to. Then put a small re-check in your calendar for a month from now.
