Touchstones: your shared language

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full guide

A touchstone is a small piece of shared language that says “we’re us”: a nickname, an inside joke, the way you say goodnight, a line from a movie, a song, a story you always retell. This guide treats them like the love languages: a menu to notice, keep fresh and make together, not a label or a test.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice which small words make you feel at home. Between you and others: keep them fresh and invite each other in.

Sunday, 8 a.m.

Mara and Theo have a word for the way the kettle whistles: “the little train.” Years ago it made them both laugh in a rented kitchen. Lately they still say it. Nobody laughs. The word is still there; the warmth has quietly gone out of it.

An honest word about the evidence

People have studied the private words couples use. One study of married couples found that those who used more of their own idioms and pet names tended to report being more satisfied (Bruess and Pearson, 1993). That is a link, not proof that words cause happiness, and the study was small. Rituals of connection and shared humor also show up in the research on close relationships. So treat touchstones as one gentle thing worth noticing, not a formula.

  1. Words: names, phrases and the way you say hello.

    Pet names, a family phrase, “ready when you are,” the line you always say at the door. A few words carry a whole history.

    Healthy

    • A name or phrase that both people like.
    • Said on purpose, with a smile or a look.
    • Allowed to change as you do.

    When it goes sideways

    • A name one person quietly dislikes.
    • A phrase said on autopilot, with no one home.
    • Used to tease in front of others, or in a fight.

    Try: “Do you still like it when I call you that? Or would you like a new one?”

  2. Jokes: the funny thing only you two get.

    An inside joke is a little door that only you can open. Telling the story of how it started is often as good as the joke.

    Healthy

    • A joke that includes everyone in the room, or is easy to explain.
    • Retelling where it came from.
    • Making a new one this week.

    When it goes sideways

    • A joke at someone’s expense that is called “just a joke.”
    • A joke that keeps others out on purpose.
    • Using it to dodge a real talk.

    Try: “Remember when…? I still laugh about that.”

    Dig deeper: jokes that bring people closer

  3. Rituals: the small things you always do.

    Tea on Sunday, the goodbye wave from the window, the Friday takeaway. A ritual is a touchstone you do instead of say.

    Healthy

    • Small, regular and easy to keep.
    • Both people would miss it.
    • Can bend on a hard week.

    When it goes sideways

    • A ritual kept up out of duty, with resentment.
    • One person always organising it.
    • Treated as proof of love, so a missed one becomes a fight.

    Try: “Which of our little habits do you like best? Let’s keep that one.”

    Dig deeper: rituals big and small

  4. Stories: the ones you tell again.

    How you met, the worst holiday, the day the dog ate the cake. Retelling a shared story reminds you who you are together.

    Healthy

    • Telling it with the other person, not over them.
    • Letting each person tell their own part.
    • Adding a new chapter now and then.

    When it goes sideways

    • Always the same version, told as a performance.
    • A story told to embarrass.
    • A story about a hard time retold to win a point.

    Try: “Tell me the story of the first time we…”

    Dig deeper: stories, and how to keep them kind

  5. Songs, places and things.

    A song that was on in the car, a bench by the river, a chipped mug. They hold a feeling so you don’t have to explain it.

    Healthy

    • Choosing one on purpose, and saying why.
    • Visiting or playing it now and then.
    • Letting a new one join the old ones.

    When it goes sideways

    • Treating an object as more important than the person.
    • Keeping a place or song tied to a painful memory in the mix without asking.
    • Expecting the other person to feel the same way about it.

    Try: “Can we put our song on while we cook tonight?”

    Dig deeper: songs, places and things

  6. How to practice: notice, refresh, make one.

    1. Notice. Each of you privately lists three small words, jokes or habits that feel like “us.” Real ones, not ideal ones.
    2. Share. Take turns. Listen to why each one matters. You may find yours are different.
    3. Check. Ask of each: “Do we still like this?” Keep, refresh or retire it, kindly.
    4. Make one. Make up one new touchstone this week: a name for something, a silly rule, a Friday habit.
    5. Review. In your ten-minute weekly review: “Which small thing felt most like us this week?”

How it ties together

Touchstones are one part of a bigger set of “languages.” Love languages are how you show care. Apology languages are how you repair. Touchstones are the words and habits you make together. See how they all fit together, and what to reach for when.
