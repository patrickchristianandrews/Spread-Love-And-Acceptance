Family and money

Supporting family: parents, money home and duty

In many families around the world, grown children are expected to look after their parents and help the wider family: sending money home, paying a cousin’s school fees, hosting relatives for weeks, giving elders a real say. For many people this is not a habit or a choice. It is a duty, and often a loving one. This page is about planning around it together, so it doesn’t turn into a fight about who is right.

A real obligation, not a spending habit

If one of you sends money to your parents every month, that is not the same as eating out too often. It is closer to rent: a commitment that was there before the relationship, and that will keep going. Treating it as extra spending makes the partner who sends it feel their family is being judged. Treating it as something that can never be discussed leaves the other partner shut out of a big part of the household’s money.

The middle way is to treat it as a real, planned line in your shared budget, agreed by both of you.

Researchers sometimes call this sense of duty “filial obligation.” How strong it is varies a lot between cultures and between families. It is often tied to closeness and pride, and it can also bring strain when money is tight. Most research on couples and money comes from Western countries, where sending money to parents is less common, so it says little about how to do this well. What follows is practical advice, not research findings.

Agree a monthly family-support amount

This site’s idea

  1. Write down what goes to family now. Regular transfers, one-off help, school fees, medical bills, gifts for visits, travel home and the cost of hosting. Include both families. Many couples are surprised by the real total, in either direction.
  2. Agree a monthly amount, as its own line. It sits next to rent, food and savings, not inside “spending.” You both know the number.
  3. Agree what happens in an emergency. Families have hospital bills and funerals. Agree an emergency amount, or a simple rule: “Above this much, we talk first, and we decide within a day.”
  4. Review it at set times. Once or twice a year, or when your income changes. Not every time money goes out.

Some couples also agree that each partner has some personal money that is theirs to spend or send without asking. That can sit well alongside a shared family line.

Words you could use

If you support your family: “Helping my parents isn’t optional for me. I’d like us to agree a monthly amount together, so it’s planned, not a surprise every month.”

If it’s your partner’s family: “I want to support your family. I’d feel better if we knew the number and planned for it. Can we write it down together?”

Try it now: put your family-support amount and your savings in Who does what?, as amounts you agree, never as a debt between you.

Each partner leads with their own family

It usually goes best when each of you talks to your own family about money, visits and limits. You know the language, the history, and how to say no without giving offense. Your partner backs you up in private, and doesn’t have to be the one who says no to your mother.

  • Agree your position together first, in private.
  • Then the partner whose family it is speaks for you both: “We’ve decided,” not “She won’t let me.”
  • Don’t make your partner the excuse, and don’t leave them to face your family alone.

When one of you grew up with it and the other didn’t

This is where a lot of hurt comes from. To the partner who grew up with it, helping family is simply what a good son or daughter does. To the partner who didn’t, money going out every month can feel like a hole in your plans, or like the other family comes first. Each of you is reacting to the way you were raised. Neither of you is greedy, and neither of you is foolish.

  • Tell each other the story. What did your parents do for their parents? What happened in your family when someone didn’t help? What would it mean to you if you stopped?
  • Ask before you argue. “What does sending money mean to you?” is a better start than “How long will this go on?”
  • Name the value under the worry. Often one of you is protecting family and the other is protecting your future. You can plan for both.

Words you could use

If you grew up with it: “When I send money home, I’m not choosing them over us. It’s how I was raised to show love and respect. I’d like you to understand it, not just put up with it.”

If you didn’t: “I don’t want you to stop. I get scared when I can’t see where our money is going. Can we put it on paper together?”

Saving goals alongside it, and the partner who worries

Supporting family and saving for your own future don’t have to compete. They both just need a place in the plan.

  • Set aside the agreed amounts first. Family support and savings both go out at the start of the month, then everything else.
  • Name the goals you are saving for: an emergency fund, a home, your children’s schooling, retirement. A goal with a name feels less like money taken away.
  • If money gets tight, adjust both lines together, rather than one of you cutting the other’s.

If you are the one who worries about savings, your worry is real and worth saying. It can help to ask for something specific, like three months of costs put aside for emergencies, rather than a general “we send too much.” If you are the one who sends money, take the worry seriously too: “Let’s look at the numbers together” calms more than “It’s fine.”

A calm money talk at a set time works better than one set off by a surprise transfer. Check-ins show a simple way to have one. If you share bills, the money tab in Who does what? shows who pays which shared cost.

Words you could use

If you worry about savings: “I’d feel a lot calmer with three months of costs saved. Could we build that up alongside what we send to your parents, not in place of it?”

If you send money home: “I hear that you’re worried. Let’s sit down on Sunday with the numbers and see what we can do on both sides.”

Visits, long stays and the role of elders

Supporting family isn’t only about money. It can mean a parent moving in, relatives staying for months, or elders having a real say in big decisions. Plan these the same way: agree together first, then each of you leads with your own family.

  • Talk about visits before dates are set. How long, where people sleep, who cooks, and what happens to your usual routines.
  • Share the extra work. Hosting is real work: meals, cleaning, driving, translating, keeping the peace. Put it on the list and give each job one owner. One owner per job helps.
  • Agree how elders’ advice is used. Listening with respect is not the same as handing over the decision. Decide together what you will ask them about, and what the two of you will decide.
  • Keep some time as a couple during long stays, even a walk on your own each evening.

Words you could use

If it’s your family: “My parents would like to come for three months. Before I say yes, can we talk about what that would mean for you?”

If it’s your partner’s family: “I want your parents to feel welcome. Can we agree how long, what help we’d like, and how we each get a little time to ourselves?”

If an elder has a say in a decision: “I’ll tell my mother we’ve chosen the school. I’ll make it clear we decided together.”

This works for faith and naming decisions too. Agree together first, then each of you leads with your own family. Two faiths or cultures, one family has more on holidays, ceremonies and choosing a child’s name.

Talking about it: between you, and with family

These talks are hard because love, duty and money are tangled together. A partner’s question about money can sound like criticism of your parents. A request from home can come by phone, in another language, from an elder you were raised never to refuse.

  • Don’t decide on the call. When a request comes, say you’ll talk with your partner and call back.
  • Listen for the value, not just the number. Ask what this help means to your partner, or to your family.
  • Be respectful, and still clear. You can honor an elder and still say “not this month.”
  • Keep your partner out of the firing line. Say “we decided,” never “she won’t let me.”
  • Talk money at a set time, not straight after a transfer or a phone call home.

Words you could use

To family: “I want to help, and I need to talk with my partner first. I’ll call you back tomorrow.”

To your partner: “My mother called about the roof. I didn’t promise anything. Can we look at it together tonight?”
