At work: fair workloads, clear messages, real appreciation

Most friction at work is not about people being difficult. It comes from jobs nobody clearly owns, messages that land harder than they were meant, and thanks that doesn’t fit. This page gathers simple, free tools for all three. It is written so a team lead or HR manager can share it with a team as it is.

Fair workloads

Every team runs on work that never appears on a project plan: sending the follow-up after a meeting, keeping the notes, booking the room, chasing the missing figure, remembering the deadline nobody wrote down, welcoming the new starter. This is coordination work. It keeps everything moving, and it is easy to miss for three reasons.

  • It is invisible when it works. Nobody notices the reminder that went out. They only notice when it doesn’t.
  • It drifts to whoever is reliable. The person who did it once becomes the person who always does it, without anyone deciding that.
  • It rarely shows up in a review. Over time the person carrying it does more and gets less credit, and resentment builds quietly.

When a job belongs to “everyone,” it often ends up belonging to no one, or to the same one person every time. The fix is not to blame anyone. It is to write the recurring jobs down and give each one a single owner, with a backup for holidays and busy weeks. Fix the setup, not the person.

    Team check-in 10 minutes

    Once a week, go round once. Each person says how full their week is, one thank-you, and one thing that slipped. Then name any job that needs a new owner. No debating, and nobody is rated: a full week is useful information, not a complaint. Print it, or keep it on a shared screen.

    Messages that land

    At work, a short message carries more than its words. Pay, reviews and reputation sit underneath it, so a quick line can read as a criticism, an order or a hidden note about someone’s competence, even when it wasn’t meant that way. A little extra clarity costs a few seconds and saves a lot of second-guessing.

    The Signal Translator is a free tool for checking a message before you send it. Type the sentence (this link opens it on the work-message setting), and it shows how it may land and offers a clearer, kinder way to say it. What you type stays on your device.

    Three before-and-after examples

    Before “Per my last email, the figures are attached.”

    After “Sending the figures again here in case the first email got buried. The totals are on page 2.”

    Why: “per my last email” is often read as “you didn’t read it.” The new version gives the same information without the sting.

    Before “We need to talk about your performance.”

    After “Could we meet Thursday at 2 for twenty minutes? The last two client reports went out a few days late, and I’d like to work out together what would help.”

    Why: a vague, heavy opener leaves someone worrying for days. Being specific about the topic, the time and the aim is kinder, not harsher.

    Before “Just circling back on this.”

    After “Could you send the signed contract by Friday at noon? If that date doesn’t work, let me know what would.”

    Why: “circling back” hints that something is late without saying what is needed. A clear ask with a date is easier to answer.

    Fact, impact, request

    Elsewhere on this site, a hard message has three parts: a fact, a feeling and an ask. At work, the same shape becomes fact, impact, request.

    1. Fact. What happened, in words a camera could record. No labels like “careless” or “always.”
    2. Impact. What it meant for the work, the customer or the team. You can say how it affected you, briefly: “I was caught off guard.”
    3. Request. One clear, small ask, with an owner and, if it helps, a date.

    For example

    “The report went out on Monday without the March figures. The client asked about them on the call and I didn’t have an answer. Could we add a check of the figures to Friday’s list, and could you own that check?”

    Appreciation that counts

    People like to be thanked in different ways at work. One person loves public praise in the team meeting; the person at the next desk would much rather have a quiet note. Appreciation at work walks through five kinds: specific words, real attention, practical help, a small thoughtful token, and (for those who welcome it) a handshake or a high-five.

    An honest word about the evidence: the “five languages of appreciation” idea is popular in workplaces but has been tested very little. The sturdier finding is simpler. People tend to value appreciation that is specific, timely, sincere and given in a way that fits them. So use the five as prompts for a question, not as labels for coworkers.

    • Ask. “When something goes well, how do you like to be thanked: in public, or one-to-one?”
    • Be specific. “Thank you for catching that error. It saved us a day.”
    • Check who you’ve missed. Once a month: “Whom have I not thanked in a while?” The coordination work above is often the first thing to go unthanked.

    Thanks never replaces fair pay or a fair workload. Appreciation that goes with fair treatment feels real. Appreciation used in place of it, or to avoid a harder conversation about pay, workload or boundaries, tends to feel hollow.

    A 45-minute team session

    An outline for a team of about three to ten people, adapted from the site’s guide for leading a group. You don’t need a facilitator. Anyone on the team can host it. Bring this page on a screen, or print the one-owner list above.

    Ground rules read aloud at the start

    • No one has to share. Listening is taking part, and “pass” is a full answer.
    • Talk about the work, not personalities. Jobs, handoffs and messages, not who someone is.
    • No one is rated. Nothing said here goes into a review, a file or a scorecard.

    Opener 5 minutes

    Go round the table: “Name one small thing someone on this team did recently that made your week easier.” Anyone can pass.

    The one-owner list 15 minutes

    Together, list the recurring jobs that keep the team running, especially the invisible ones: notes, follow-ups, onboarding, the shared inbox, the rota. For each, agree one owner and one backup. If a job has no volunteer, give it a rotation. Use the team one-owner list on a shared screen.

    Messages that land 15 minutes

    In pairs, each person brings one ordinary, low-stakes message: a request, a reminder, a handover. Rewrite it using fact, impact, request, or run it through the Signal Translator. Swap and ask: “How does this land for you?” Keep examples general. Nobody brings a real complaint about a colleague.

    Agreements 10 minutes

    Agree two or three small things to try for the next month, such as “every meeting ends with one owner per action” or “deadlines go in the first line of the message.” Write them down and set a date to look back.

    Keep it voluntary, and keep it separate from performance. This is a session about how the team works, not about how well any one person works. Never use what people say or write here to evaluate anyone. If an issue with one person comes up, take it to a private conversation, not the group.

    Better one-to-ones

    A regular one-to-one between a manager and a team member works best as a short, calm talk, not a status report. The site’s check-in idea fits it well.

    • A set time. Same day, same time, kept. Moving it once is fine; cancelling it again and again tells someone they’re not a priority.
    • One topic. Pick the one thing that matters most this week. If another comes up, write it down for next time.
    • Hear it back before you answer. One person says what happened and how it landed. The other says back what they heard and asks, “Did I get that?” Solutions wait until both have been heard.

    Words you could use

    “What’s the one thing that would make next week easier?”

    “Let me check I’ve understood: the handover from sales is arriving without the contract details, and you’re losing a day chasing them. Did I get that?”

    “I don’t have an answer yet. I’ll come back to you by Wednesday.”

    ADHD and neurodiversity at work

    People focus and take in asks in different ways. One person catches every word in a meeting; another needs it written down; someone else loses a spoken ask by the time they’re back at their desk, however much they care. That is wiring, not effort, and a few small habits help everyone, not only the people who mention it.

    • Written asks after meetings. One line per action: what, who owns it, and by when. Send it the same day, so nobody has to rely on memory.
    • One ask per message. Put the deadline in the first line, and say plainly if something is urgent or can wait.
    • Protect focus time. Agree blocks with no meetings and no pings, and one clear way for a real emergency to reach someone.
    • Ask how people work best, not what’s wrong. “Do you prefer asks in writing or out loud?” works for everyone.
    • Share how you’re wired, if you want to. The Wiring Card makes a short card about how you focus, take in asks and like feedback. Its team version, “Copy as a team snippet”, drops each person’s card into a team wiki or channel.

    Disclosure is a personal choice. Nobody has to share a diagnosis, or explain why a habit helps them. Offer the helpful habits to the whole team, so nobody has to ask. If someone does tell you, keep it to yourself unless they say otherwise.
