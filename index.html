Front Matter

Preface: Unbilled Debt

Every household runs a set of books nobody agreed to keep.

Someone notices the milk is low. Someone remembers the dentist appointment three weeks out. Someone feels the temperature of the room change before an argument starts and quietly redirects it. None of this shows up on a calendar, and none of it gets a thank-you — thank-yous require the work to be visible first, and this work is built to be invisible.

Over months and years, that invisible work accumulates like a debt only one party can see on their books. This book calls that gap between labor performed and labor acknowledged unbilled debt.

This isn't a book about keeping score to win an argument. A ledger only one person gets to read isn't a ledger — it's a grudge with better formatting. Writing things down here means making the invisible column visible to both people, in the same units, at the same time, so the conversation starts from shared facts instead of competing impressions.

What follows borrows vocabulary from accounting, systems engineering, and nervous-system science on purpose: these fields are good at describing a system's actual state without moralizing about who's to blame for it. Read the chapters, use the tools, and close the books together at the end of each week.

Chapter I

The Radio Frequency Paradigm

A model, not a measurement — nothing here records or analyzes anyone's actual voice.

Tune two radios to frequencies that are close but not identical, and you get a squeal: the audible beat created by two signals that are almost, but not quite, in sync. This book borrows that image for a familiar moment — two people each saying something reasonable, in a tone shaped by their own fatigue or urgency, producing a fight neither of them intended to start.

Three things tend to shift someone's "transmission frequency" without their noticing: pace (mobilized people talk faster and read pauses as stonewalling), register (a status update can transmit as an accusation), and urgency (two people's "when you get a chance" can mean very different windows of time).

Before continuing a conversation that's started to squeal: name the mismatch out loud without blame, check your own state, slow the pace deliberately, and only then return to the actual content of the conversation. The paradigm isn't a diagnosis of who's transmitting badly — it's a shared vocabulary for the moment when both of you are just out of sync.

Chapter II

The Epistemic Verdict Engine & P(Solvency)

A heuristic worksheet, not a scientific instrument — it scores the workload arrangement, never either person.

Every long-running household eventually gets asked, in one form or another: "is this actually working?" Usually the question gets answered with whichever incident happened most recently. The Verdict Engine asks a narrower, calmer question instead: based on what's actually logged this week — hours in the ledger, tasks with a named owner, self-reported nervous-system load — does the current workload arrangement look sustainable, or is it quietly running a deficit?

P(Solvency) = (workload_balance × 0.40) + (ownership_clarity × 0.35) + ((1 − autonomic_saturation) × 0.25)

Above roughly 0.70, the current arrangement is carrying its own weight. Between 0.40 and 0.70, something is drifting — usually ownership clarity first. Below 0.40, the workload as structured isn't sustainable — a statement about the arrangement, not about either person's love, effort, or worth.

Tool

The Lemonade Stand Heuristic

A thought experiment about why shared visibility in work accounting matters.

Imagine two people running a lemonade stand. One makes the lemonade. One sells it. Both are necessary work. At the end of the day, one person opens the register and counts the money. If only that person gets to see the ledger, the other person can't tell whether they're carrying an unfair share or whether the business is actually balanced.

Writing the numbers down together, in the same units, at the same time—that's when honest conversation becomes possible. A household works the same way. Someone cooks. Someone remembers birthdays. Someone fixes things. Someone carries emotional labor nobody names. If only one person keeps the books, resentment is the only possible outcome. If both people can see the same page, fairness becomes something you can actually talk about.
