Where your lens came from: how growing up shapes the way you see yourself, others and what you expect of them

Every stage of growing up asks a big question, and the answer we land on can become an understanding many people carry forward and keep using as adults. Most of those understandings serve us well. Some were right for the child we were and quietly out of date for the adult we are now.

Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed and the kitchen is a mess. Before you’ve had a single thought, you feel it: a hot, tight “Of course. It’s always me.” Nobody said that. Your partner may not even know the dishes are there. But in the house you grew up in, the person who noticed the mess was the person who cleaned it, and anyone who rested while there was work to do was lazy. That rule comes from your childhood, and it just made a decision for you.

We like to think we see the world as it is. Mostly, we see it through a lens we started grinding very early, long before we could choose. Family, school, friends, culture, faith communities, the money situation at home, the shows we watched and, more and more, the screens in our hands all taught us what to expect: of ourselves, of other people, and of how a “good” partner, parent, friend or coworker behaves. By adulthood, that lens feels less like a lens and more like simple reality.

This guide is about taking the lens off for a moment and looking at it. Where did each part come from? How old were you? Is it still true here, with these people, now? You can’t choose what shaped you. You can choose, a little at a time, which understandings you keep using.

Where this fits: This page mainly uses Pillar III, Read your state first (is this feeling the size of the moment, or older?) and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives (what we were rewarded for as children keeps pulling on us), with help from Pillar I, See the whole load, Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, and Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. In you: notice when an old understanding is doing the thinking. Between you and others: say out loud the rules you each brought from home, and choose your own together.

An honest note before you read on

This page is general education about how people grow, not a diagnosis, therapy or treatment, and it can’t explain any one person’s life. Stage models are maps, not measurements: real childhoods are messier, cultures differ, and people move through these questions at their own pace, often more than once. Nothing here is about blame. Many of the adults who raised us were working with their own lenses too. Not every home felt calm or kind, though, and this page doesn’t ask you to decide that yours did. If you’d like to explore your own story more deeply, a qualified professional can help, if and when you want that.

The lens idea: understandings we carry forward

WHAT WE LEARN, WE KEEP USING

A child can’t look everything up. So a growing mind does something clever: it builds working answers from what keeps happening around it, and then uses those answers to predict what will happen next. “When I cry, someone comes.” “Mistakes get laughed at.” “Dad handles money; Mom handles feelings.” “People like me when I’m helpful.” Psychologists have different names for these working answers (John Bowlby called the ones about closeness internal working models; Aaron Beck and later Jeffrey Young wrote about core beliefs and schemas), but the everyday idea is the same.

These understandings are efficient. They let us act fast without thinking everything through. That’s exactly why they’re so easy to miss as adults: they don’t feel like beliefs. They feel like noticing. You don’t think “I believe rest must be earned.” You just feel uneasy sitting down while someone else is busy.

About yourself

What you’re worth, what you’re good at, how much space your needs may take up, and what happens when you get it wrong.

About other people

Whether people are reliable, what silence or a certain tone means, and why people do what they do.

The unwritten rules for how a partner, parent, friend, roommate or coworker should act, often never said out loud.

The stages: a big question at each age

A MAP, NOT A MEASUREMENT

Psychologist Erik Erikson described human development as eight stages across the whole life, each built around a central question. His model has been widely influential, though debated: later research has refined and argued with the details, there is limited direct evidence for the stages themselves, and ages vary a lot from person to person. The part most researchers accept is that development continues across the whole life. The useful part for everyday life is simpler: at different ages we are often especially busy with different questions, and the answer we reach can become a lasting part of how we see things.

These questions don’t get answered once and filed away. We return to them, especially at big transitions: a new relationship, a move, becoming a parent, a new job, a loss of routine. Each return is a chance to answer a little differently.

Why timing matters: the same event lands differently at different ages

WHEN IT HAPPENED CHANGES HOW DEEP IT GOES

Here is the heart of it. Something that happens while you’re busy answering a particular question tends to become part of the answer. The same event can leave a light mark at one age and a deep groove at another.

  • A family move at 4 may mostly be about routines and the people who stayed close. A move at 14, right in the middle of “Where do I belong?”, can leave a longer-lasting feeling of being the outsider, or a habit of not getting attached.
  • Being compared to a sibling at 8, when “Am I capable?” is the question, can settle into “I’m the less-good one,” long after everyone has forgotten the comparisons.
  • Parents who argued a lot when you were 5 can leave a different mark than the same arguments when you were 16. Young children often quietly decide they caused it; teenagers are more likely to take sides or become the go-between.
  • Being praised mainly for helping through the school years can grow into an adult who feels worthwhile only when useful, and uneasy when simply resting or receiving.

Researchers use the term sensitive periods for windows when a growing brain is especially ready to learn certain things from experience. The clearest evidence is for early childhood, when the basic architecture of the brain is being built. A second, strong wave of sensitivity comes in adolescence, especially for the social brain: neuroscientist Sarah-Jayne Blakemore and others have shown that teenagers are unusually tuned to peer approval and exclusion. That’s why the teen years leave such strong marks on how we see ourselves socially.

What makes an experience land harder

  • Timing: it touched the question you were answering at the time.
  • Repetition: something that happened again and again usually shapes more than a single event.
  • Meaning: what you concluded it said about you (“This happened because I’m not enough”) often matters more than the event itself.
  • Support afterward: a calm adult who helped you make sense of it is one of the strongest cushions there is. The same event with no one to talk to tends to land harder.
  • Your wiring: a highly sensitive child may take in more from the same room than a sibling does. See how wiring and patterns shape each other.

Sensitive doesn’t mean fixed. The brain keeps learning across the whole life, and later relationships, reflection and practice genuinely reshape early understandings. Early marks tend to run deeper; they are not a sentence. See how change actually happens.

What we carry forward, and how it shows up

OLD ANSWERS IN NEW ROOMS

You usually can’t see an old understanding directly. You see its footprints. A few common ones:

  • A reaction bigger than the moment. A small thing hits like a big thing. Chapter III calls this leftover stress: some of the feeling belongs to an older moment.
  • An “always” or “never” feeling. “It’s always me.” “Nobody ever listens.” Absolutes are often the voice of an old understanding, not a count of what actually happened this week.
  • An automatic role. In any group you slide into the same seat: the fixer, the peacekeeper, the funny one, the invisible one, the one who plans. Family therapists noticed long ago that children often take on roles like these to keep a family steady.
  • A rule you’ve never said out loud. You find out it exists only when someone breaks it, and you feel it as a small betrayal.
  • Feeling like a different age. In certain moments you feel eight, or fifteen, instead of your actual age. That’s often a clue about which stage the understanding comes from.

How the world shapes the lens

IT’S NOT ONLY FAMILY

Home is the first classroom, but it’s far from the only one. The wider world hands every child a set of scripts:

  • Family: how love was shown, how anger looked, whether feelings were talked about, who did which jobs. Psychologist John Gottman’s research on how parents respond to children’s emotions found that kids whose feelings were noticed and named (“emotion coaching”) tended to handle feelings more easily later, while kids whose feelings were brushed aside often learned to hide them.
  • Culture and community: whether it’s the group or the individual that comes first, how directly people speak, what respect looks like, what’s private. Researchers call the unwritten rules about which feelings may be shown, and where, display rules.
  • Gender scripts: the messages about what boys, girls and everyone else are supposed to be good at, feel and do. “Boys don’t cry.” “Girls keep the peace.” “Men fix things; women remember birthdays.” These scripts are one of the big quiet reasons invisible work at home still tends to fall unevenly.
  • School: grades, rankings, being picked or not picked, and which kinds of smart got noticed. Many adults still carry a school-age verdict about whether they’re “good at math” or “not a creative person.”
  • Money and circumstances: whether money was tight, whether plans could be counted on, whether you had to grow up fast.
  • Media and screens: what a “normal” body, family, home, relationship and success look like. Social psychologist Leon Festinger described how we judge ourselves by comparing with others; today many people, and especially teens, compare their everyday life with everyone else’s best, edited moments.

None of these are good or bad in themselves. Many are beautiful: a family’s warmth, a culture’s respect for elders, a community’s generosity. The point is simply to know which parts of your lens you chose and which were handed to you, so you can decide what to keep.

When the lens is wrong about you

THE STORY YOU TELL ABOUT YOURSELF

Children draw conclusions with the information they have, and they have very little. A child can’t know that a parent was exhausted, worried about work or grieving; they only know the parent was short with them. So many of the harshest things adults believe about themselves were concluded by a small person who didn’t have all the facts. And sometimes the child read things exactly right: not every hurt was a misunderstanding. Some common examples:

The old understandingWhere it often comes fromA fairer, adult reading
“I’m only okay when I’m useful.”Praise mostly for helping, being the responsible child“I’m allowed to rest and receive. Helping is something I do, not the price of being loved.”
“My needs are a burden.”A busy, stretched or stressed household; being told to stop fussing“Whatever was going on for the adults, it wasn’t a verdict on my needs. Asking clearly is fair.”
“I’m too much.” / “I’m not enough.”Big feelings or energy that didn’t fit the room; being compared“I was a different fit for that room. Some rooms, and people, suit me.”

“I have to be the strong one.”Growing up fast, looking after others, gender scripts“Strong people also lean. Letting someone help is part of a fair share.”

Two ordinary thinking habits keep these old stories running. The spotlight effect (studied by Thomas Gilovich) makes us overestimate how much others notice our slips. And our memory tends to file away evidence that fits what we already believe and let the rest slide by, which is why an old understanding can feel so well proven.

When the lens is wrong about other people

THE STORY YOU TELL ABOUT THEM

The same lens colors how we read the people closest to us, often in ways that make them strangers to us:

  • Reading an old face onto a new person. If silence at home meant someone was angry, your partner’s quiet evening can feel like trouble is on the way, even when they’re just tired.
  • Character instead of circumstances. We tend to explain other people’s slips by who they are (“lazy,” “careless”) and our own by our situation (“I was swamped”). Psychologists call this the fundamental attribution error, a term from Lee Ross. It’s one of the biggest sources of unfair blame at home.
  • Assuming they learned your rules. Someone who grew up in a home where nobody talked about feelings isn’t cold for not asking how your day went. They may simply never have seen it done.
  • “Everyone else has it together.” Comparing your partner, family or home with the polished version others show can make ordinary, good-enough people look like they’re failing.

The kind correction isn’t to assume the best about everyone no matter what. It’s to ask before you conclude: “When you went quiet, what was going on for you?” See the double empathy idea: a mix-up usually runs both ways.

The expectations we place on others

UNWRITTEN RULES, UNSPOKEN BILLS

This is where old understandings do the most damage in relationships, because expectations feel like facts about how things should be, not preferences we brought from somewhere. A few of the most common:

  • “If you loved me, you’d just know.” Mind-reading as proof of love. Many of us grew up with someone who seemed to anticipate our needs, or with stories where true love always does. Adults who love each other still need to be told.
  • “A good partner (or parent, or roommate) does X.” Plans the birthdays. Handles the car. Never raises their voice. Always answers texts quickly. Each of these is somebody’s family rule, not a law of nature.
  • “Rest is earned.” If rest was only allowed when everything was done, a partner lounging on a Sunday can feel like a moral failure rather than a different rhythm.
  • “Anger looks like this.” If anger in your home was loud, a quiet partner can seem calm when they’re actually upset; if it was silent, a loud partner can seem dangerous when they’re just venting.
  • “The person who notices does it.” In many homes the person who noticed the mess, the empty fridge or the permission slip was the person who handled it. Carried into adulthood, this rule quietly hands the whole mental load to whoever notices first, and then feels unfair to them. See the Preface on the work nobody sees.

An expectation that’s never said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only miss it, often without knowing it was there. That’s how many resentments start: not with one person doing wrong, but with two people following two different rulebooks, each sure theirs is simply “normal.”

The rulebook from home

SAY THE RULES OUT LOUD

Everyone grew up with an unwritten family rulebook. Writing yours down, even just a few lines, is one of the quickest ways to see your lens. Try finishing these sentences the way the home you grew up in would have:

  1. In my family, love looked like… (doing things, saying things, spending time, gifts, touch, or not being shown much at all)
  2. When someone was upset, we… (talked, went quiet, got loud, made a joke, left)
  3. The jobs were split like this… (who cooked, cleaned, planned, paid, remembered, fixed)
  4. Resting was okay when…
  5. Asking for help meant…
  6. The unforgivable thing was…

Then, for each line, ask one question: “Do I still want this rule?” Some you’ll keep with pride. Some you’ll want to soften. Some you may realize you’ve been living by without ever agreeing to.

A lens check for the moment

FOUR QUESTIONS, ABOUT A MINUTE

When a reaction feels bigger than the moment, or an “always” or “never” shows up, try this:

  1. Where did I learn this? Whose voice does the thought sound like? A parent, a teacher, a friend group, a show?
  2. How old does this feel? If you feel eight or fifteen, the understanding probably comes from then.
  3. Is it true here, with this person, now? What would you think if a friend described this exact situation to you?
  4. What would the adult me choose? Keep the old rule, soften it, or try something new just this once.

Do it after you’ve settled, not in the middle of a hot moment. If your battery is low, check it first with Today’s Weather or WP-02, and use the Calm-Down Kit. Old understandings get loudest when we’re tired.

Saying it to someone else

“I think this is my old family rulebook talking. Where I grew up, the person who noticed the mess had to clean it. Can we work out our own rule?”

“That reaction was bigger than what you did. Part of it is older than us. Give me a minute and I’ll tell you what I actually need.”

You never owe anyone your history. Share the part that helps them understand the moment.

Doing this together

TWO RULEBOOKS, ONE HOME

Every couple, family and shared house is a meeting of rulebooks. The goal isn’t to decide whose family did it right. It’s to write a new, shared one on purpose.

  • Swap rulebooks on a calm day. Each person fills in the six lines above, then you read them to each other with curiosity, not correction. Some people find it makes sense of an argument that keeps coming back.
  • Name the jobs, then choose. Lay out the jobs you each assumed “someone” would do, and give each one a single owner by choice rather than by habit. The Lemonade Stand and WP-03 help here.
  • Agree on how upset looks. Tell each other how anger, hurt and tiredness show up in you, and what helps. The Wiring Card is a good place to write it down.
  • Talk in a check-in, not in the heat. Use a check-in to bring up one expectation at a time.
  • With children: you’re shaping their lens right now. You don’t need to be perfect. Research on repair suggests that noticing a rupture and making it right, again and again, teaches children more than never getting it wrong.

How this uses the Five Pillars

  • Pillar I, See the whole load: unspoken expectations are invisible work. Holding an old rule in check, or quietly meeting a rule nobody agreed to, costs real energy.
  • Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person: a learned rule is part of the setup you were raised in, not a flaw in your character or theirs. Change the rule, not the person.
  • Pillar III, Read your state first: ask whether a feeling is the size of this moment or older, before you act on it.
  • Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive: the lens colors what you hear. Checking the lens is part of tuning in.
  • Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives: what we were praised or punished for as children keeps quietly pulling on our choices as adults.

Going deeper: the research, in plain words

WHERE THESE IDEAS COME FROM

  • Erik Erikson’s psychosocial stages (from the 1950s) described eight stages across the whole life, each with a central tension such as trust versus mistrust or identity versus confusion. The exact ages and stages are debated, and his model reflects the culture he wrote in, but the idea that development continues across the whole life is widely accepted.
  • Jean Piaget showed that young children think differently from adults, not just with less information. Young children tend to see events from their own point of view, which helps explain why they so often decide they caused things they didn’t.
  • Attachment research (John Bowlby, Mary Ainsworth and many since) describes how early relationships shape expectations of closeness. Later work found these patterns can shift, including toward what’s called “earned security.” See the library on attachment theory.
  • Sensitive periods and early brain development: the Harvard Center on the Developing Child summarizes decades of research showing that early experiences, especially responsive “serve and return” relationships, help build the brain’s foundations, and that supportive relationships buffer stress.
  • Adolescence as a second sensitive period: Sarah-Jayne Blakemore and colleagues have shown the teenage brain is especially tuned to peers and social belonging.
  • Emotion socialization: John Gottman’s work on “meta-emotion” found that how parents respond to feelings shapes how children come to handle their own.
  • Core beliefs and schemas: Aaron Beck’s cognitive therapy and Jeffrey Young’s schema work describe deep, early beliefs about self and others that shape how we read events.
  • Social comparison (Leon Festinger), the spotlight effect (Thomas Gilovich) and the fundamental attribution error (Lee Ross) are well-studied everyday biases that keep old stories running.
  • Learning chores at home: studies of families over time have found that the way parents split housework is linked with how their grown children later split it, one reason these patterns pass down quietly.
  • Positive childhood experiences such as a trusted adult or feeling you belonged at school are linked with better adult well-being, even alongside hard experiences. See hard and adverse experiences for a careful reading of that research.

These are introductions, not the final word. Research on development is huge, active and often debated, and population findings never predict what will happen to any one person.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t this just blaming my parents?

No. Understanding where a rule came from isn’t the same as blaming the people who handed it to you. Many parents passed on what they were given, often while doing their best with less than they needed. If that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit: understanding where a rule came from never means saying it was okay. You can understand, appreciate what was good, and still choose differently.

I don’t remember much from when I was little. Can I still use this?

Yes. You don’t need memories, just footprints: the reactions that feel older than the moment, the rules you notice only when they’re broken, the roles you slide into. Those are enough to work with.

If it happened early, is it permanent?

Earlier and more repeated experiences tend to go deeper, but people keep learning all their lives. New relationships, reflection and practice genuinely reshape old understandings, usually slowly, and often by layering a new habit on top of the old one.

What if my partner won’t look at their rulebook?

Start with your own. Saying “I think this is my old rule talking” often makes it easier for the other person to notice theirs, without being asked. You can also just ask one small, curious question: “How did your family handle this?”

Is my culture’s way of doing things a “wrong lens”?

No. Cultural ways of living carry deep wisdom. The question is never “Is this right or wrong?” but “Is this working for the people in this home, and did we choose it together?”

Limits and cautions

  • This is not a way to diagnose yourself or anyone else, and it isn’t therapy.
  • Stage ages are rough guides. Many people move through these questions at different times, or more than once.
  • Don’t use someone’s childhood as a weapon in an argument (“You’re just like your father”). Share your own lens; let them name theirs.
  • If looking back feels heavy, you can stop, come back another day, or explore it with a qualified professional, if and when you want that.
