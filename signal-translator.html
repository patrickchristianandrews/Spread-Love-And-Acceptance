3. HEAR BOTH SIDES

A productive conversation is two people heard, not one person convinced. Acknowledgement is not agreement. Hearing how it landed does not cancel your fact, and your fact does not cancel how it landed.

  1. Say the sentence. Stop. Do not explain it, and do not defend your intent yet.
  2. They say what they heard. You confirm the fact, or correct the fact. Not the person. "That's not how it felt" is not a correction of the fact.
  3. They name how it landed. You say it back. "That can make sense" means you heard them. It does not mean you are guilty, and it does not mean they have to drop it.
  4. Switch. Same moves the other way. One side heard is not a conversation. Do not skip their turn because you already know what you meant.
  5. Only then, if a fact is still missing: "Your fact, as I heard it. My part. One fact I want added. I am not asking you to cancel how it landed."
  6. One ask that works for both. One owner. One time. Then stop. If another sentence is on this page, ask whether both of you can still take it in. If not, park it.

Do not invalidate. "You're too sensitive," "that isn't what happened," and "I didn't mean it, so it doesn't count" end the hearing. Intent can be said after they have been mirrored, not instead of it. The longer order is on Check-ins. The mirror-before-answer step is the speaker-listener order (Family Institute). Complain about the event, not the person (Gottman Institute).

WHEN TO REDIRECT, AND WHEN TO STOP

Redirect when it gets heated (a raised voice, or sharp replies in a thread), a second topic shows up to win, or they heard a verdict you did not mean. Say once: "That's real. We are still on this sentence. I am not against you." Then say the chosen sentence again, and stop.

Stop when you repeat yourself, you go blank, they shut down or stop replying, the room is not safe, or a note on this page says Stop. Say: "I need a pause. I am not leaving this. We are okay. I will come back at [time]." Do not finish the point.

5. IF IT STARTS TO DERAIL

Stop the topic. Do not finish the point.

Say: "I need a pause. I am not leaving this. We are okay. I will come back at [time]." Say "we are okay" only if it's true. At work: "I'd like to pick this up at [time]."

Someone who is flooded often needs at least 20 minutes, and the return should not slip past the next day (Gottman Institute). Come back to the same topic. Do not open with what happened during the pause.

Care does not require a solution. Lower your voice. Sit down. Get water. Put the phones away. If they asked for space, do not follow them, and do not send a stack of messages. One line is the signal. The return is the care.

WHERE IT FITS When to use this tool, and what it is for

It looks at how a sentence is built (pressure words, questions that carry a complaint, absolutes, hints, a missing when) and how that structure may land for someone wired differently. That is Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. Timing and state are Pillar III, Read your state first. Inside you, it shows how you send. Between you and others, it helps the message you meant be the one that arrives.

Use it anywhere a sentence has to cross from one person to another. Inside the program, or with no program at all.

  • In the program. A check-in, a weekly catch-up, a WP-09 message, the line you wish you had used, or the sentence you will say when you come back from a pause. Once you have both checked your state and can listen. Once a wiring card exists, test the sentence, not the person.
  • Outside it. A text, email or Slack message you have not sent. A note to a coworker, a manager, a teacher, a landlord, a doctor, or a friend. A family dinner, a co-parent handoff, a meeting. Or just learning how you hear, with no conversation planned.

Do not read a score out loud at the other person. You can add more than one sentence to the talking notes. How to build them, and when the list is getting heavy, is on Check-ins.

What it looks for

The list of patterns appears here.

What this tool is, and isn't. It uses rules built from published research and lived-experience guidance, collected in Wired Differently. It looks for patterns in the words, like hints, sarcasm, vague timing and labels, that commonly cause static for particular wirings. It can't hear tone, see faces or know your history, and it can't diagnose anyone. Every reading starts with "may" for a reason: people vary widely within every group. The best use is to find the question worth asking each other. It scores the wording, never the person.
