For parents: raising kind, steady kids

Kids don’t need a perfect parent. They need a grown-up who stays fairly steady, helps them understand their feelings, and makes things right after a hard moment. This page is free, and you can read just the part you need today.

Helping kids name and handle big feelings

Psychologist John Gottman studied how parents respond when their children are upset. He called one helpful way emotion coaching. Kids whose feelings were noticed and named tended to handle feelings more easily as they grew. Kids whose feelings were brushed aside often learned to hide them. Here is the idea, in five small steps.

  1. Notice. Catch the feeling while it’s still small: the quiet face, the slammed door, the “I don’t care.” Small upsets are the easiest place to practice.
  2. Name it. Offer a word, gently, as a guess: mad, sad, worried, jealous, left out, disappointed. A name makes a feeling smaller and easier to hold.
  3. Accept the feeling. All feelings are allowed. You don’t have to agree with the reason to say the feeling makes sense. Skip “You’re fine” and “Don’t be silly.”
  4. Limit the behavior. Feelings are okay; hurting people or things is not. Say the limit calmly and briefly, and keep everyone safe.
  5. Solve it together. Once they’re calmer, ask what they could try next time. Let them come up with ideas first, then help them choose one.

Words you could use

“You look really frustrated. That tower took you a long time.”

“It’s okay to be mad. It’s not okay to hit. I’ll keep you both safe.”

“That was a big feeling. When you’re ready, let’s think of what could help next time.”

You won’t do this every time, and that’s fine. Tired parents speak sharply sometimes. What matters most is coming back and making it right, which you’ll find in the calm-down section. Siblings who fight

Living with kids who are fighting all the time is exhausting, and wanting more peace at home is completely fair. Most fights between kids are about the same few things: turns, space, fairness and the grown-ups’ attention. When the setup makes those clear (whose turn, whose space, what happens next), there is less to fight about. This is the site’s idea of fixing the setup, not the person, and it lets you be a coach instead of a judge.

Fix the setup first

  1. Notice when the fights happen. The same toy, the same seat, the same time of day. Most of them repeat.
  2. Fix the setup for the top one. A turn timer for the tablet. A turn chart for the front seat, on the fridge. A box of things that are only theirs and don’t have to be shared.
  3. Give each child one-on-one time. A few minutes with each child, most days, with your full attention. A lot of fights are really asking for you.

When a fight starts

  • Describe, don’t judge. Say what you see, without blame: “I see two kids who both want the blue cup.” Then ask what they could try, and let them pick.
  • Stay out of the “who started it” trial. You almost never saw the start, and a verdict leaves one child feeling unfairly treated. Focus on what happens next.
  • Step in if someone is getting hurt. Separate them calmly. Comfort the hurt child first. The talking can wait until everyone is calm.
  • Coach the repair. Once things settle, help each child say what happened for them, then ask: “What could you do to make it right?” A fixed tower or a shared turn often means more than a forced “sorry.”

Words you could use

“I see two kids who both want the same thing. What could we try so you both get a turn?”

“I’m not going to pick a winner. I’ll help you find a plan you can both live with.”

“The timer decides whose turn it is, not me. Ten minutes each, starting with whoever’s name comes first today.”

One family’s story: Sam’s two kids fought over the front seat every single morning. Sam put a calendar on the fridge with their names on alternate days. The fights didn’t vanish, but that one did, and Sam stopped being the referee at 8 a.m.

Try tonight: pick the one thing they fight over most and decide a simple rule for whose turn it is. Tell them both before it comes up. For giving older kids real jobs of their own, see WP-03: One owner per job.

Calm-down routines for kids

Calm-down tools work best when kids practice them on easy days, not only in the middle of a meltdown. Keep them short, playful and the same each time.

A simple breathing game

Smell the flower, blow out the candle. Hold up one finger as a flower. Breathe in slowly through your nose, like smelling it. Then hold up another finger as a candle and breathe out slowly through your mouth to blow it out. Do it three times, together. Little ones like to blow out “birthday candles” on all five fingers, one by one.

For a screen-based version, the Night Garden is a breathing game: breathe slowly and the flowers bloom. There are no timers and nothing to lose. You can also tap the moon-shaped Breathe button on any page of this site for a guided breathing break of one, three or five minutes.

A calm corner

Pick a cozy spot together: a beanbag, a few pillows, a blanket. Add a soft toy, a couple of books, maybe some paper and crayons. It is a place to feel better, not a punishment. Let your child choose to go there, and say it’s okay to go with them. Grown-ups can use it too.

Words you could use

“Your body looks really tense. Want to go to the calm corner? I can come with you.”

“Let’s blow out three candles together, then we’ll talk.”

When you yell

Most parents yell sometimes. It does not make you a bad parent. When you notice your voice going up, stop and step back. Lower your voice, and get down to your child’s eye level.

Say what is happening: “I’m getting too loud. I’m going to take a breath.” Breathe for one minute, in for 4 and out for 6. Then make it right: “I’m sorry I yelled. That wasn’t fair to you. Let’s try again.”

There is a short step-by-step version on Upset right now?, for when it is happening.

Coming back after a big upset

Everyone loses their temper sometimes, kids and parents both. The repair afterwards teaches more than never getting it wrong. When you’ve both calmed down, come back. If you lost your temper, go first: it shows them how it’s done.

Our Apology languages guide says a good sorry has a few parts. At kid size, that’s four simple steps:

  1. Say sorry for the thing. “I’m sorry I knocked over your tower.”
  2. Say it was yours. “That was my fault.” No “but.”
  3. Help fix it. “Can I help build it back?”
  4. Say what you’ll do next time. “Next time I’ll ask before I touch it.”

Then let the other child answer in their own time. “Not yet” is allowed. Forced, instant sorries tend to sound empty; a sorry that comes after a calm-down and includes a fix usually means more to both kids.

Words you could use

“I’m sorry I yelled. That wasn’t fair to you. Next time I’ll take a breath before I talk.”

“You’re not in trouble for being upset. Let’s figure out how to fix the tower together.”

Talking with teens

Teens are working on becoming their own people. Some extra friction is a normal part of that, and it doesn’t mean the relationship is failing. A few things tend to help:

  • Talk side by side. In the car, cooking, walking the dog. It feels less like a meeting.
  • Listen more than you advise. Ask open questions, then wait. Lectures tend to close the door.
  • Keep the rules clear and fair, and explain why. Hand over a little more responsibility as they show they can handle it.
  • Notice the small reaches. A meme they send, a story at bedtime. Turning toward these little moments keeps you close.

Words you could use

“Do you want my advice, or do you just want me to listen?”

“I don’t need every detail. I just want to know you’re okay.”

Stepfamilies and blended families

Joining two families takes time. Many families find it takes a few years, not a few months, to feel settled. Going slowly is normal, and it often works better.

  • Let the parent lead on discipline at first. Rules and consequences land better from the child’s own parent. The stepparent builds warmth first: interest, fun, being reliable. Once there’s trust, the stepparent can take on more.
  • Spend time side by side. Cooking, a game, a drive to practice. Small, easy time together builds a bond without pressure.
  • Don’t force names. Let kids choose what to call a stepparent. A first name is fine. “Mom” or “Dad” may come later, or not at all.
  • Two households can have two sets of rules. Kids can handle “at this house, we do it this way.” Keep yours clear and calm, and skip criticizing the other home.
  • Older kids and a new baby or toddler. A new little one takes a lot of attention, and older stepkids can feel pushed to the edge. Keep some one-on-one time with each of them, and don’t make them the default babysitter.
  • Watch for loyalty binds. A child may feel that liking a stepparent is disloyal to their other parent. Say out loud that they don’t have to choose, and never ask them to pick a side.

Words you could use

To a stepchild: “I’m not trying to replace your mom. I’d just like us to get along.”

To a stepchild: “You can love your dad and still like hanging out with me. You don’t have to pick.”

For the couple: “Can you take the lead on bedtime rules with your kids, and I’ll back you up?”

Your own child who visits

If your child lives with you only part of the time (every other weekend, say), it’s easy for them to feel like a guest in a busy house.

  • A space that stays theirs. A drawer, a shelf or a bed that’s there even when they’re at the other home.
  • A small ritual. Saturday pancakes, a walk, a show you always watch together.
  • One small job of their own, so they belong, not just visit. The Lemonade Stand can give a child who lives here part of the time a smaller share.
  • Light contact between visits: a quick message or photo, without pressure to reply.
  • Same house rules for everyone who’s here, explained once, calmly, even if the other home does it differently.

Two adults who agree on the plan, in private first, make everything easier. For who does which job in a bigger household, try Share the load. For working with the other household, see Separated co-parents. And For teens is a page an older stepchild can read on their own.

Watching Frequency Buddies together

Frequency Buddies is the site’s free cartoon. Two puppy pals, Tidbit and Sugarfoot, hit real tough times and find their way through, together. Episodes come in short chapters of about 2 to 4 minutes, so you can watch one and stop. Captions are always on, and a short note before each episode says what happens.

The pups make it easier to talk about feelings, because it’s about them, not your child. After a chapter, try two or three gentle questions. There are no wrong answers, and it’s fine if they just want to watch.

Sharing

In The Heavy Basket, Sugarfoot keeps saying “it’s fine” under a heavy picnic basket, until the pals stop and share the load.

  • Why do you think Sugarfoot said “it’s fine” when it wasn’t?
  • Is there something at home that feels too heavy for one person?
  • What’s a job we could share this week?

Feeling left out

In The Longest Night, one pal always hurries ahead and the other always follows, until they stop and ask, “Do you want to lead this time?”

  • How do you think the pup at the back felt?
  • Have you ever felt left behind or left out? What happened?
  • What could someone say to help a friend feel included?

Saying sorry

In The Storm Over the Treehouse, the pals snap at each other after a storm, then say sorry and build their treehouse back better. In Who Broke the Kite?, they blame each other until someone asks what broke, not who broke it.

  • How did the pups make things right?
  • What helps you feel better after someone hurts your feelings?
  • Is there anyone you’d like to say sorry to, or fix something with?

When to get more help

Every child has hard weeks. It’s a good idea to talk to your child’s doctor, the school counselor, or a licensed child therapist if:

  • worries, sadness or anger last for weeks rather than days,
  • they’re getting in the way of sleep, eating, school or friendships, or
  • your gut tells you something is wrong. You know your child.

Asking for help is not a sign you’ve failed. It’s part of looking after them.

If your child talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to be alive, take it seriously and get help the same day. Stay with them, stay calm, and listen.

  • In the US, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time. Parents can call for advice too.
  • If someone is in danger right now, call 911 or your local emergency number.
  • Outside the US, findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country.

If anyone at home is not safe, please see Not safe at home? for free, private hotlines.
