Every kind of relationship

How it fits your relationships

Full version← Back to the simple version

For every road below there’s a workpaper package and report: one fillable PDF with the right workpapers, and a detailed report with findings, recommendations and a plan.

The Objective Ledger starts with you. It helps you see your own stress, rhythms and reactions clearly and without shame. Then it gives any two people who share time, space, work or care a fair way to see what each of you carries, so hard conversations can start from shared facts instead of competing impressions.

Where this fits: This page belongs to Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, part of the Five Pillars. In you: notice everything you carry, including the invisible, mental and emotional load. Between you and others: put it on one shared, fair page and give each job a clear owner, instead of blaming anyone.

The ideas stay the same across every relationship. What changes is what's being shared: a home, a child, a project, a parent's care, or a friendship's give and take. This page shows how each part of the program connects to each kind of relationship, with an example of what it can look like in real life.

Yourself

Every relationship runs through your own nervous system first. This is the self-discovery half of the program: learning your own load, rhythms and reactions well enough to explain them, to yourself and then to others.

You snap at someone at 4 p.m. and can't figure out why. A quick WP-02 check shows you've been running near empty since morning. The snap was leftover load, not about them, and now you can say so.

Start with

  • Today’s Weather: a minute on today’s conditions, with a forecast, a talk window and one next step.
  • WP-02, the Battery & Stress Meter: separate what you're already carrying from what just happened.
  • WP-11, the Calm-Down Kit: decide in advance what settles you, so it's ready when you need it.

How the rest connects

Chapter III explains why a reaction can be bigger than its cause. Frequency Calibration shows your natural pace for money, decisions and check-ins, which is useful to know before comparing it with anyone else's. Every other tool works better once you can name your own state.

Wired Differently explains how your neurotype shapes the way words reach you, and how they reach people wired differently. The Signal Translator lets you test a sentence against both people's wiring before you say it. Check-ins gives you the steps for the conversation itself, once the setting is calm enough to have it.

Self-discovery isn't self-criticism. The aim is to understand yourself as objectively and kindly as you'd want to be understood.

Partners

Two people sharing a life, whether married, dating, living together or not. This is where the invisible work of running a shared life is usually heaviest, and where it's least likely to be said out loud.

One of you tracks every birthday, bill, appointment and running-low item. The other genuinely doesn't know that work exists, and hears "you never help" as unfair, because from where they stand, it is.

Start with

  • WP-01, the Field Audit: a week of who actually did what, written down by each of you.
  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: one owner for every recurring task, so it stops being renegotiated.
  • WP-13, the 90-second check-in: 90 seconds a day that keeps small things small.

How the rest connects

The whole program applies. The Preface and Chapter II name the imbalance. CALC-01 gives a plain read on whether it's sustainable. PROG-01 walks you through it over six weeks, and REPORT-01 keeps the record so progress builds. When a topic is too big for the 90-second check-in, Check-ins gives you the steps. Before you sit down, test your opening sentence against both of your wirings in the Signal Translator. Stick to one topic. Don't pile on other topics, assign blame or criticize the person. Say that you're not against them. The ground rules cover the rest, including how to pause and come back.

The tools describe the arrangement, never the person. If either of you starts using the numbers to win, stop and go back to WP-11.

Family

Parents and adult children, siblings, in-laws, and grandparents: the relationships you didn't choose but often share a roof, a group chat or a holiday table with.

Your adult son moves back home. You think "helping out" means cooking twice a week; he thinks it means keeping his room tidy. Every dinner turns into the same argument about what was agreed, when nothing actually was.

Start with

  • Chapter I, the Radio Frequency Paradigm: families often clash on urgency and tone more than on substance.
  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: write down who owns what, so nobody has to guess.
  • WP-09, the Tone Filter: old family patterns make tone land hard, and this slows things down.

How the rest connects

Chapter III helps when a reaction carries years of history. Chapter IV helps a family agree on what "fair" means before arguing about whether something was. The Carrier Wave Decoder works for any two family members after a conversation goes sideways. For the talk itself, Check-ins puts acknowledgment ahead of the usual comeback. Test the opening line in the Signal Translator first, especially if the talk would happen at a holiday table or in a group chat. Those are poor places for a hard topic.

The worksheets are written for adults. With children and younger teens, use the ideas, like naming your state and owning tasks clearly, rather than the forms. Never ask a child to audit a parent.

Co-parents

Two people raising a child together, whether in one home or two, together or separated. The work is constant, the stakes are high, and most of it is invisible to the other house.

Two homes, one nine-year-old, and a standing argument about who handles school forms, dentist visits, and the permission slip that nobody saw until the morning it was due.

Start with

  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: every recurring kid task gets exactly one owner.
  • WP-09, the Tone Filter: turn a charged message into fact, feeling and ask before you hit send.
  • WP-04, the Deficit Audit: once a month, sort what keeps slipping into a real gap or a one-off.

Four steps for calmer handoffs

A court order or parenting plan always comes first. These steps sit inside it and never replace it. They belong to Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person (clear owners and handoffs) and Pillar III, Read your state first. In you: notice how full your own battery is before exchange day. Between you: keep the handoffs short, plain and about logistics.

  1. Use an exchange-day script. Keep handoff talk short and logistical, and say it in front of the child the same way every time: “Hi. Homework’s done, the reading folder is in the front pocket, and the trip form is signed. Pick-up is Sunday at five. See you then.” If something bigger comes up, try: “Can we put that on Thursday’s check-in?” Kids are never the messengers, and handoffs are not the place to settle anything.
  2. Give each task one owner on a shared calendar. School forms, dentist visits, permission slips and sports kit each get exactly one name, so nothing depends on guessing which house has it. The worksheet for this is one owner per job (WP-03, the RACI Treaty).
  3. Check a message before you send it. Run it through the Tone Filter (WP-09), which turns it into fact, feeling and ask, or the Signal Translator with the relationship set to co-parents. A message that reads as a verdict makes the next handoff harder.
  4. Hold a short weekly check-in across two homes. Ten minutes by phone or video, about the coming week only: dates, owners, anything that changed. The 90-second check-in (WP-13) works well weekly for parents in two homes.

How the rest connects

The Lemonade Stand and CALC-01 show whether the load is lopsided. The WP-13 check-in works well weekly instead of daily for parents in two homes. REPORT-01 keeps a calm, factual record that both of you can see. A charged message should go through the Signal Translator before it is sent, with the relationship set to co-parents. If you do meet, use the steps in Check-ins. What's said there is never a case file for later.

If there's a court order or parenting plan, it comes first. These tools are for the day-to-day, not a substitute for legal advice or mediation, and never for building a case against the other parent.

Friends

Friendships run on unspoken give and take, which makes them easy to strain without anyone noticing until someone is already hurt.

One friend always plans, books and reminds. The other always just shows up. After a few years the planner feels taken for granted, the other has no idea, and neither has said a word.

Start with

  • The Carrier Wave Decoder: for the conversation that went sideways and still stings.
  • WP-09, the Tone Filter: say the unseen thing as fact, feeling and a clear ask.
  • WP-01's Neutral Refusals: kind, ready-made ways to say no without it becoming a rift.

How the rest connects

The Preface explains why the planner's work is invisible. Chapter I explains why "whenever works" means different things to different people. Most of the record-keeping tools are more than a friendship needs. If you do name it, Check-ins is enough structure, and the Signal Translator is worth ten minutes on the sentence so it arrives as a fact, not a verdict. Between the big conversations, Turning Toward has small, everyday ways to show a friend you notice them.

Use a light touch. The point isn't a ledger of favors. It's naming what's gone unseen, once, kindly.

Roommates

People sharing a home without sharing a life: housemates, or a friend in the spare room. The chores are real, and the goodwill runs out fast when they aren't shared.

Four people, one kitchen, and a chore chart everyone agreed to in September and nobody has looked at since October.

Start with

  • The Lemonade Stand: list the week's tasks and hours so everyone sees the same picture.
  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: a named owner for trash, bills, supplies and cleaning.
  • WP-13, the 90-second check-in: a quick weekly version at a house meeting works well.

How the rest connects

Chapter IV helps a house agree on how fairness will be judged: by time, by task, or by outcome. WP-04 catches the problem that comes back every month. CALC-01 shows whether the current setup is sustainable. A house meeting is a check-in. Follow the steps on Check-ins, stick to one topic, and run the opening sentence through the Signal Translator so it does not land as a call-out in front of the others.

Three or more people, and money

This is Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. In you: notice which bills you quietly cover or chase, and how that feels. Between you: put every bill on one page so nobody has to keep score in their head.

  • Agree the rule for rent and bills first. Equal shares, by room size, or another split you all choose. Deciding how “fair” will be judged comes before arguing about whether something was. Write the rule down.
  • Keep one shared-expenses note. One page everyone can see: each bill, the amount, the due date, who pays it, and how and by when the others pay them back (a payment app is fine). Give the note itself an owner who updates it once a month.
  • Talk about money without a fight. Raise it at a house meeting, not late at night in the group chat. Stick to one topic, start with the numbers rather than with who is late, and talk about the setup, not the person. Try: “The electric bill went up and our split hasn’t. Can we look at the numbers together on Sunday?” Test that opening line in the Signal Translator first.

The Lemonade Stand works with more than two people, so a whole house can see the week’s jobs and hours side by side. For more on money and shared homes, see the Library entries on roommates and shared homes and disagreeing about money.

With more than two people, the tools work for the whole house. If you are using a paper sheet with two columns, fill it in pairs or add columns.

Coworkers and teams

You spend more waking hours with some coworkers than with family. The same invisible work shows up at work: the follow-ups, the notes, and the reminders that keep things moving.

One teammate quietly sends the follow-ups, keeps the notes and remembers the deadlines. None of it appears in anyone's review, and she's starting to resent the whole team.

Start with

  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) started in project management, so it fits a team naturally.
  • Chapter I, the Radio Frequency Paradigm: urgency and tone mismatches cause most workplace friction.
  • WP-09, the Tone Filter: check a message before it goes out on chat or email.

How the rest connects

The WP-13 check-in works like a short daily team meeting (a stand-up). WP-04 works like a monthly look-back, or retrospective. WP-02 and WP-11 are for you, privately, on a hard day. For a team of three to eight, the Lemonade Stand and the workpapers take more than two names, so everyone sees the same list of jobs and owners. Before a message goes out in a team chat, the Signal Translator shows how it might land. The Library’s Systems and teams page has more on ownership, handoffs and blameless reviews.

Use these among peers who agree to them. They aren't a way for managers to evaluate people, and nothing here should become a performance record or HR documentation about anyone.

Caregivers

Caring for an aging parent, a husband, wife or partner after surgery or an illness, a relative who's ill, or someone with a disability, often while sharing that care with siblings or other family members.

Three siblings share care for their mother. The one who lives closest ends up with the appointments, the medications and the 2 a.m. calls, while the others help "whenever they can."

Your husband has knee surgery. For six weeks you are the driver, the cook, the one who remembers every appointment and the one who still goes to work. He is grateful, but neither of you has said out loud how much has moved onto your side.

When the person you care for is your partner

This is Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar III, Read your state first. In you: notice what you are carrying. Between you: name it together as a setup that changed for a while, not a debt one of you owes.

  • What to notice. The jobs moved to you without either of you choosing it. Your own things (a walk, a friend, sleep) quietly drop off. Snapping or going quiet is often a low battery, not a verdict on the relationship.
  • One small thing to try today. Pick one: check your battery with the battery check (WP-02) or Today’s Weather; take a one-minute break with Breathe, the button in the bottom corner of every page; or ask one person for one specific help, like “Could you do Thursday’s drive to the appointment?” instead of “let me know if you need anything.”
  • When you are both ready. Write the week’s jobs on one page together. Some can move back to your partner as they are able, and some can go to friends or family, each with one owner.

Start with

  • WP-02, the Battery & Stress Meter: caregiver strain builds quietly; this makes it visible.
  • WP-03, the RACI Treaty: one owner for each part of the care, so "whenever" becomes a plan.
  • WP-11, the Calm-Down Kit: for the moments you're too drained to talk well.

How the rest connects

The Preface describes caregiving exactly: unbilled work only one person can see. The WP-13 check-in helps with handoffs between the people sharing care. CALC-01 and REPORT-01 show, calmly and in numbers, when the arrangement can't hold. Sibling logistics belong in a check-in, not in the hallway after an appointment. Test the ask in the Signal Translator first. The person you care for is not the audience for that meeting.

If WP-02 keeps showing you near empty, that's a signal to get more support, not a personal failing.
