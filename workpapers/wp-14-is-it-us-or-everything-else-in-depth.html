Is it us, or is it everything else?

The Static Dampening Log

This worksheet puts Pillar III (“Read your state first”) to work, with help from Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person. In you: you notice how much of today’s mood came in from outside. Between you and others: outside strain gets a shared plan, instead of being pinned on the person nearest to you.

Purpose: to separate outside pressure (money, work, illness, broken sleep, family) from friction that really starts between two people, so that outside strain stops being blamed on the other person by default. It is a one-week log, done by each person about their own days, read together at the end of the week.

How often: one week at a time, whenever life gets heavy or you notice more snapping than usual. Many people run it once, then again in a hard season.

Why outside pressure gets blamed on the person next to you

Pressure from outside doesn’t stay outside. Researchers call this stress spillover. In daily-diary studies of newlyweds, Lisa Neff and Benjamin Karney found that spouses under more stress outside the relationship reacted more strongly to everyday negative moments within it. In an earlier, smaller study of air traffic controllers, Rena Repetti found that after high-workload days they were more withdrawn and less expressive with their spouses that evening.

Both are correlational, so they show a link, not a rule. But the pattern is familiar: on a heavy day, a dish left out feels like a statement, and a quiet partner looks cold. Each of you then answers what you think happened, not what did. This log is a week-long way to check.

What counts as outside pressure

Anything that weighs on you from beyond the two of you:

  • Work: deadlines, shifts, a difficult boss, a long commute, job worries.
  • Money: bills, debts, an unexpected cost, not knowing if it will stretch.
  • Health: illness, pain, a hospital visit, someone you care for.
  • Sleep: a bad night, a baby, a snoring partner, worry at 3 a.m.
  • Family and friends: a parent who needs help, a falling-out, a child having a hard time.
  • Everything else: the news, the weather, a move, the car breaking down.

If you want a number for it, How much are you carrying? (WP-02) gives a one-minute load score.

Part A: the evening log

About a minute each evening. Each person fills in only their own row, about their own day.

A tense moment is any small snap, sulk or silence. Write what happened in a few plain words, not who was wrong: “short with each other about the weekend”, not “he was rude”. Skip a day if you miss one; a few evenings are enough to see a pattern.

Sorting each moment

For each tense moment, mark your best guess:

  • Outside pressure: on a lighter day, it probably wouldn’t have happened.
  • Between us: it would have come up anyway. It’s about the two of you, or how you share things.
  • Some of both: a real issue, made louder by a heavy day. This is the most common one.
  • Not sure yet: a fine answer. Look again at the end of the week.

Parts B and C: the end-of-week look

At the end of the week, together, look at the week at a glance. The sheet counts each person’s heavy days, where the pressure came from, and how many tense moments came on a day when someone had a lot of outside pressure. If you both wrote down the same moment, it counts twice; that’s fine for a rough picture.

Then the important question: what got blamed on the wrong thing? Common mix-ups:

  • Tiredness read as “not interested in me”.
  • Going quiet after a hard day read as coldness, or as anger.
  • A short answer read as “you’re annoyed with me”, when they were annoyed with their day.
  • Worry about money coming out as a row about a small purchase.

Write each one down as the moment, what it seemed to be about, what was really going on, and what would help next time. Without blame: everyone does this.

Part D: one plan for the pressure

Outside pressure rarely goes away because you talked about it. What helps is facing it on the same side:

  • A way to flag a heavy day. “Rough day, not you.” “I’m at about 20%.” Said early, it changes how everything after it lands.
  • What you’ll go easy on until it passes. Simple dinners, no big decisions on weeknights, fewer plans at the weekend.
  • One bit of outside help. A friend covers a school run, a sibling takes a hospital visit, takeaway on Fridays.
  • A talk about the pressure itself. The stress-reducing conversation on Turning Toward is a simple one: one of you talks about the outside stress, and the other listens, firmly on their side, without fixing.

The “between us” column still matters

This sheet isn’t a way to explain everything away. Outside pressure explains a hard day. It doesn’t excuse unkindness, and it doesn’t make what anyone felt less real. Moments that really are between you deserve their own calm talk, on a lighter day: try Say it so it lands (WP-09) for the first sentence, and Check-ins for the talk. If it’s about who does what, One owner per job (WP-03) is the place.

If the pressure isn’t going away, because it’s a long illness or shifts that never line up, see When one of you is ill or Different hours.

Not just for couples

Housemates, a family, a team: anyone who shares space or work can run it. On a team, keep the outside column general (“a lot on outside work”); nobody owes anyone the details of their private life.

Where this comes from

  • Neff, L. A., & Karney, B. R. (2009). Stress and reactivity to daily relationship experiences: How stress hinders adaptive processes in marriage. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 97(3), 435–450. Daily-diary studies of newlyweds; correlational. On the research page.
