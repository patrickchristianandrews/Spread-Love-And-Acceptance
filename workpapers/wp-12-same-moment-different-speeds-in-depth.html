This worksheet puts Pillar IV (“Tune how you send and receive”) to work, with help from Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person. In you: you learn your own speed in the moments that matter. Between you and others: a difference in pace becomes something to plan around, not a reason to blame.

Purpose: to map mismatched pace. One person is moving fast, the other is at the end of their capacity and moving slowly, and the conversation produces noise neither of them meant. It is a difference in tempo, not in willingness. This sheet looks at it moment by moment, then turns the biggest gap into one small agreement.

How often: once, together, in about 20 minutes. Again every few months, or after a big change: a new job, a baby, a move, an illness.

Pace, not willingness

When two people meet the same moment at different speeds, each one’s normal can look like a problem to the other. The fast one sees the slow one as stalling or not caring. The slow one sees the fast one as pushy or panicky. Usually neither is true. They are running at different tempos, like two radios tuned a little apart, and the whistle between them sounds like a fight.

The Frequency Framework calls that whistle the beat frequency. This sheet gives you a way to hear it before it turns into one. It doesn’t ask who is right. It asks where you differ, and what small agreement would let both speeds fit.

An honest note: there is no study behind these particular questions. It’s a structured way to talk about a difference most people recognize right away. Treat the gaps as a starting point for a conversation, not a measurement.

Part A, first: choose the moments

The fill-in starts you with five moments that come around in most shared lives:

  • A plan changes at the last minute.
  • A bill, letter or form needs dealing with.
  • Something breaks or goes wrong at home.
  • One of you is upset about something.
  • Deciding what to do with free time.

Change any of them to fit your life. Other moments that often show a gap: a message that needs a reply, getting out of the door in the morning, a visit from family, a big purchase, a work problem one of you brings home. Pick the ones that come around often, and the ones that tend to end in a snap.

Part A: each person’s speed

Each person fills in only their own rows, ideally before seeing the other’s. Answer for how you really are on an ordinary day, not how you think you should be.

QuestionThe choices, in order
Speed: how soon do I want to act or decide?Right away · Within the hour · Later the same day · After a night’s sleep
Talk: how do I like to handle it?Quiet time first · A few words, then act · Talk it all through
Urgency: how pressing does it feel to me?Low · Medium · High
What helps me (optional)In your own words: “a heads-up first,” “a time we’ll decide by,” “ten minutes on my own”

Every answer here is a normal way to be. “Quiet time first” is not avoidance, and “Right away” is not impatience. They are just speeds.

Part B: reading the gaps

For each moment, the sheet counts how many steps apart your answers are on each list. “Right away” and “After a night’s sleep” are three steps apart. “Low” and “High” are two. Two or more steps in total is worth an agreement. It then suggests where to start: the moment with the biggest total gap.

Two things to look for as you read it together:

  • Speed flips. Most people are fast about some things and slow about others. If one of you is fast about plans and the other about money, neither of you is “the slow one.”
  • Urgency gaps hurt most. When one person feels “High” and the other “Low,” the first can feel alone with it. Saying “I can see this feels urgent to you” often helps more than moving faster.

Part C: one small agreement

A good agreement gives the faster person a way to start and the slower person a time to land. Some shapes that work:

  • Flag now, decide later. “When a bill comes, I’ll flag it the same day, and we’ll sort it on Sunday.”
  • A pause with a time on it. “I need a night on this. Can we talk at breakfast?” A pause with a time feels like a plan, not a door closing. The Calm-down plan has more on this.
  • One line first. “When plans change, I’ll say the new plan in one line, then give you an hour before we decide anything.”
  • Split the job. The faster person gathers the facts. The slower person makes the call by an agreed time.

One or two agreements are plenty. Try each for two weeks before you judge it. If a job keeps coming up in these agreements, give it one owner on One owner per job.

The gaps: the bills are 3 steps apart on speed and 2 on urgency. The plan changes are 2 apart on speed and 2 on urgency. When one of them is upset, they are 1 step apart on speed and 2 on talk: Rosa wants to talk it through, Tom needs quiet first.

Their agreements:

  • “When a bill comes, Tom flags it the same day. We sort it together on Sunday morning, not the minute it lands.”
  • “When plans change, Rosa says the new plan in one line, then gives Tom an hour before anything is decided.”
  • “When one of us is upset, Tom gets twenty minutes of quiet, then comes and finds Rosa.”

Two weeks later, the Sunday bill time has stuck. The twenty minutes is harder. They’re trying a text (“I’m coming, give me ten”) so Rosa isn’t left wondering.

Not just for couples

Pace gaps show up anywhere people share things. Roommates: the trash, the bills, a broken boiler. A team: how fast to reply, how much to talk before deciding. A parent and a grown-up child: how soon to sort out a worry. The sheet takes two to eight people. Add a row for each person and moment.

When it goes sideways

  • Turning a speed into a label. “You’re just slow” or “You always panic” undoes the point. The gap belongs to both of you.
  • One person always adapting. If every agreement asks the same person to change speed, look again. A fair agreement asks a little of each of you.
  • Using pace to dodge. “I need time” is fine. “I need time” with no time on it, again and again, is a different conversation. Check-ins can help with that one.

How it fits with the other tools

Rhythms side by side (also called Frequency Calibration) maps your general rhythms in five areas: money, recovery, decisions, check-ins and the big picture. This sheet goes moment by moment, and ends in agreements. Do either first. The 90-second daily check-in then gives you a shared rhythm to hang the agreements on.

Not a compatibility test and not a diagnosis. It describes how you each work right now, not whether you fit.
