Purpose: write down what actually happened this week, before either of you starts remembering it as a story. Fill it in together, or each on your own and then compare. Each of you writes only your own side, never the other person's.

This worksheet has two parts. Part A is a plain log of what happened, written down before anyone decides what it means. Part B is a set of scripts for saying "no" or "not yet" to a task without starting a fight. The totals from Part A go into the Lemonade Stand calculator and into the Verdict Engine's workload_balance input (the Verdict Engine is the program's overall check, explained in Chapter II).

Part B

The Neutral Refusals

An Expanded Scenario Guide

No list of scripts can cover every situation two people might face, and it would be a false promise to claim otherwise. Instead, here is a wide range of common, everyday situations in three groups. Find one close to yours and adapt the wording. The last section shows the pattern underneath, so you can build your own script for anything not covered here.

All three refusal types follow the same three-part shape: Acknowledge the request is real and reasonable, State Capacity honestly, and Offer an Alternative so the other person isn't left with nothing. None of these is meant to end a conversation on your say-so alone. Each one is an opening line. The other person can still answer, push back, or suggest something different.

The Capacity Check

For requests that arrive when you're already full, and saying yes right now would cost you something real.

End-of-day exhaustion
Acknowledge: "I know bedtime is a lot to handle solo."
State Capacity: "I'm running on empty tonight and don't have much left in me."
Offer Alternative: "Can we split it — I'll do bath, you do the rest?"
Overbooked weekend
Acknowledge: "That errand does need to get done this weekend."
State Capacity: "My Saturday's already full, and I don't want to rush through it."
Offer Alternative: "Could it move to Sunday morning, or could you take it this time?"
Being asked for emotional support when you're running low yourself
Acknowledge: "I can tell you need to talk this through."
State Capacity: "I don't have enough left in me to really be there for you right now."
Offer Alternative: "Can we talk after I've had a chance to reset — maybe in an hour?"
A favor passed along from extended family
Acknowledge: "I get why your mom's request feels urgent to you."
State Capacity: "I don't have room to take this on this week."
Offer Alternative: "Can you let her know it'll have to wait, or handle this one yourself?"
Low energy while you're sick or getting over something
Acknowledge: "That project really has been sitting for a while."
State Capacity: "I physically don't have the energy for it today."
Offer Alternative: "Let's pencil it in for this weekend once I'm feeling steadier."
Being asked to handle the finances at the end of a hard week
Acknowledge: "The budget conversation is overdue, you're right."
State Capacity: "My head isn't clear enough for numbers tonight."
Offer Alternative: "Can we set thirty minutes aside this weekend instead, when I can actually focus?"

The Delegation Pivot

For tasks that don't really belong on your side of the list. It's not that you won't help. Someone else is simply better placed to do it.

School details you don't usually handle
Acknowledge: "The school schedule change does need a response today."
State Capacity: "I don't have the context on this the way you do — you've been handling the school stuff."
Offer Alternative: "Can you take this one, or walk me through what's needed so I can?"
Handling things with extended family
Acknowledge: "I know your uncle's visit needs planning."
State Capacity: "I don't know the family dynamics well enough to make the right call here."
Offer Alternative: "You're better placed to decide the details. I'm happy to help carry it out once it's planned."
A task that needs a skill you don't have
Acknowledge: "The car noise does need looking at."
State Capacity: "I genuinely don't know enough about cars to handle this well."
Offer Alternative: "Can you take the lead, and I'll take something else off your plate in exchange?"
Organizing plans for a friend group that isn't yours
Acknowledge: "Planning the trip with your friends does need to happen soon."
State Capacity: "I don't know that group well enough to organize it well."
Offer Alternative: "This one makes more sense as yours to run — let me know how I can help from the sidelines."
Managing another family member's feelings
Acknowledge: "Your dad being upset does need addressing."
State Capacity: "That's not a conversation I can have on your behalf."
Offer Alternative: "I'll support you in having that conversation, but it needs to come from you."
An account or system only the other person manages
Acknowledge: "The insurance renewal does need attention."
State Capacity: "I don't have login access or the history on that account."
Offer Alternative: "Can you handle the renewal itself, and I'll take on a task from your list to even it out?"

The Time Commitment

For tasks you will do, just not on the timeline the other person expects.

Interrupted mid-task
Acknowledge: "I'll fix that today."
State Capacity: "I'm in the middle of something I don't want to drop right now."
Offer Alternative: "I'll get to it in about half an hour, once I'm finished here."
A request during rest time you need
Acknowledge: "That's on my list for this week."
State Capacity: "Tonight is the wind-down time I need before tomorrow."
Offer Alternative: "I'll take care of it first thing in the morning instead."
Wanting to think before committing to specifics
Acknowledge: "I do want to plan the trip with you."
State Capacity: "I don't want to commit to dates on the spot without checking my week."
Offer Alternative: "Let me look tonight and give you a real answer tomorrow."
Needing to finish something before switching tasks
Acknowledge: "I hear that the dishes are piling up."
State Capacity: "I want to finish what I'm working on first so I don't lose my place."
Offer Alternative: "I'll handle them right after — give me fifteen minutes."
A request when your feelings are running too high
Acknowledge: "This conversation matters and I want to have it properly."
State Capacity: "I'm too worked up right now to think clearly or be fair to you."
Offer Alternative: "Can we come back to this in twenty minutes, once I've settled?"
A bigger project that needs its own block of time
Acknowledge: "The garage does need to get organized."
State Capacity: "Squeezing it into a spare hour won't do it justice."
Offer Alternative: "Let's block out Saturday morning for it, so it actually gets done right."

For a situation not listed here: every scenario above uses the same three moves, in this order.

1. Say something true and specific about why the request is reasonable. Not a vague "I understand," but the real need, in plain words.

2. Say honestly what you have left. Not an excuse, and not making it sound worse than it is. Say what's really true about your time, energy, skill, or focus right now.

3. Offer something concrete instead of a flat no: a different time, a different person, part of the job, or a plan to come back to it. That way the other person is never left with nothing, even when the answer isn't "yes, right now."

What a neutral refusal is not: a long list of reasons, a tally of everything you've already done, or a refusal to ever talk about it again. It's one calm statement of your limit that leaves the door open to talk it over again, instead of ending the conversation.
