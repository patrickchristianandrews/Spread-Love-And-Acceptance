This worksheet puts Pillar IV (“Tune how you send and receive”) to work over the long run, with a nod to Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. In you: you notice which small shared moments still matter to you. Between you and others: you keep them alive on purpose, instead of finding out they stopped months ago.

Purpose: long-cycle calibration: the shared touchpoints that keep two people in step across years rather than weeks, and what to do when they’ve quietly lapsed. Every few months, you list your rituals, mark how each is doing, restart one in its smallest form, and, if it’s time, let one go with thanks.

How often: about every three months. The change of season is an easy reminder.

Why little rituals matter

A ritual is a small, repeated thing that means “this is us”: the coffee before work, the wave from the window, the Friday takeaway. In a review of 50 years of research, Barbara Fiese and colleagues found that family routines and rituals were linked with marital satisfaction, parenting confidence and children’s wellbeing. They also noted the weak spots: most studies were correlational, with small or convenience samples. So rituals may help people feel close, or close people may simply keep more rituals. Probably both.

Carol Bruess and Judy Pearson asked married couples and friends about the rituals they share, and found many everyday kinds: shared routines, private jokes and names, ways of celebrating, ways of getting through hard times. Most were small. None needed money or much time.

The trouble is that rituals stop quietly. Nobody decides to end the Sunday walk. A new job moves a shift, and the walk just doesn’t happen, and nobody mentions it. This sheet is a regular, gentle look before “we used to” becomes the whole story.

Part A: what counts as a ritual

Anything small and repeated that you share and would miss. Some to get you started:

  • Daily: coffee together, a goodbye at the door, a goodnight text, “how was your day?” at dinner, the 90-second check-in.
  • Weekly: a walk, a takeaway night, a film, a Sunday phone call, a shared chore with music on.
  • Monthly: a date, a new place, a long call with a faraway friend.
  • Yearly: birthday breakfasts, an anniversary, a holiday tradition, the first swim of summer.
  • Now and then: the way you celebrate good news, the way you look after each other when one of you is ill.

List the ones that have stopped, too. They are often the most useful.

Marking how each one is doing

MarkWhat it means
AliveStill happening, and still means something to you both.
FadingStill happening, but on autopilot, or less often, or with one of you half there.
StoppedIt used to happen, and it doesn’t now.
New ideaSomething you’d like to try.

Rituals lapse for ordinary reasons: a baby, a move, a new job, an illness, a hard year. A stopped ritual isn’t a failure, and nobody is to blame. Write what got in the way, plainly: “stopped when Saturday shifts started”.

Part C: restart one, in its smallest form

Just one at a time. Pick the one you’d both miss most, or a new one you’re both curious about. Then make it small:

  • The smallest version that still counts. Twenty minutes round the park, not the whole hike. A takeaway on the sofa, not a restaurant.
  • The first time. When, and who will suggest it. Rituals that are “whenever” rarely happen.
  • What would make it easy to skip. Phones, tiredness, a clash with something else. Then one way to protect it.

The same few things show up in rituals that last: they are small, regular, easy to keep, both people would miss them, and they can bend on a hard week. Touchstones has more on what makes a ritual healthy, and when one goes sideways.

New ideas count too. In short lab experiments, Arthur Aron and colleagues found that couples who did a new, mildly challenging activity together reported higher relationship quality afterwards than couples who did something routine. A new ritual doesn’t have to be big. One new place a month is plenty.

Part D: let one go, with thanks

Not every ritual needs saving. Some belonged to an earlier season: the quiz night near the old flat, the long Saturday lie-in before the children. Letting one go on purpose, and saying what it gave you, is kinder than letting it fade with guilt attached. It also makes room for the next one.

A filled-in example (made up)

Made up · A quiet Sunday in spring

June and Ray have been together eleven years. Nothing is wrong. But when Ray asks “when did we last go for a walk?”, neither of them can remember. It was before June started working Saturdays.

Where they stand: 5 rituals. Alive: 2 · Fading: 1 · Stopped: 1 · New idea: 1.

Restart: the Sunday walk. Smallest version: twenty minutes round the park after lunch. June suggests it this Sunday. What would make it easy to skip: Ray’s football on the TV. Protect it: go before kick-off.

Let go, with thanks: Thursday quiz night. It was great when they lived near the pub, and it gave them their oldest running joke.

Three months later, the walk is alive again. The Friday film is still fading, so that’s the next one to look at.

For friends, families, housemates and teams

Every lasting group has rituals. Friends: the yearly trip, the group chat birthday song. Families: Sunday dinner, the first-day-of-school photo. Housemates: a monthly house dinner. Teams: Friday cake, the end-of-project lunch. Couples who live apart: the call before bed, the countdown to the next visit. The sheet takes two to eight people.

When it goes sideways

  • A ritual kept out of duty. If one of you dreads it, it isn’t doing its job. Change it or let it go.
  • One person always organising. If the same person always remembers, books and reminds, that’s a job. Give it an owner on One owner per job, or take turns.
  • A missed ritual treated as proof. “You forgot our walk, so you don’t care.” A missed one is just a missed one. See Complacency for the difference between a slip and a drift.

About the name

On the PDF, this sheet is called Harmonic Resonance & Entrainment. Entrainment is the physics of two rhythms falling into step when they are linked. In 1665 Christiaan Huygens noticed that two pendulum clocks hanging from the same beam ended up swinging together. The program borrows the idea for staying in step over years, not weeks. The 90-second daily check-in (WP-13) keeps you in step day to day; this sheet does it season to season.
