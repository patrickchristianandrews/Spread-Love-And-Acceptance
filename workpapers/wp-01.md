# WP-01 — Field Audit & Neutral Refusals

**Purpose:** capture what actually happened this week before either person's memory of it hardens into a story. Fill this in together, or independently and compare — don't fill it in *about* the other person.

## Part A — Field Audit

**Audit rule:** log what happened, not what should have happened. "I noticed the trash needed to go out three times before doing it" is one row, logged honestly, not three rows of resentment.

## Part B — Neutral Refusals: An Expanded Scenario Guide

Half of unbilled debt isn't unequal effort — it's one person being unable to say no to a request without it becoming a conflict. A neutral refusal is a scripted way to decline or defer a task that states the boundary without relitigating whether the request was reasonable.

No fixed set of scripts covers every situation two people might run into — that would be a false promise. What follows is a wide range of common, realistic scenarios across three categories, so you can find something close to your actual situation and adapt the wording. The final section teaches the underlying pattern directly, so you can build your own script for anything not covered here.

All three refusal types follow the same three-part shape: **Acknowledge** the request is real and reasonable, **State Capacity** honestly, and **Offer an Alternative** so the other person isn't left with nothing. None of these are meant to end a conversation unilaterally — they're an opening line, and the other person can still respond, push back, or propose something different.

### The Capacity Check

*For requests arriving after you're already at capacity — when saying yes right now would come at a real cost to you.*

**End-of-day exhaustion**
- Acknowledge: "I know bedtime is a lot to handle solo."
- State Capacity: "I'm running on empty tonight and don't have much left in me."
- Offer Alternative: "Can we split it — I'll do bath, you do the rest?"

**Overbooked weekend**
- Acknowledge: "That errand does need to get done this weekend."
- State Capacity: "My Saturday's already full, and I don't want to rush through it."
- Offer Alternative: "Could it move to Sunday morning, or could you take it this time?"

**Being asked for emotional support while depleted yourself**
- Acknowledge: "I can tell you need to talk this through."
- State Capacity: "I don't have the emotional bandwidth to be fully present right now."
- Offer Alternative: "Can we talk after I've had a chance to reset — maybe in an hour?"

**A relayed favor request from extended family**
- Acknowledge: "I get why your mom's request feels urgent to you."
- State Capacity: "I don't have room to take this on this week."
- Offer Alternative: "Can you let her know it'll have to wait, or handle this one yourself?"

**Low physical energy during illness or recovery**
- Acknowledge: "That project really has been sitting for a while."
- State Capacity: "I physically don't have the energy for it today."
- Offer Alternative: "Let's pencil it in for this weekend once I'm feeling steadier."

**A hard week, being asked to also handle finances**
- Acknowledge: "The budget conversation is overdue, you're right."
- State Capacity: "My head isn't clear enough for numbers tonight."
- Offer Alternative: "Can we set thirty minutes aside this weekend instead, when I can actually focus?"

### The Delegation Pivot

*For tasks that don't actually belong on your side of the register — not because you're refusing to help, but because someone else is genuinely better positioned.*

**School logistics outside your usual domain**
- Acknowledge: "The school schedule change does need a response today."
- State Capacity: "I don't have the context on this the way you do — you've been handling the school stuff."
- Offer Alternative: "Can you take this one, or walk me through what's needed so I can?"

**Extended family relationship management**
- Acknowledge: "I know your uncle's visit needs planning."
- State Capacity: "I don't know the family dynamics well enough to make the right call here."
- Offer Alternative: "You're better positioned to decide the details — I'm happy to help execute once it's planned."

**A task requiring a skill you don't have**
- Acknowledge: "The car noise does need looking at."
- State Capacity: "I genuinely don't know enough about cars to handle this well."
- Offer Alternative: "Can you take the lead, and I'll cover something else off your plate in exchange?"

**Coordinating a friend group that isn't yours**
- Acknowledge: "Planning the trip with your friends does need to happen soon."
- State Capacity: "I don't have the relationships with that group to coordinate it well."
- Offer Alternative: "This one makes more sense as yours to run — let me know how I can support from the side."

**Managing another family member's feelings**
- Acknowledge: "Your dad being upset does need addressing."
- State Capacity: "That's not a conversation I can have on your behalf."
- Offer Alternative: "I'll support you in having that conversation, but it needs to come from you."

**An account or system only the other partner manages**
- Acknowledge: "The insurance renewal does need attention."
- State Capacity: "I don't have login access or the history on that account."
- Offer Alternative: "Can you handle the renewal itself, and I'll take on a task from your list to even it out?"

### The Time Commitment

*For tasks you'll take on — just not on the timeline currently being assumed.*

**Interrupted mid-task**
- Acknowledge: "I'll fix that today."
- State Capacity: "I'm in the middle of something I don't want to drop right now."
- Offer Alternative: "I'll get to it in about half an hour, once I'm finished here."

**A request during needed rest time**
- Acknowledge: "That's on my list for this week."
- State Capacity: "Tonight is the wind-down time I need before tomorrow."
- Offer Alternative: "I'll take care of it first thing in the morning instead."

**Wanting to think before committing to specifics**
- Acknowledge: "I do want to plan the trip with you."
- State Capacity: "I don't want to commit to dates on the spot without checking my week."
- Offer Alternative: "Let me look tonight and give you a real answer tomorrow."

**Needing to finish something before switching tasks**
- Acknowledge: "I hear that the dishes are piling up."
- State Capacity: "I want to finish what I'm working on first so I don't lose my place."
- Offer Alternative: "I'll handle them right after — give me fifteen minutes."

**A request during emotional flooding**
- Acknowledge: "This conversation matters and I want to have it properly."
- State Capacity: "I'm too activated right now to think clearly or be fair to you."
- Offer Alternative: "Can we come back to this in twenty minutes, once I've settled?"

**A larger project better done with dedicated time**
- Acknowledge: "The garage does need to get organized."
- State Capacity: "Squeezing it into a spare hour won't do it justice."
- Offer Alternative: "Let's block out Saturday morning for it, so it actually gets done right."

### Adapting This to a Situation Not Listed Here

The pattern behind every scenario above is the same three moves, in order:

1. Say something true and specific about why the request is reasonable — not a generic "I understand," but naming the actual need.
2. State your actual capacity honestly — not an excuse, and not an overstated inability. Say what's really true about your bandwidth, energy, skill, or timing right now.
3. Offer something concrete in place of a flat no — a different time, a different person, a partial version, or a plan to revisit it. The goal is that the other person is never left with nothing, even when the answer isn't "yes, right now."

**What a neutral refusal is not:** a justification essay, a counter-list of everything you already did, or a refusal to ever revisit the request. It's a single, calm boundary statement that keeps the door open for renegotiation instead of ending the conversation.
