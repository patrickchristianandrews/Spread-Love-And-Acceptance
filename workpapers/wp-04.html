WP-04

The Deficit Audit A Monthly Look-Back at What Keeps Slipping

A monthly look back over four weeks of WP-01 logs, to find the tasks that keep slipping.

This worksheet puts Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person to work, with a nod to Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. In you: you look for what in the setup lets things slip, instead of blaming yourself. Between you and others: repeat problems get fixed in the setup, and you catch jobs quietly drifting back to one person.

Part A — Gather Part B — Sort Part C — Wrap Up Not For

Purpose: a monthly look-back, done together. You look across four weeks of WP-01 logs (who did what) (WP-01) and your RACI Treaty (WP-03) to find the tasks that keep slipping. (Here, a "deficit" simply means a job that needed doing and didn't get done.) The goal is not to tally what either of you "owes." It's to find where the household's setup needs a real fix, instead of another one-off patch.

How often: monthly. Not weekly, because this audit looks for patterns you can't see from one week to the next.

Part A

Gather the Month's Notes

Before you start, run WP-02. This is a look back, together, at four weeks of dropped balls. Of all the sessions in the suite, it is the one most likely to go badly. If either of you has a high battery score, move the audit to another day. A month-old pattern will keep.

List every task that was logged as noticed but not done, or done only after being raised more than once, in any week this month. Use whichever record you actually keep:

  • Four weekly WP-01 logs (who did what) — if you log a full audit every week, this is your richest source.
  • The WP-13 daily loop log — if you only run a full WP-01 log once a quarter, use the "one small thing that didn't feel great" line from your daily check-ins as the month's record. It runs every day, which is exactly what this audit needs.

Either one works. You don't need to keep a WP-01 log going forever to run this. Logging forever turns a quick check into one more chore, and extra chores are where all of this started.

Put a check in a week's box if the task was noticed as a gap that week (not done, or raised more than once before it got done).

Twice or more is the cutoff. Those are the tasks you sort in Part B. Three or four times is a strong signal: a task flagged nearly every week is a problem with the setup, not a fluke.

Structural gap

There's no clear owner, or the named owner isn't the person who actually handles it.

Fix: update the RACI Treaty (WP-03) in this session.

Capacity issue

It has a named owner, but that person hasn't been able to keep up with it.

Fix: an honest conversation, using a neutral refusal or a handover (WP-01, WP-09). Not a private promise to just try harder.

Standard in question

Nobody has done it in four weeks, and nothing went wrong. The task may reflect a standard only one of you holds, rather than something the household actually needs.

Fix: talk about whether the task is needed, and how often. Don't talk about who failed to do it. If it is needed, treat it as a structural gap and give it an owner. If it isn't, take it off the treaty and stop counting it as a gap.

One-off, no action

Flagged more than once, but tied to a clear, temporary cause (illness, travel, a busy month at work).

Fix: note it and move on. Don't let a temporary cause push you into a permanent change.

When a fix doesn't hold, it usually means the owner was named but the conditions weren't set up: no reminder, no time, no realistic slot in the week. Handing it to the other person rarely helps. Ask what would have to be true for it to happen, not who should have made it happen.

Then: wrap up the month

  1. Update the RACI Treaty (WP-03) for every task sorted as a structural gap.
  2. For anything sorted as a capacity issue, set a time for that conversation. This audit brings it to light, but it doesn't replace the real conversation.
  3. For anything sorted as a standard in question, decide now: either it gets an owner and a schedule, or it comes off the treaty. If you leave it undecided, you'll be arguing about it again next month.
  4. Below, record how many structural gaps you found this month. A count that falls month after month is the clearest sign this program is working. A rising count means something bigger changed, and it may need its own conversation.

Scope

What This Audit Is Not For

  • Not a bill for the past. Past hours don't get added up and "charged" to anyone. The audit looks back only to find the pattern, and it only makes changes going forward.
  • Not a performance review. A task with no owner is a gap in the treaty, not a verdict on the person who happened to keep covering it.
  • Not a substitute for the weekly closing. This audit works because the weekly routine (the Lemonade Stand check and a quick look at the RACI Treaty) keeps giving it real data. Skip the weekly checks, and this audit gets far less accurate.

Feeds into: the ownership_clarity_score used by the Verdict Engine (Chapter II). Assigning an owner to a task already on the treaty raises that score the following month.

Expect a well-run audit to lower the score first. Ownership clarity is the number of tasks with an owner, divided by the total number of tasks listed. Adding five truly invisible tasks to the treaty adds five rows to the total. So in the month you finally bring hidden work to light, the number can drop even though nothing got worse.

That's the score working as it should. Before, it was measuring a treaty that didn't know about those tasks. A lower, honest number beats a higher one worked out from an incomplete list. It should recover over the next month or two as the new rows get owners.

The same rule as WP-03 applies: never raise the score by deleting rows. Removing a task because the household truly doesn't need it is a Part B decision, made on purpose and written down. It's not a way to improve the math.
