Know yourself: next, shaping who you are becoming

Paperback textRead the expanded version

You aren’t finished, and some of who you become is up to you. This chapter is about choosing a direction and building small steps that still work on a tired day.

In the paperbacks: Book 1, Know Yourself Kindly, Chapter 3.

Wednesday, 9:10pm

The sneakers have been by the front door since Sunday, laces tucked in, pointing outward like a promise. You were going to walk after dinner, and dinner was two hours ago. Monday, you think. Monday has a nice clean feel to it.

Seen fast, a missed walk is three things stacked up. On the surface, sneakers by the door. Underneath, a plan sized for your best day rather than your worst. Underneath that, the old voice that says you never stick to anything. The roots here are feedback loops and balance. The rest of the chapter takes them in turn.

  1. Start with what matters to you.

    A goal built on somebody else’s idea of a good life is very hard to keep going with. So before you pick one, ask what you want your days to feel like, and write two or three plain words. Calm, say, or close to family, or steady, or making things, and whatever they are, they give you a direction to face instead of a score to hit. If you’re not sure what fills you up and what drains you, How you connect can help you notice.

    The week their youngest went off to college, Marion and Keith each wrote two words on the back of a gas bill, hers saying “useful, outdoors” and his “calm, curious,” and Keith looked at hers for a long while before he said, “That’s why you keep going on about the bird hut.” She had been eyeing the volunteers’ hut at the nature reserve since February. He had been spending a suspicious amount of time staring at the vegetable garden (see Empty nest). Write yours in pencil, since they may well change.

    Dig deeper: values first

  2. Pick one small goal, in plain words.

    Just one, and say it as something you’ll do. “Walk for ten minutes after dinner” can go in a diary. “Get healthier” is a hope, and hopes are lovely, but you can’t do anything with one on a Tuesday. Your own actions are up to you, while how people respond, and how it all turns out, aren’t.

    A student in a shared room in the dorms wanted to be “less messy,” which her roommate had heard before. As an action it became “clothes in the basket before bed.” She managed it about four nights in seven, and her roommate said that was four more than last semester (see Sharing a room). Try rewriting one wish so it starts “I will…”

    Dig deeper: one goal, in plain words

  3. Name what gets in the way.

    This is where Then and Now earn their keep. The obstacle might be your wiring, and a plan that ignores how you’re built was made for somebody else. It might be an old rule (“rest has to be earned” is a popular one), or it might simply be a tired Tuesday. Whichever it is, change the arrangement and leave yourself alone.

    Kofi kept missing his evening run and had more or less decided the problem was his willpower, when the truth was that he works in a warehouse on a schedule that changes every single week, so “evenings” meant something different every Monday and his plan never stood a chance. Once he tied the run to his first day off, whenever it fell, he stopped missing it. He’s still slightly annoyed it took him a year to see (see Different hours).

    Dig deeper: what gets in the way

  4. Make the step tiny, and repeatable.

    It needs to be small enough to survive your worst week, which means “Put my shoes on” counts, and I do mean that. Tie it to something you already do, and do it often. The 90-second daily check-in in Part Two, Chapter I runs on the same idea, that small and often outlasts big and rare, and Start in 10 minutes shows how small a whole path can be.

    Six months after the breakup, Ade wanted to “get back out there,” which is far too big a thing to start on a Sunday night. What he actually did was send one message to one friend every Sunday, usually from the sofa during the weather forecast and usually something feeble like “you watching this?” and after a few weeks two of the friends started replying with whole paragraphs (see On my own). If you like a game for this sort of thing, the Re-check Drive turns small steps into calm football. If your step takes more than two minutes, shrink it again.

  5. Decide ahead with a simple if-then.

    “If it’s after dinner, I put my shoes on.” Deciding ahead means one decision fewer on a tired day. It’s the same thinking as the Calm-down plan, made while you’re calm.

    If-then plans hold up This is one of the better-supported ideas in the book. Across 94 tests, Gollwitzer and Sheeran found that plans in the form “when X happens, I’ll do Y” had a medium-to-large effect on people reaching their goals, though the size varied with the task.

    Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006) · Strong: one of the more robust findings in behavior change

    Handovers with her ex had been going badly for a year, so Kerry made a rule for the car. When she pulls up outside his house she turns the engine off and takes three slow breaths before she opens the door. It doesn’t make him any less irritating, but it does mean she gets out of the car as herself (see Co-parenting).

    Pick a cue that happens every day anyway, like the kettle or brushing your teeth. If the if-then doesn’t work, change it.

    Dig deeper: if-then plans

  6. Ask for support, as information.

    Tell one person what you’re trying and what would help. “I’m trying to walk after dinner. Would you ask me how it’s going on Sunday?” They can say no, and you don’t need anybody’s permission to start anyway.

    Since her husband’s stroke, Glenys has done nearly all of his care, and for months she said “I’m fine” whenever her brother asked, until in the end she asked him something so specific that it would have been very hard to refuse. “Could you sit with him Thursdays, two till four, so I can swim?” He said yes before she’d finished, and he brings cookies. She’s swum eleven Thursdays out of fourteen, which she thinks is pretty good.

    Dig deeper: asking for support

  7. Look back kindly once a month.

    This is the monthly look-back from Part Two, Chapter V, pointed at yourself instead of a household. About twenty minutes, once a month, asking what went well and what kept slipping. Was the gap in the plan, in what you had to give that month, or in one strange week? You don’t add up old misses, because the only question is what to try next month. Its workpaper, What keeps coming back?, is written for a shared home, but the two questions work fine on your own.

    Pauline is raising her two grandchildren. On the first of March she sat down with a pot of tea and a pen and saw, fairly quickly, that bedtime was working and homework very much wasn’t, so in March homework moved to the kitchen table straight after a snack, before the TV went on. It worked for one child, and the other one, she says, is “a work in progress, like me.”

  8. Kindness to yourself is part of the plan.

    There’s an old image of the second arrow that I find useful here. The first arrow is the slip itself. The second is the scolding you give yourself afterward, which hurts more and helps less. A plan that needs you to be harsh with yourself is mostly second arrows. Rest belongs inside the plan, and you can shrink a goal or drop it whenever you like.

    Three parts of being kind to yourself Kristin Neff, who defined self-compassion for researchers, splits it into kindness instead of harsh judgment, remembering that everyone struggles instead of feeling alone with it, and noticing a painful feeling without being swallowed by it.

    Neff (2003) · Moderate: widely studied and promising; much of the research is correlational

    Harvey is sixteen, and he planned to study every night, missed three in a row, and announced at breakfast that he was useless and might as well not bother. His grandma went on buttering toast. “What would you tell Kian?” she said. Harvey rolled his eyes so hard it looked painful. That evening he did twenty minutes before dinner, and said nothing about it to anyone.

    Dig deeper: kindness as part of the plan

  9. Set goals inside your circle, and spend energy where it comes back.

    A goal that needs someone else to change is really a wish. You might wish your sister called more, but that’s outside your circle. Calling her on the first Sunday of the month, and telling her you’d love her to call too, is inside it. Then treat your energy like the limited thing it is. Put more into the people and plans that give something back, and less into the ones that only take, so you’ve still got some left when you get near the goal.

The first of the month

You open your notes app. Of the four small things you tried, two have stuck and two quietly stopped somewhere in the second week. You still don’t know why the Saturday swim fell away. You liked the Saturday swim, as far as you can remember, so you put a question mark next to it and close the app.

Two ways to read this

Small steps

Some minds find a fixed small routine restful, because once it’s set, nothing needs deciding. Others find the same routine dull within a week and do better with a little variety built in. If you’re the first kind, a friend who keeps changing the plan isn’t being unreliable. Their brain is hunting for something new. If you’re the second, the friend who wants it the same every time isn’t being rigid. Sameness is how they save energy for everything else.

Where the fields meet. Behavioral science says habits beat good intentions, and that a routine too small to skip keeps going on the worst day. Finance adds a quieter idea, the reserve: small steps kept up become a margin you can draw on when a bad week comes.

You, in all your relationships

A goal for yourself nearly always brushes against other people, and small steps work everywhere. With a partner, offer one small habit instead of demanding one. With friends, one message a week can keep a friendship warm for years. In a family it might be a regular call, a visit you can actually keep up, or a first step after a long silence (see Reaching out). At work it could be one comment per meeting, and with money, one small saving habit that’s yours alone.

Common mix-ups

The commonest, and the least true, is that a goal has to be big to count. Small and repeatable is what survives a bad week, and most big goals turn out to be a lot of small ones in a trench coat. Some people think that once they understand themselves they should be able to change anything. Wiring stays, though, so the aim is a goal that fits how you’re built. Asking for support doesn’t mean you couldn’t manage, it just gives people what they need to help, and “I’ll start on Monday” isn’t always a dodge either. A fresh start can genuinely help, though the tiny version you could do today counts just as much.

A note on care: This part is general education. It isn’t therapy or coaching, and it can’t promise any result. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared. If a goal ever starts to feel like a weight, you can shrink it or put it down.

If you want to start tonight, write your two words, one goal as something you’ll do, the smallest first step you can think of and one if-then. Then put twenty minutes in the calendar for a month from now. Your self-discovery path and Start in 10 minutes are free on the site.

What to remember

  • Start with what matters to you.
  • Name what gets in the way.
  • Decide ahead with a simple if-then.
  • Look back kindly once a month.
  • Set goals inside your circle, and spend energy where it comes back.
