You aren’t finished, and some of who you become is up to you. This part is about choosing a direction, and building small steps that still work on a tired day.

Five Pillars: This part mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars? (They are five plain ideas the whole program is built on.)

Wednesday, 9:10pm

You meant to start walking after dinner. You meant to last week, too. Tonight you’re tired, the shoes are by the door, and you tell yourself you’ll begin on Monday.

  1. Start with what matters to you.

    A goal built on someone else’s idea of success is hard to keep up. Before you pick anything, ask: what do I want my days to be like? Write two or three plain words, like calm, close to my family, steady or creative. They are a direction, not a score. Wavelength can help you notice what fills you up and what drains you.

    Dig deeper: values first

  2. Pick one small goal, in plain words.

    Just one. Say it as something you will do, not something you hope will happen: “Walk for ten minutes after dinner,” not “get healthier.” Your own actions are up to you. How other people respond, and how things turn out, are not.

    Dig deeper: one goal, in plain words

  3. Name what gets in the way.

    Use what you learned in Then and Now. Is it your wiring (a plan that ignores how you’re built is built for someone else)? An old rule (“rest has to be earned”)? Or today’s conditions (a tired evening)? Then change the arrangement, never yourself: move the step to the morning, not the person to a better version.

    Dig deeper: what gets in the way

  4. Make the step tiny, and repeatable.

    Small enough to survive your worst week. “Put my shoes on” counts. Tie it to something you already do, and do it often. This is the same idea as the 90-second daily check-in in Chapter I: small and often beats big and rare. Start in 10 minutes shows how small a whole path can be.

    Dig deeper: tiny, repeatable steps

  5. Decide ahead with a simple if-then.

    “If it’s after dinner, then I put my shoes on.” Deciding ahead means one less decision on a tired day, the same idea as the Calm-Down Kit (WP-11), which you make while calm. If a plan isn’t working, that’s information about the plan, not about you. Change the plan.

    Dig deeper: if-then plans

  6. Ask for support, as information.

    Tell one person what you’re trying and what would help: “I’m trying to walk after dinner. Could you ask me about it on Sunday?” It isn’t a demand, they can say no, and you don’t need their agreement to start. Some goals are better with a qualified professional, and asking for one is a sensible step, if and when you want that.

    Dig deeper: asking for support

  7. Look back kindly once a month.

    It mirrors Chapter V, pointed at yourself. Once a month, take ten minutes: what went well? What kept slipping? Was the gap in the plan, in how much you have right now, or just a one-off week? You don’t add up old misses. You only ask what to try next month.

    Dig deeper: a monthly look at yourself

  8. Kindness to yourself is part of the plan.

    A plan that needs you to be harsh with yourself is built on a second arrow. A missed day is information. Rest is part of the plan, not a prize for finishing. You can shrink a goal, change it or drop it. Nothing here is required, and you can stop any time.

    Dig deeper: kindness as part of the plan

  9. Set goals inside your circle, and spend energy where it comes back.

    A goal that depends on someone else changing is a wish. Shape each goal around what you can do: “I’ll ask once, kindly, by Friday,” not “They’ll start helping.” Then budget your energy like the limited thing it is. Put more into the people and plans that give back, and less into the ones that only take. That isn’t selfish. It’s how you keep going long enough to reach the goal, and still have something left to give.

    Dig deeper: planning inside your circle

The ideas, one at a time

Each idea above again, with a real-life example, words you could use and one small step. Read one and stop if you like.

1. Start with what matters

For example: After their youngest leaves home, a couple each write two words. Hers are “useful, outdoors.” His are “calm, curious.” Suddenly it makes sense that she wants to volunteer and he wants the garden. See Kids grown and gone.

Words you could use: “What I want my days to feel like is ___.”

Try this: Write two plain words for what matters to you right now. Pencil is fine. You can change them.

2. One small goal, in plain words

For example: A student in a shared dorm room wants “to be less messy.” As an action, it becomes “Put my clothes in the basket before bed.” See Sharing a room.

Words you could use: “My one goal this month is ___.”

Try this: Rewrite one wish as something you will do, starting with “I will…”

3. What gets in the way

For example: A shift worker keeps missing his evening run. It isn’t laziness. His rota (his work timetable) changes every week. He ties the run to “my first day off” instead of “evenings.” See Different hours.

Words you could use: “It’s not that I don’t care. The setup doesn’t fit yet.”

Try this: Pick one thing that keeps slipping. Ask: is it my wiring, an old rule, or today’s conditions?

4. Tiny and repeatable

For example: Someone on their own after a breakup wants to “get back out there.” The tiny step: one message to one friend every Sunday. See On my own.

Words you could use: “I’m starting really small, on purpose.”

Try this: Shrink your step until it takes less than two minutes.

5. Decide ahead

For example: A separated parent wants calmer handovers. Her if-then: “If I pull up outside, then I take three slow breaths before I get out of the car.” See Co-parenting.

Words you could use: “If ___ happens, then I’ll ___.”

Try this: Write one if-then. Pick a cue that happens every day, like the kettle, the front door or brushing your teeth.

6. Ask for support

For example: A woman caring for her husband after a stroke asks her brother, “Could you sit with him on Thursday afternoons so I can swim?” Because it is specific, it is easy to say yes or no. See When one of you is ill.

Words you could use: “I’m trying ___. Would you be up for ___? It’s fine to say no.”

Try this: Name one person and one small, specific thing they could do. (If someone uses your goals to check up on you or control you, Not safe at home? has people to talk to.)

7. Look back kindly

For example: A grandmother raising her two grandchildren looks back on the month. Bedtime is working. Homework isn’t. Next month she tries homework at the kitchen table straight after a snack. See Foster and kinship families.

Words you could use: “What went well, what kept slipping, and what will I try next?”

Try this: Put ten minutes in your calendar for the first of next month.

8. Kindness is part of the plan

For example: A sixteen-year-old sets a goal to revise every night, misses three nights and says, “I’m useless.” His grandmother asks, “What would you tell your best friend?” He shrinks the goal to twenty minutes.

Words you could use: “That was a missed day, not a missed life.”

Try this: Next time you miss a step, say one sentence to yourself that you would say to a friend.

9. Inside your circle

For example: You’d like your adult sister to call more. That goal sits outside your circle. Inside it: “I’ll call on the first Sunday of each month, and tell her I’d love her to call too.” See Family rifts.

Words you could use: “I’d love it if ___. Either way, I’ll ___.”

Try this: Rewrite one goal that depends on someone else, so the next step is yours.

The first of the month

You open your notes. Of the four small things you tried, two stuck and two quietly stopped. Nothing went wrong. You just know now which two need a different setup.

You, in all your relationships

A goal for yourself often touches the people around you. Small steps work everywhere.

  • With yourself: Rest, health, a hobby you dropped, a kinder inner voice.
  • With partners: One small habit you offer, not one you demand. See Gaming and time together.
  • With friends: One message a week. See Friends.
  • With family: A regular call, a visit you can keep up, or a first step after years apart. See Reaching out.
  • At work: One comment per meeting, one clear handover note.
  • With money: One small saving habit you control. See Money together.
  • On your own: A week that suits you. See On my own.

Find your own situation in help by relationship.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Treat your own routines as a setup you can adjust, not a character test (Pillar II). Notice the defaults and cues that steer you, and let the easy path be the one you want (Pillar V). Pick the step for the energy you really have (Pillar III).
  • Between you and others: Say what you’re trying and what would help, as information. Let people’s answers be theirs, and fix the setup, not the person.

The fields behind this part

This part draws on 6 of the author’s thirteen fields (see the Preface or the About page):

  • Behavioral science (Pillar V): Habits beat willpower, especially when you’re tired. Cues and defaults steer us more than intentions do.
  • Psychology (Pillar IV): What matters to you, and what you tell yourself when a day goes wrong.
  • Philosophy (Pillar IV): What Is Up to Us, and Aristotle’s idea that we become what we repeatedly do.
  • Business (Pillar II): A small, regular review catches problems while they are small.
  • Economics (Pillar I): Attention is limited, so one small goal beats five big ones.
  • Holistic therapies (Pillar III): Small repeated acts are rhythms, and rest is part of the rhythm.

Two fields, one idea: Philosophy + Behavioral science. Aristotle thought we become what we repeatedly do. Character, like a habit, is built by small acts, not by one big decision.

In real life

  • Partners: One partner wants calmer evenings. They pick one small step, no phones at the table for ten minutes, and say so: “I’m trying this. Would you try it with me?” The other can say yes, not yet or no.
  • Family: You’d like to be in touch with a sibling more. “If it’s Sunday after lunch, then I call for ten minutes” is easier to keep than “I should call more.”
  • Roommates: You want the kitchen to stay clear. Your part is a setup, not willpower: “If I make a drink, then I wash the cup before I sit down.”
  • Coworkers: You’d like to speak up more in meetings. One comment per meeting, written down beforehand from the agenda, is a goal you can actually do.

Common mix-ups

  • “A goal has to be big to count.” Small and repeatable is what survives a bad week. Big goals are often just many small ones.
  • “If I miss a day, I’ve failed.” A missed day tells you about the plan. Ask what got in the way, then adjust the setup.
  • “If I know myself, I should be able to change anything.” Wiring stays. The aim is a goal that fits how you’re built, not a different you.
  • “Asking for support means I couldn’t manage alone.” It’s information that helps others help. People who ask are using the setup well.
  • “A monthly look-back means grading myself.” It’s a look, not a trial. It never adds up old misses.
  • “I’ll start on Monday.” A fresh start can help, but today’s tiny version counts too. Put your shoes on now.

A note on care: This part is general education. It isn’t therapy, coaching or medical advice, and it can’t promise any result. Goals about your health, money, work or a hard stretch in life may deserve a qualified professional, and there’s no shame in asking for one. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared. If a goal ever starts to feel like a weight, you can shrink it or put it down.
