Know yourself: next, shaping who you are becoming

Simple versionRead the full version

You aren’t finished, and some of who you become is up to you. This chapter is about choosing a direction and building small steps that still work on a tired day.

In short

  • Start with what matters to you in two or three plain words, then pick one small goal you can actually do.
  • Make the step tiny and decide ahead, as in “If it’s after dinner, then I put my shoes on.”
  • Ask for support as information, and look back kindly once a month.
  • A missed day tells you about the plan, so change the plan and keep going.
  • Set goals inside your circle, and spend your energy where it comes back.

Wednesday, 9:10pm

You meant to start walking after dinner, and you meant to last week too. Tonight you are tired, the shoes are by the door where you left them on Sunday, and you tell yourself you will begin on Monday.

  1. Start with what matters to you.

    A goal built on someone else’s idea of success is hard to keep up. Before you pick anything, ask what you want your days to be like, and write two or three plain words, such as calm, close to my family, steady or creative. They give you a direction to face rather than a score to reach. How you connect can help you notice what fills you up and what drains you.

    For example, after their youngest leaves home, a couple each write two words. Hers are “useful, outdoors” and his are “calm, curious”, which suddenly explains why she wants to volunteer at the nature reserve while he wants to dig over the vegetable patch (see Kids grown and gone).

    Try this. Write two plain words for what matters to you right now, in pencil if you like, since you can change them.

    Dig deeper: values first

  2. Pick one small goal, in plain words.

    Choose just one, and say it as something you will do. “Walk for ten minutes after dinner” is a goal you can keep, while “get healthier” is a hope. Your own actions are up to you, and how other people respond and how things turn out are not. A student sharing a room in halls wants to be less messy, and as an action it becomes putting her clothes in the basket before bed (see Sharing a room).

    This week. Rewrite one wish as something you will do, starting with “I will…”

    Dig deeper: one goal, in plain words

  3. Name what gets in the way.

    Use what you learned in Then and Now. The obstacle may be your wiring, since a plan that ignores how you are built was made for someone else. It may be an old rule, such as “rest has to be earned”, or simply today’s conditions, like a tired evening. Then change the arrangement and leave yourself alone, for example by moving the step to the morning. A shift worker kept missing his evening run, which had nothing to do with laziness, because his rota changed every week. Once he tied the run to his first day off, he stopped missing it (see Different hours).

    Dig deeper: what gets in the way

  4. Make the step tiny, and repeatable.

    Make it small enough to survive your worst week, so that “put my shoes on” counts. Tie it to something you already do, and do it often. The 90-second daily check-in in Part Two, Chapter I works on the same idea, that something small and frequent lasts longer than something big and rare, and Ten-minute start shows how small a whole path can be. Someone on their own after a breakup wants to get back out there, and the tiny step they choose is one message to one friend every Sunday (see On my own).

    One small step. Shrink your step until it takes less than two minutes.

  5. Decide ahead with a simple if-then.

    “If it’s after dinner, then I put my shoes on.” Deciding ahead means one decision fewer on a tired day, which is the same idea as the Calm-Down Kit, made while you are calm. A separated parent who wants calmer handovers decides that when she pulls up outside, she will take three slow breaths before she gets out of the car (see Co-parenting). If a plan isn’t working, change the plan.

    Something to try. Write one if-then, using a cue that happens every day, such as the kettle, the front door or brushing your teeth.

    Dig deeper: if-then plans

  6. Ask for support, as information.

    Tell one person what you are trying and what would help, as in “I’m trying to walk after dinner. Could you ask me about it on Sunday?” They can say no, and you don’t need their agreement to start. A woman caring for her husband after a stroke asks her brother whether he could sit with him on Thursday afternoons so she can swim, and because the request is so specific, it is easy for him to answer. Some goals are better shared with a qualified professional, and asking for one is a sensible step whenever you want it.

    Try this. Name one person and one small, specific thing they could do. (If someone uses your goals to check up on you or control you, Not safe at home? has people to talk to.)

    Dig deeper: asking for support

  7. Look back kindly once a month.

    This is the monthly look-back from Part Two, Chapter V, pointed at yourself. Once a month, take ten minutes to ask what went well and what kept slipping, and whether the gap was in the plan, in how much you had to give that month or in one unusual week. Old misses don’t get added up, and the only question is what to try next month. A grandmother raising her two grandchildren looks back and sees that bedtime is working and homework isn’t, so next month she tries homework at the kitchen table straight after a snack.

    This week. Put ten minutes in your calendar for the first of next month.

  8. Kindness to yourself is part of the plan.

    A plan that needs you to be harsh with yourself rests on what is sometimes called the second arrow. The first arrow is the slip itself, and the second is the telling-off you give yourself afterwards, which hurts more and helps less. Rest belongs inside the plan, and you can shrink a goal, change it or drop it at any time. A sixteen-year-old who planned to revise every night misses three nights and calls himself useless. His grandmother asks what he would tell his best friend, and he shrinks the goal to twenty minutes before tea.

    One small step. Next time you miss a step, say one sentence to yourself that you would say to a friend.

    Dig deeper: kindness as part of the plan

  9. Set goals inside your circle, and spend energy where it comes back.

    A goal that depends on someone else changing is really a wish. Shape each goal around what you can do, such as asking once, kindly, by Friday. You might wish your adult sister called more often, which sits outside your circle, while calling her on the first Sunday of each month and telling her you would love her to call too sits inside it. Then budget your energy like the limited thing it is, putting more into the people and plans that give back and less into the ones that only take, so that you keep going long enough to reach the goal.

    Something to try. Rewrite one goal that depends on someone else, so that the next step is yours.

    Dig deeper: planning inside your circle

The first of the month

You open your notes. Of the four small things you tried, two have stuck and two quietly stopped somewhere around the second week. You still don’t know why the Saturday swim fell away, and you write a question mark next to it for next month.

You, in all your relationships

A goal for yourself often touches the people around you, and small steps work everywhere. With a partner, offer one small habit rather than demanding one, and with friends, one message a week can be enough. In a family it might be a regular call, a visit you can keep up or a first step after years apart (see Reaching out). At work it could be one comment per meeting, and with money, one small saving habit you control.

Common mix-ups

  • “A goal has to be big to count.” Small and repeatable is what survives a bad week, and big goals are often just many small ones.
  • “If I know myself, I should be able to change anything.” Wiring stays, so the aim is a goal that fits how you are built.
  • “Asking for support means I couldn’t manage alone.” Asking gives other people the information they need to help.
  • “I’ll start on Monday.” A fresh start can help, but today’s tiny version counts too.

A note on care: This part is general education. It isn’t therapy, coaching or medical advice, and it can’t promise any result. Goals about your health, money, work or a hard stretch in life may deserve a qualified professional, and there’s no shame in asking for one. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared. If a goal ever starts to feel like a weight, you can shrink it or put it down.

To start, write two plain words for what matters to you, one small goal as something you will do, the tiniest first step and one if-then. Then put a ten-minute look-back on the calendar for a month from now. Your self-discovery path and Ten-minute start are free on the site.
