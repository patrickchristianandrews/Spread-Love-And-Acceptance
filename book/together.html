If someone handed you this book

Maybe it was left on your side of the bed with a page turned down. Maybe someone said, “Could you just read this bit?” Either way, you didn’t choose it. Fair enough. Here is the short version, written for you.

This isn’t a case against you

The book starts from something researchers have found again and again: when two people each estimate their share of the housework, the two answers usually add up to more than 100%. Nobody is lying. You see every minute of your own effort and only some of theirs, and so do they (Chapter II, page 44). So the book never asks who the bad guy is. Its numbers score the setup, never a person.

What’s in it for you

  • Fewer repeat arguments about the same jobs.
  • Jobs that are clearly yours, so nobody needs to remind you about them.
  • Credit for what you already do. Your jobs go on the list too: the car, the trash, the late-night lifts, the things you fix.
  • A say in what “fair” means in your house. It isn’t always 50/50 (Chapter IV, page 80).

It doesn’t ask for therapy talk, and you never fill anything in about the other person. You can say no.

A first week: three small things

  1. Log your own week. Rough minutes on “Who did what,” including the thinking part: booking, remembering, planning. Ten minutes in all. Only yours, never theirs.
  2. Ask one question, and just listen. “What’s the one job you’d most like to stop thinking about?” Don’t defend or count. Write the answer down.
  3. Take over that job, the whole of it, including the noticing (below).

Words to open the talk

“I read the bit you gave me. I don’t agree with all of it, but I get the point about the stuff nobody sees.”
“I’d rather own a couple of things properly than ‘help’ with everything. Which one would you most like off your mind?”
“Give me a week to see what I actually do. Then let’s look together.”

Best left out, for now: “Just tell me what to do” (it hands the thinking straight back), “You should have asked” and, in the first five minutes, “I do loads too.” That may be true. Save it for when you both have your logs.

Taking over a whole job, noticing included

Every job has a part you can see (doing it) and parts you can’t: noticing it needs doing, remembering, planning and checking it got done (the Preface, page 16). Taking a job over means all of it.

  1. Pick one job that repeats: the children’s clothes and shoes, the trash, the car, school forms, the dentist.
  2. Get the handover once. What it involves, when it’s due, where things are, who to call. Ask your questions now, not every week.
  3. Move the remembering out of their head. A phone reminder, the calendar, a list on the fridge: anywhere but the other person.
  4. Do it start to finish for a month without being reminded. If you need reminding, fix your reminder, not the relationship.
  5. Do it your way. If the result is fine, the method is yours. Their part is to let it be done differently.
  6. Ask for a backstop if you like: someone who notices a slip and says so once, kindly. A backstop isn’t a supervisor (Chapter II, page 44).

A made-up example. Hester has always looked after the children’s clothes: spotting toes at the end of shoes, school sizes, swimming kit, passing on what’s outgrown. Wilf takes it over. Hester writes the handover note: sizes, the shop, which drawer holds the next size up. Wilf sets a reminder for the first Sunday of each month: “Shoes, uniform, kit.” In week two he misses the gym clothes for a school trip. Hester says once, “The trip kit is Thursday,” and he adds “school letters” to his Sunday check. A month in, Hester says the biggest change is that she no longer thinks about shoes at eleven at night.

If you think it’s already fair

Log your week anyway. If it comes out even, that is useful to know, and the book will say so: a fair score doesn’t silence anyone, and a low one doesn’t blame anyone. And if this book feels like the wrong place to start, Find it fast, page 7 lists other ways in.

At a glance

  • Running a home takes unseen work, the noticing, remembering and planning that many people call the mental load.
  • The other person usually just hasn’t seen it. This work hides by nature, and missing it says little about how much someone cares.
  • Each of you writes only your own side of one ordinary week, and you read the two lists together.

Sunday, 9pm

The laundry has been in the machine since Saturday morning and will need doing again, because it has that smell. The school trip form is due Tuesday. The birthday card for Aunt Jean is on the hall table, still in its cellophane, unsigned. Somebody in the house has noticed all three of these things, and it is probably you.

Kin work The anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo called the cards, calls, visits and presents that keep a wider family connected “kin work.” In the families she studied it was done mostly by women and treated as if it took no effort at all. The unsigned card on the hall table is a small piece of it.

di Leonardo (1987) · Moderate: an influential qualitative study

Somebody has also noticed that nobody else has noticed. From the hallway the house looks fine, which is exactly the trouble.

Quickly: a birthday card on the hall table is the surface. Underneath, the noticing that it needs signing. Underneath that, a whole list held in one head. The roots are balance and limited capacity. The chapters that follow slow down on each part.

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment before it turns into a fight. None of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you, and over months it piles up. This book calls the pile unbilled debt, because nobody ever sends a bill for it. (It’s a ledger word, and I make no apology for it. I spent my career as a government auditor, where a control is just an agreement about how a thing gets done, who owns it and how anyone would notice if it stopped. Who notices that the milk is low is a control in your home too, and if it only works because the same person quietly catches it every time, it was never really set up.) You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled.

    The second shift Arlie Hochschild’s interviews and home visits with working couples found mothers doing far more of the housework and childcare, a second shift on top of the paid one, and many couples telling themselves a story in which the split was fair. Large time-use surveys since show the gap persisting, and narrowing.

    Hochschild with Machung (1989) · Moderate: an influential qualitative study; its figures describe its own sample

    Marcus remembers every school form, every dentist appointment and every bit of lunch money for their two children. His husband, Theo, sees two children who always seem to have what they need, gym clothes included, and genuinely has no idea how. When Marcus went to Baltimore for three days in March, Theo found out. Both children went to school on non-uniform day in uniform, and one of them went without a coat. Theo called Marcus on the second night and said, “How do you know?” He isn’t careless, and never was; the work had simply been out of his sight for eleven years.

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Four parts of the thinking Interviewing 35 couples, Allison Daminger split household thinking work into anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. Women in the study did more of it overall, especially the anticipating and the monitoring, deciding was shared more equally, and much of it went unseen by both partners.

    Daminger (2019) · Moderate: a rich interview study of mostly college-educated couples; not population figures

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check that it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, though nobody can see it. In a shared apartment above a laundromat, Yasmin noticed the broadband deal ended in three weeks. She compared six plans on a spreadsheet, picked one, set it up, and checked the next morning that it had gone through. Paying took two minutes, while the thinking took most of a Thursday evening, and as far as she knows nobody else in the apartment has any idea it happened. When the internet kept working, one of them said, “Oh good, the broadband’s fine.”

    Pick one job at home and split it into its steps (noticing, planning, doing, checking), then ask who usually does each one.

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen, so the other person is rarely ignoring it. From where they stand, things look as though they take care of themselves. Edna minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own hospital appointments to fit, and her daughter thinks it all slots in easily. It doesn’t. Edna has canceled her eye clinic twice. A gentle question often opens it up (“What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?”), followed by listening without explaining.

    Sometimes it only becomes visible when the person who carried it has gone. Over his first winter alone, a widower found out how many jobs his wife had quietly held, from the birthday cards and the water heater service to knowing who to call when someone was ill. He didn’t see any of it until it stopped getting done, and the water heater broke in January. If that’s where you are, the section for people on their own, further on in this chapter, was written with you in mind.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time, so the talk starts from facts you share. This book calls that shared record a ledger, a plain list of who did what, kept for seeing.

    Two colleagues who job-share each listed a week of tasks. It turned out every single difficult client call had landed on Tamsin, every one of them, and nobody had decided that. It had just happened, the way these things do, because she was better at them. They left last year alone and agreed to take turns with Friday calls. A quick look at who does what (spreadloveandacceptance.com/lemonade-stand) is a gentle place to start. Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself honestly whether it’s to plan next week or to prove a point. If it’s the second, keep it in the drawer for now.

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did and then read the two lists together, which keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, a long week at work, children off school with colds, so you both understand what you read.

    Arran, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a rented house in Leith. Each wrote only their own list, in their own handwriting, which in Dani’s case needed some translating. Reading them together at the kitchen table, Sol learned for the first time that Arran had called the landlord about the damp every single month since February. “Every month?” said Sol. “Every month,” said Arran. Dani said, “I thought it had just stopped.” It hadn’t; it had spread behind the dresser.

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what is happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    Two brothers share their mom’s care, and one of them had been about to say “You never help” for about a year. What he said in the end was “There’s some work here neither of us has looked at properly.” His brother, braced for a fight, found he didn’t need to have one. If words like “ledger” or “debt” feel heavy, use your own. “Our list” and “the stuff nobody sees” do the same job.

Friday, after work

“You didn’t have to do all that,” they say, and they mean it kindly.

Someone did, though. The list only ever lived in your head, so to them it looked as if it took care of itself, the way a house seems to clean itself if you’re never in it on a Saturday morning. You don’t say this. You say “It’s fine,” which it isn’t, quite.

zqaxprefacexxmentalxloadqzThe mental load: what the research says

Many people call the thinking side of home life the mental load, meaning the noticing and planning that come before a job gets done and the checking after. It includes knowing the baby needs the next size of clothes, and remembering the form and the party present. It is real work, even when it doesn’t look like any.

Studies of couples find some patterns, on average. In couples of a man and a woman, women tend to do more of the noticing and checking (Allison Daminger, 2019). After a first baby a gap often opens, and in one study of working couples, mothers took on most of the new childcare while keeping their paid hours (Jill Yavorsky and colleagues, 2015). Arlie Hochschild called the home work that starts after paid work ends “the second shift.”

There is a fair point on the other side too. Sometimes the parent who knows how things are done holds on to that, without meaning to, which researchers call gatekeeping. When a parent openly invites the other in and lets them do it their own way, the other parent tends to do more of the care (Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins, 1999; Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, 2008).

These are averages across many homes, and they say nothing certain about yours. In your home it may be the other way around, or already even, or shared between two men or two women, which is why the book asks you to look at your own week on paper instead of guessing.

Hollie and Gareth have a toddler and a baby, and Gareth works shifts. Their week’s minutes come out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked anyone to do. Their week is in Chapter II (page 58).

You could say “You do lots of the doing. It’s the remembering I’d love you to take some of, whole jobs, start to finish.” Or the other way around, “Show me once how you like it done, then let me do it my way.”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing it. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember (the appointments, the smoothing-over) and write them down for yourself. Count it without charging for it. The point is to see your real load so you can plan your energy. Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which just landed on you, and pick one you could hand back or do less often.

Nicola has the children every other week. Four years after the divorce, she sat down with a pen and found she was still buying the birthday presents for both sides of the family. His mom, his sister’s twins and his godson in Portland were all still on her list. She handed his side back in one friendly message, and braced herself. He replied with a thumbs-up and, to her surprise, “fair enough.” More for people living alone or after a breakup is on the On my own (spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own) page.

One household, followed for a month

Three grown-up siblings share the care of their dad, who lives on his own in the house they grew up in. On paper the visits are split evenly, two days each. When each of them wrote a week of their own side, the picture changed. Ffion, the eldest, also held the prescriptions, the hospital letters and the calls to the doctor, none of which counted as a visit. Their brother was surprised and a bit defensive, and the first conversation ended early, with someone leaving to “check the car.” A month later they tried again. He took over the prescriptions completely, with the reminders on his own phone. Their sister still finds the hospital letters too much to face, so Ffion keeps those for now, and they’ve agreed to look again in the spring. Two ways to read this

The last minute, two ways

Some people start a task as soon as it appears, and a form left for a month feels like a weight. Others find that interest and urgency switch the engine on, and a far-off deadline simply doesn’t. If you start early, the night-before person usually does care. Their brain starts late. If you’re the late starter, agreeing on an earlier “pretend deadline” together takes the worry off the other person without asking you to change how you work.

Where the fields meet. Finance calls this unbilled debt: every entry has two sides, and work nobody sees still goes in the ledger. Economics adds that attention is a budget, and business that work belonging to everyone belongs to no one.

Common mix-ups

“If it mattered, they’d have asked for help” misses how hidden this work is, often from the person doing it, until it’s written down. Nobody needs exactly 50/50, either. What you’re after is a split you can both see and both agree to. Please don’t start by going over the last ten years. Start with one ordinary week, and thank each other for the past without trying to settle it. And the one doing less usually isn’t the one who cares less. Usually they just can’t see it yet, and once you’ve both seen it, what happens next is a choice you make together.

To start, each of you can list the things you did this week that nobody saw, then swap lists and read the other one slowly. Say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed. Pick one item and thank them for it in a specific way. If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a coffee with the same questions works too. Fixing comes later. Who did what (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-01) turns this into a gentle week-long log, and How to say a specific thank-you (spreadloveandacceptance.com/turning-toward) has more ideas.

Limits and cautions

A shared list and keeping tabs on someone can look alike on paper. A shared list is something you both chose. Each of you writes your own side, and either of you can stop. Keeping tabs is one person asking for the other’s notes, checking them, or using them to track where the other goes or what they spend. Some people just like a lot of detail, and that’s fine when it’s about their own side. If a list starts to feel like the second kind, set it aside and talk about how it’s being used before you go back to it.

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.”

“I know. I’m just asking when.”

“Why does it matter when?”

“It doesn’t, I just…”

Four sentences later nobody is talking about the trash. Somebody has brought up last Christmas. Neither of you could point to the moment it turned.

The radio picture in this chapter is my own. When two people who care about each other keep missing, what’s usually lacking is timing and a shared way of putting things, and this chapter is an attempt at the shared way.

Quickly: the trash is the surface. Underneath, two different paces, one fast and one slow. Underneath that, two bodies in two different states at the end of a long day. The roots are signal and noise, and state and setting. Then slowly, one knob at a time.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something perfectly reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. Think of two radios slightly out of tune with each other. Together they make a crackle neither of them was making on its own.

    “Is anyone using the oven tonight?” Lisa asked. Simple question. She had a lasagna in mind. Her roommate went pink and said, “Why, did I leave a mess?” and there was a pause in which the baking tray from Sunday, still soaking, became the only thing in the room. “I meant the lasagna,” Lisa said eventually. “I know,” said the roommate, who hadn’t known, and went and washed the tray.

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on completely different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they’re close but not quite matched, which is why people who know each other well still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we’re close to, because we assume they already understand.

    Two sisters who are on the phone every single day argue about vacation plans far more than either of them ever does with the cousins they see once a year, at christenings and funerals. The cousins get things spelled out. The sisters get shorthand, half a sentence, “you know what I mean.” Quite often they don’t.

    With someone close to you, check one meaning today. “Just so I’ve got this right, you mean…?” It feels a bit formal, which is rather the point.

    Dig deeper: why close can be noisy

    Tidbit

    Compare your daily rhythms: whose engine runs fast in the morning, and whose at night? Different speeds aren’t wrong, they’re just different.

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    Pace is one, and tone is another, as when a plain update lands as a complaint. The third is urgency, since “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and some time before Easter to another. Texts lose most of their tone, so urgency is especially easy to misread there.

    We think our tone travels Across five experiments, people writing emails overestimated how well readers would pick up their tone, sarcasm or seriousness, largely because they couldn’t hear their own message without their own voice in it.

    Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005) · Strong: several experiments, widely cited

    Between meetings, Vikram texted his business partner two words. Need to talk. She read it as a crisis and lay awake until two in the morning running through every disaster available, including the bank, the landlord and a client she’d always slightly distrusted. At nine he called. It was about the printer. She has since asked him, politely and also not entirely politely, to add a time and the word “printer.”

    Ways to say it “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you’re tense you may talk faster and hear a pause as a snub. When you’re tired and low, your replies get slower and shorter. The Nervous System Ladder (spreadloveandacceptance.com/quick-checks), a thirty-second self-check, gives you a best guess at which state you’re in. People’s ordinary rhythms differ too, before stress even arrives, and Rhythms side by side (spreadloveandacceptance.com/tools/frequency-calibration) lets you compare whose engine runs fast in the morning and whose at night.

    Barry comes in off a night shift at the depot and his son asks him about Saturday. He gets “Fine” and “Yep” and “Later.” His son decides, on the spot, that his dad doesn’t care about Saturday or, by extension, him. Barry is simply running on empty after ten hours of forklifts and a forty-minute drive home with the window down to stay awake. He’d have told you so if he’d had any words left. He went to bed.

    Dig deeper: how stress shifts your tune

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work perfectly will still crackle if they’re slightly off, and arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning will, which just means getting back in step.

    On the doorstep, coat still on, Sylvia tells her grown-up son he looks tired. She means it as care. He hears “you’re not coping,” which is what he has been privately worrying about all week, and says “Thanks, Mom” in a voice that could strip paint. Both of them are right about what they meant and what they heard. That’s exactly why arguing about it goes nowhere. They manage to get through the visit, just, and both feel bad about it on the way home.

    You could say “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say out loud that you think you’re out of sync, and check how tense you are. Either of you can call a pause, and whoever pauses names a time to come back. Slow down, take shorter turns, and only then go back to the actual topic.

    Two students sharing a room in the dorms agreed a pause word one calm Sunday. They picked “static,” mainly because it made them laugh. Three weeks later, at one in the morning, in the middle of a fight about someone’s music, one of them said it, through gritted teeth. They stopped. Both lay there fuming in the dark. They came back to it at nine the next evening, as agreed, and worked it out in ten minutes with headphones, a second set of headphones and a slightly sheepish packet of cookies.

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    The first three minutes In 124 newlywed couples, how the first three minutes of a disagreement went predicted which couples had divorced six years later. It’s one lab’s sample and not a crystal ball, but it’s a good argument for opening gently.

    Carrère and Gottman (1999) · Moderate: one research group’s sample; striking, not widely replicated as a predictor

    When you go back to the topic, say what happened, how you feel and what you’d like, in that order and in one go. “The trash goes out early on Wednesday. I get anxious when they’re late. Could you put them out tonight?” Or, from one co-parent to the other, on a Sunday evening, “The swimsuit wasn’t in the bag and I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?” Write one about something small before you need it, so the shape is in your hand when you do.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-13-daily-check-in), makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big fight.     Orla is in Chicago and Marc is in Toronto. Every morning each of them sends one 90-second voice note, usually something like “Trash went out, my boss is still weird, I miss you.” On the weeks they forget, they notice it by Thursday, usually as an odd phone call about nothing in particular that leaves them both slightly put out.

Your part, and only your part. You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back. On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace and your own sense of urgency, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. Learn your early signs of drifting. A clipped reply is a common one. So is a sudden urge to win. Learn how you take words in best and say it, once (“I hear things better in writing”). When you drift, step out, settle, come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission for that. One example, followed through

Cerys, Marisol and Dougie share a house and a car. Cerys and Marisol talk fast, while Dougie talks slowly and thinks before he does it. Two ways to read this

Out of tune, both ways

Some people think by talking and need the answer right away. Others think first and talk second, and a quick reply asked for too soon comes out wrong. If you talk to think, the slower person’s pause isn’t agreement, and it isn’t a protest. It’s thinking. If you think first, the fast talker isn’t trying to win. Out loud is where their thinking happens.

Where the fields meet. Music has a name for a good conversation: call and response, one phrase answering another at a tempo both can keep. Neurobiology explains why the tempo slips, since your state sets the volume, and a stressed body changes station without asking you.

Retuning isn’t giving in, because you still come back to the topic, at a time when you can hear each other. Being out of sync doesn’t mean one of you is wrong, either. Two fair points in two different tones will still clash. And a pause never means the topic is dropped, since a pause comes with a time to come back, or it isn’t a pause.

To try it, agree a shared phrase while things are calm (“I think we’re out of tune” does fine) so that it works as a signal when you need it. For a week, each of you note one moment you felt out of step, and on the weekend compare whether it was mostly pace, tone or urgency. Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about, like the trash schedule and the fish food, the reply to the school, the dentist reminder, and the bit at the bottom of the shopping list that just says “bulbs?”

When the other one says it feels pretty even, they mean it. From inside their own week, it does.

The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives. Then the chapter takes the three parts of the score in turn.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    Ask whether the split is working and the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently, which is usually last night’s pans. Then they looked at the whole month, written down, and it was three ordinary weeks and one exam week. The fight didn’t happen, which was almost a disappointment after all that build-up. They bought paper plates for exam week and left it at that.

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    Log one week each, and put in the small jobs that take under ten minutes. Those are the ones that go missing. They are often the ones that start the fights, too.

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Each job gets two names. The owner does the job from start to finish. The backstop notices if it slipped and checks in gently, which is very different from supervising. A decision you make together, such as which school, can be marked “Both (decide together)” and counts as owned. A job that needs doing gets one name.

    Two sisters care for their dad. Nobody owned his prescription refills. For two weeks each of them thought the other had ordered it, and each, it turned out, had thought about calling to check and hadn’t, so as not to seem pushy. He ran out on a Sunday. Redoing a job, two ways

Some people care most that a job got done. Others care how it was done, because things have a system and a cup in the “wrong” place means hunting for it later. If you care that it’s done, a quiet reload of the dishwasher can feel like your work was graded. If you care about the system, saying the reason (“The bowls go at the back so the door shuts”) turns a silent redo into a plan you can share.

Where the fields meet. Politics would say a home is a small government, and a rule lasts when the people who live by it helped make it. That is why the score is filled in together, from both people’s own notes. The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives.

Common mix-ups

A low number doesn’t mean the relationship is failing. It means the current setup needs changing, and setups change all the time. Please don’t guess the numbers. A guessed number is a feeling with a decimal point, so use your written logs. One of you can’t fill it out for both, either. Each person writes their own log and scores their own stress tank. And fair doesn’t have to mean exactly 50/50. Fair is what you both agree to with your eyes open, and an uneven split you chose together can be the fair one. zqaxchapterx2xxsmallxchildrenqzSmall children and a new baby

Small children bring a flood of new jobs, and most of them are thinking jobs, from the daycare bag and the shots to the party invitations, the next size of clothes and the nights. That’s why the mental load (see the work nobody sees, page 25) often grows after a baby arrives, usually without anyone deciding it. Log the children’s jobs too, with the thinking time, and count the nights. Hand over whole jobs, like clothes and sizes or the doctor and the shots, so the new owner does the noticing too, in their own way. If a baby is on the way, write your first job list together before the birth, plan the nights, and agree to look again at six weeks, at three months and before either of you goes back to work. In the first weeks, share by energy more than by hours, so whoever was up in the night gets the rest.

Hollie and Gareth have Alfie, who is three, and Poppy, who is eight months, and Gareth is a paramedic on shifts. Their logged minutes came out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked for. They left the minutes alone and moved one whole job, the children’s clothes, to Gareth. In the first two weeks Alfie went to daycare in a vest in October. Hollie said nothing, heroically.

Thursday, 5:15pm

“Did you remember to call the dentist?”

A perfectly pleasant question. What comes out of you in reply is about four sizes too big for it, with a bit about the car thrown in for good measure, and you can hear it happening while it happens, like watching yourself trip on a stair.

Eight in the morning after a night shift, quickly. On the surface, a question about the meter. Underneath, twelve hours on your feet. Underneath that, two people seeing one moment from different angles. The roots are state and setting, and signal and noise. The chapter slows down on the tank, then the angles.

  1. A full tank overflows.

    If you’re already tired or stretched from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction, which is simply human. Anything decided in that moment is worth coming back to once you’ve both calmed down.

    Stress from outside leaks in Daily diaries from newlywed couples showed that spouses under more stress outside the relationship reacted more strongly to everyday irritations inside it. Marnie’s morning is a fair example.

    Neff and Karney (2009) · Moderate: daily-diary studies of newlyweds; correlational

    It’s eight in the morning and Marnie has just done a twelve-hour night shift and is standing in the kitchen in her coat. Her roommate, eating cornflakes, asks if she saw his message about the electricity meter. She bites his head off, though really it was twelve hours on her feet that did the biting. The meter just happened to be standing at the end of them. She apologized at four in the afternoon, when she woke up, via a note under his door. The note said, “Sorry. That was bigger than the question. I’m running on empty.”

  2. Some stress is left over from earlier.

    Ask yourself how much of this feeling is about the thing in front of you and how much you carried in with you. Being upset about the dishes and being exhausted, with the dishes as the thing you happened to notice, are different problems. Some of what we bring in is much older, like an understanding from growing up that whoever notices the mess has to clean it. If a feeling seems a lot older than the moment, Where your lens came from (spreadloveandacceptance.com/growing-up) helps you find where it started.

    Corey is fourteen and has had a horrible day at school, the sort he won’t talk about. He comes home and finds his brother has taken the phone charger, again. The explosion that follows can be heard two doors down. The charger was the last drop in a tank that had been full since lunchtime.

    Words you could use “I think I brought this in with me. Give me ten minutes.”

  3. Check your stress tank in a minute.

    How much are you carrying? measures how full your stress tank is, and a higher score means more stress in the tank. Score five things from 0 to 4, which are sleep, work elsewhere, any unsettled argument, how your body feels and time pressure today, then add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 is medium and 0.60 or more is high. If it’s been high for a long time it can start to feel normal, so a quick reading each week is worth it.

    Vince has been looking after his wife since her hip operation. He scored 0.65 three days in a row. That told him something he hadn’t let himself think, which was that he needed help more than he needed to try harder. He sat with it for an evening, in the chair by the window with the radio on low. Then he called his son, who said, “Dad, I’ve been waiting for you to ask since March.”

    Dig deeper: the stress-tank score

  4. Three everyday states.

    Knowing whether you’re calm and connected, revved up or running on empty tells you whether now is a good time to talk. The Nervous System Ladder (spreadloveandacceptance.com/quick-checks), a thirty-second self-check, helps you make a best guess. The labels come from polyvagal theory, whose main claims are still debated by scientists, and here they’re used only as everyday words. In one long-distance couple, he takes the video call at midnight his time and is usually running on empty by then, so they’ve moved the hard talks to Saturday mornings. It helps to know one body clue for each state. A tight jaw is a common one for revved up.

    Dig deeper: the three states

  5. There are seven angles on the same moment.

    The seven angles are tiredness, time pressure, history, what home was like growing up, your sense of fairness, how you communicate and stress from outside. Many arguments are two people looking from different angles, each sure the other sees what they see. The “See their side” step of Rewind the talk (spreadloveandacceptance.com/carrier-wave-decoder) is built on these seven angles and helps you picture how the same moment might look from the other side, with questions to ask in place of guesses.

    Naive realism Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described the quiet assumption that we see things as they really are, so anyone who disagrees must be uninformed or unreasonable. Saying your angle out loud is a small way of admitting you have one.

    Ross and Ward (1996) · Strong: a foundational account whose predictions are well replicated

    Christmas, again, and Sasha sees it from family habit, because they always go to her mom’s. There’s a stocking with each child’s name on it, stitched in 2016, and her mom would be heartbroken. Kemi sees it from fairness: they went to her mom’s last year, and the year before, and, now that he counts, the year before that.

    Dig deeper: the seven angles

  6. Both views can be true.

    You can each be right about what you see and still be looking at different parts of the same thing, so the useful question is which angle each of you is seeing it from. Two brothers argued for a year about their late mom’s house. One saw thirty years of memories. Height marks on the kitchen door frame, the apple tree, the step where she sat to take her shoes off. The other saw a roof that needed $12,000 of work. Both of them were looking at the real house. In the end they sold it, and the one who saw the memories took the door frame with the height marks on it, which is now leaning against a wall in his garage.

    Words to try “You’re right about what you see. Can I tell you what I see?”

    Dig deeper: two true views

  7. Say it instead of making each other guess.

    Before a hard conversation, each of you says how full your tank is and which angle you’re seeing from, ideally right at the start.

    Two colleagues had a meeting about a missed deadline that both were dreading. One of them, feeling foolish, began with, “Before we start, I’m about half full today and I’m mostly seeing this from the time angle.” After a pause the other said, “Right. I’m pretty full. Fairness angle, if I’m honest.” It felt very odd, like reading out your own weather forecast. The meeting was shorter and calmer than the last one, and nobody cried in the restroom afterward, which last time somebody had.

  8. A full tank means “later,” not “never.”

    A high score is a reason to pause and never a way out of the talk. Say when you’ll come back, and let the Calm-Down Kit (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-11) help in the meantime.     Flooding John Gottman’s long-term studies of couples gave a name to the point where someone is so physically overwhelmed in a conflict that listening and thinking become very hard. A full tank means later partly because a flooded person can’t really do now.

    Gottman (1994) · Moderate: large observational studies; some specific predictions are debated

    It’s about phone time, and a dad and his fourteen-year-old are both shouting. “I’m too wound up,” he says, “I’ll talk to you at seven.” She slams her door, and at seven he knocks. It matters more than anything he says once he’s inside. That isn’t much, or very good, and it is a lot better than not knocking.

Your part, and only your part. Your stress tank is the only one you can empty. You can’t calm someone else’s tank for them, and pouring yours into theirs leaves two empty tanks. Words this chapter uses

Your stress tank is how much stress you’re already carrying before this moment even starts. Leftover stress is the part of a feeling you brought in from somewhere else, like poor sleep or a hard day, while this-moment stress is caused by what is in front of you right now. On your own: reading your own tank

On your own, the most useful habit is a one-minute reading of your tank before you react. Check it for a few mornings in a row and look for a pattern. Ask yourself before a hard moment how much of it is left over from earlier. Decide on a calm day what settles you, so it’s ready when you need it. Living alone after a breakup, Beth noticed she snapped at her sister on the phone every single Monday. Her morning readings showed why. Sunday night was her worst sleep of the week, every week, without fail. Now she asks for Tuesday instead, and her sister has stopped asking what’s wrong (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own).

Two ways to read this

A full tank, two ways

Some people can say “I’m running high today” easily. Others only realize how full they were once they’ve snapped. If you can name it, the other person may genuinely not know their own number yet. Give them time. If you can’t, the one who names it isn’t making excuses in advance. They’re giving you a weather report so you can plan around it.

Where the fields meet. Neurobiology says your state sets the volume. Psychology adds that mood filters what we hear, and aromatherapy, read modestly, that the setting matters, a quieter room or a later hour. The evidence for particular oils is weak. The room and the timing are what help.

Common mix-ups

A big reaction sometimes means it was a big deal. Quite often it means the tank was already full. A high score is never an excuse, either. It’s a note to come back later, never a pass to skip the talk. You can’t tell how full someone else’s tank is, however well you know them, which is why each person scores only themselves. And a pause isn’t the silent treatment. The silent treatment is ignoring someone for days, again and again, to punish them, with no time to come back. A pause has a time to come back, which is why you always say when: “I need twenty minutes. Can we pick this up at eight?” If a pause of yours is being read as the other kind, describe it out loud rather than defending yourself.

Sometimes a reaction is far bigger than the moment, and part of it may belong to something older, from childhood or a past relationship or a frightening time. You can’t know someone’s history from outside, and guessing out loud (“This is about your ex”) rarely helps. Having lived through hard things is never the person’s fault, and nothing here is for diagnosing anyone. What you can change is your own half. Slow your pace, lower your voice, sit down, keep the doorway clear and use fewer words. If it’s still climbing, say when you’ll come back, step away, and then come back when you said you would. The same list works when the big reaction is yours.

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing in the first place. You are both counting honestly, in two different currencies, then arguing about the exchange rate. This usually happens on a Sunday evening and usually, for reasons nobody can explain, about the oven.

A Sunday-evening argument about the oven, seen quickly. The surface is the oven. Underneath, two currencies, hours and noticing. Underneath that, two kinds of fair nobody has named. The roots are balance and signal and noise. Slowly now, one kind of fair at a time.

  1. zqaxchapterx4xxextwoxkindsqz

    There are two kinds of fair.

    One kind asks whether everything got done. The other asks whether each person did what they said they would. Both are reasonable, and friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realizing.

    More than one kind of fair Morton Deutsch argued that equity (you get out what you put in) is only one fairness rule, alongside equality (the same for everyone) and need, and that people lean on different ones in different settings. Research broadly agrees that the setting decides.

    Deutsch (1975) · Moderate: an influential theory, broadly supported

    Nisha and Kezia share a room in the dorms. Nisha thinks a week was fair if the room is tidy by Friday, whoever tidied it. Kezia thinks it was fair if each of them kept her own half clean. For most of the first semester each thought the other was being unreasonable and said so to other people. Neither of them was. It came out at two in the morning over a shared packet of noodles, and they both laughed until the girl next door banged on the wall.

    You could say “When you say fair, do you mean it all gets done, or that we each do our bit?”

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind of fair judges by outcomes, by whether the right things got done by whoever had the time or energy. It’s flexible and easy-going, which is lovely when it works. Done carefully it counts the cost to whoever covered. Done carelessly it hides who is always stepping in.

    Marjorie is raising her grandchildren. Her grown-up son helps with whatever’s needed when he’s home, cheerfully and well. The house runs, and she is always, always the one who notices what’s needed. She didn’t see it herself until she marked, for one week, every job she picked up that wasn’t planned. It came to forty-one, and she showed her son. He said, “Forty-one?” and then, after a bit, “Right.”

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, and it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way. Two business partners run a café. One owns the ordering, the other owns the staff schedule. When the schedule is late (it’s the Thursday before a long weekend, it is always late then) they ask the schedule owner about it, not “the team.” And nobody takes it personally, or not for long.

    Words you could use “It’s my job, and I can’t do it this week. Could you take it, and I’ll take out the trash?”

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job from start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop, who notices if it slipped and checks in gently without supervising, and it can be the same person. If the job is yours, the question is whether you did it, and somebody quietly covering for you doesn’t answer that. It also protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues (2008) · Moderate: an observational study of one US sample; correlational

And I do want to hear you properly.” Then he decided it needed a real talk, the next evening, after her swimming, in the car, which is where they talk best, both looking at the road.

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    Whether the job was done is a question about a fact. Whether the comment hurt is a question about a feeling. Both matter, and each goes better in its own conversation.

    Separated parents share their children across two homes. The bag came back on Sunday without the school shoes, and with it a text about the shoes that felt, to the one who received it, very cold. They settled the shoes first, on the phone, in about two minutes. They talked about the text the following Sunday. It took longer and was more awkward, and it was better for waiting a week (see Co-parenting, spreadloveandacceptance.com/co-parenting). When a talk tangles, “Fact or feeling?” is a good question to ask out loud. Finish one before you start the other.

On your own: your own kind of fair

Fair starts with knowing what it means to you, and which jobs are truly yours. Write down what fair means to you in one sentence, and list the jobs you own alongside the ones you picked up without ever agreeing to. Errol lives alone and has his children every other weekend. His sentence, after a lot of crossing out, is that fair means not doing their jobs for them just because he misses them (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own). Two ways to read this

“On time,” two ways

Some people learned at home that seven means seven sharp, and arriving at five after feels like a small slight. Others learned that seven means from seven, and arriving on the dot is a little too eager. If you’re a seven-sharp person, the other person isn’t careless. They’re being polite by their own rule. If you’re a from-seven person, saying “Is that seven sharp?” costs nothing and saves the wait.

Where the fields meet. Philosophy asks you to agree which kind of fair you’re using before you argue about it. The art of debating adds: say their strongest point first. And music explains why “Thanks for doing the dishes” can still go wrong, because tone carries more than the words.

Common mix-ups

If it got done, there may still be a problem, because someone may be paying for it in time and energy. Holding the owner to the job isn’t being strict. It’s keeping the agreement you both made, which is kinder than quiet resentment. Letting a comment land doesn’t mean ignoring it. It’s about timing, and you still get to answer. And please don’t save up months of covering to bring out all at once. A long list lands like a bill, so mention covering as it happens.

Money is where the two kinds of fair meet most often. Some couples put everything into one pot. Some keep their money separate and pay agreed bills, often half each, which is fair by promises, the same rule for both. Others each pay into a shared pot in line with what they earn and keep the rest, which is fair by results, since each is left with a similar share. Many couples mix them, with a joint account for bills and food and some money each that nobody has to explain. The right way is the one you both chose with your eyes open. To talk about it, start with your money stories, how money worked at home when you were growing up, then move on to the numbers and to a “talk first” amount for big purchases that applies to both of you.

Having a say Procedural justice research, which began with Thibaut and Walker, finds that whether people feel a decision was reached fairly, and above all whether they had a voice in it, shapes how they feel about the result. A good reason to work a split out together rather than hand one over.

Thibaut and Walker (1975) · Strong: one of the more robust findings in social psychology, though early lab methods were criticized

A shared agreement, or controlling rules?

A promise is something both people chose and either can ask to change. It can still feel strict (“no new subscriptions without a chat first”), and that’s fine, because it binds you both. “Controlling” is a serious word, and it gets used for ordinary disagreements far too often.

For something to be controlling, these are usually all present:

  • one person sets rules for the other’s ordinary life, such as money, friends, time, clothes or phone,
  • the rules bind one side and not the other,
  • they’re enforced by checking or by a penalty nobody agreed to, such as money held back, days of coldness or threats to leave,
  • the other person’s no isn’t accepted, and it has become a pattern rather than one bad week.

Not enough on its own:

  • a shared agreement you both chose, even a strict one,
  • a partner who likes budgets, plans or detail more than you do,
  • asking where you are, or worrying when you’re late,
  • a limit someone sets on their own behavior (“I won’t lend money again”),
  • a need for routine or knowing what’s coming.

If a rule feels one-sided, name the rule rather than the person. “The talk-first amount seems to apply to my spending and not yours. Can we set one that covers us both?”

The third Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth you had last Tuesday, word for word. You know your lines and so do they.

Or you could notice that this is the third Tuesday running, and wonder what it is about Tuesdays.

The trash on a third Tuesday, quickly. On the surface, the same short argument. Underneath, a job that keeps slipping. Underneath that, a job nobody owns. The roots are feedback loops and ownership and structure. Then the chapter slows down and walks through the look-back.

  1. Weekly check-ins miss patterns.

    Latent conditions James Reason, writing about accidents in organizations, showed how they grow out of latent conditions, gaps that sit quietly in a system until they meet an ordinary slip on an ordinary day. A job with no owner is a latent condition.

    Reason (1997) · Strong: foundational in safety science

    Four roommates had an overflowing bathroom trash can every week, and every week it seemed like bad luck, until they looked at the month together and saw it had happened every single week since September. One missed job looks like bad luck. The same job missed week after week is a pattern, and patterns are easiest to see when you look at a whole month at once.

    In my old job the step that got skipped more than any other was going back afterward to check that the fix had actually held, and skipping it is exactly how the same problem turns up again three years later. This chapter is that step, made small. A careful, friendly glance over the month, closer to a self-check than an inspection. Choose a monthly date now, the last Sunday, say, and put it in everyone’s calendar.

    Dig deeper: why once a month

  2. You need four weeks of notes and one list.

    Bring the month’s four weekly notes from Who did what (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-01), where a few lines a week is enough, and your job list from One owner per job (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-03-one-owner-per-job). Twenty minutes is usually plenty. If either of your stress tanks is high that day, at 0.60 or more on How much are you carrying? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-02-how-much-are-you-carrying), move it a day or two.

    Kat and Nathan work different shifts and keep their weekly notes in one shared note on their phones, mostly typed at red lights. They do the look-back on the one evening a month they’re both off, with takeout from the Nepalese place. It has become, slightly to their embarrassment, the evening of the month they look forward to most.

    Dig deeper: getting ready

  3. Mark an ✗ for each week a job slipped.

    A job slipped if it was noticed and not done, only done after being raised more than once, or quietly covered by someone who isn’t its owner. Look closely at every job with two or more ✗, because three or four in a month is a real pattern.

    Two foster caregivers found the reading log had been missed three weeks out of four. Both of them had an explanation ready. They marked the ✗ in pencil first, together, before either of them said anything at all, which is much harder than it sounds and much the best order. Then the explanations, which turned out to be the same one.

    Dig deeper: gathering the month’s notes

  4. A problem that keeps coming back usually has no real owner.

    That kind of problem gets patched every week and never fixed. The owner is the person who does the job from start to finish, so the fix is to give the job one owner whose name sticks, or to talk honestly about whether anyone has the time and energy for it.

    Diffusion of responsibility In a famous experiment, people who thought others could also hear someone having a seizure were slower to help, and less likely to, because each felt less responsible. A grocery order is a long way from an emergency, but “whoever’s free” runs on the same arithmetic.

    Darley and Latané (1968) · Strong: classic and widely replicated, though weaker in clearly dangerous emergencies

    In a house with three partners, “whoever’s free” did the online grocery order. It was late three times this month, once by a whole day. With nobody owning it, everybody assumed someone else had it in hand. One Friday there was no milk at all in the house, and four jars of mustard.

    Dig deeper: spotting the gaps that keep reopening

  5. Sort each repeat into one of three kinds.

    If a job has no clear owner, update the job list. If its owner can’t keep up, have a kind, honest talk. If there was a short-term cause, like a bout of flu, note it and move on.

    The homework folder kept coming back unsigned. Two separated parents looked at it at the look-back and went through the three kinds, out loud, quite carefully, because last time they’d argued. Was nobody sure which home signs it, was one of them swamped, or was it just the week one of them had the flu? It was the first one. It had been the first one since September (see Co-parenting, spreadloveandacceptance.com/co-parenting).

    Dig deeper: sorting each gap

  6. Count the gaps each month.

    Write down how many gaps of each kind you found. If the number of jobs with no clear owner falls over several months, the setup is working, and a single month can be a normal wobble. If it keeps going up, something bigger has changed and it’s worth a talk. Two business partners write their monthly count on a whiteboard in the back office, next to the fire-drill notice. In March four jobs had no clear owner. By June, one did, and they argue quite cheerfully about whose fault that one is.

    Dig deeper: keeping count

    Professor Puddles

    The look-back has its own workpaper: a month of notes, one list, and an ✗ for each week a job slipped. It makes a pattern easy to see.

    Workpaper WP-04: What keeps coming back? spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-04

  7. Look back even when nothing is slipping.

    A month with no gaps is good news. It’s also the month most likely to go unchecked. When something has worked for a while, people stop looking, and nothing breaks on the day that happens. Keep the look-back going in quiet months and add one question, “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?,” which is how a look-back guards against complacency (spreadloveandacceptance.com/complacency).

    The children have left home and nothing whatsoever is slipping. The look-back takes eleven minutes. At the end Dave says, quietly, looking at his plate, that they don’t really talk at dinner anymore. Jan had noticed it too, months ago, without knowing how to say it. No list would have shown it.

    Dig deeper: the quiet months

  8. It’s not a review of either person.

    A just culture Sidney Dekker argues for a just culture, one that separates honest mistakes from reckless ones, and points out that who draws that line matters as much as where it’s drawn. At home, I’d draw it together, on a calm day.

    Dekker (2007) · Moderate: an influential book; the evidence is mainly case studies

    You don’t add up old hours or punish old mistakes. You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled. The only question is where the setup isn’t working yet and what you’ll try next month. After a month in the hospital, Yusuf came home to a long list of what had slipped while he was away. His partner had been dreading showing it to him. He read it, and then he said thank you for the bits that hadn’t slipped, which was most of them, and they set up the month ahead. That was all.

    Dig deeper: what the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

    Tidbit

    Start the look-back with something that went well. It changes the temperature of the whole twenty minutes.

    Tool: Kitchen-table check-in spreadloveandacceptance.com/check-ins

  9. Keep it short and kind.

    Start with something that went well and talk about the job instead of the person. If a gap needs a real talk, set it for another day using Check-ins (spreadloveandacceptance.com/check-ins). If a talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and whoever paused names a time to come back. A mom and two teenagers hold their look-back over pizza on the last Friday. Each says one good thing first (the younger one’s is always “the pizza”). One gap gets a new owner. It’s over in fifteen minutes and nobody has stormed off, which in that house is a result.

    Dig deeper: how to talk about a pattern

Your part, and only your part. In the look-back you can only fix the gaps you own, and offer help with the rest. You can’t make someone keep a rhythm. Keep the rhythms you can keep, notice which ones are kept in return, and let your effort follow: more where it’s shared, less where it isn’t, so the rhythm doesn’t run on one person’s energy. The last Sunday of the month

Two mugs of tea, four weeks of notes and about twenty minutes. You go down the list together, and most weeks were fine. Two jobs keep coming up, the recycling and the call to the landlord about the window that won’t shut. You give each of them a name, and the landlord one goes to whoever’s least scared of the landlord. You finish your coffee while it’s still warm, which hasn’t happened on a Sunday in months.

A look-back, three months in

Sam and Alex’s trash cans slipped three weeks in a row because Alex works late on Tuesdays. After the trial swap they make it official, so Sam owns the trash and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, which keeps the hours even. The second month is clean. In the third, the grocery order slips twice, both times in a week when Alex’s mom was in the hospital, so they mark it as a one-off and leave the list alone. What actually changes in month three is that Alex mentions, almost in passing, while stacking the plates, that he has stopped enjoying their Saturday mornings. No list had shown that. They agree to talk about it properly on Thursday, and Sam spends most of the week worrying about it.

You could say “I don’t think anyone dropped this. I think nobody ever picked it up.”

Two ways to read this

In writing, two ways

Some people remember a spoken plan easily and find a written list a bit formal. Others lose spoken words as they’re said, because holding them in mind while doing the job splits their attention. If you remember easily, a request for writing isn’t mistrust. It’s someone trying to get it right. If you need it written, offering to write it yourself (“I’ll send us both a note”) keeps it light.

Where the fields meet. Behavioral science says habits beat good intentions, so a look-back works best as a routine too small to skip. Holistic therapies would start with the body, a cup of tea before the list, and some of these practices are well studied while others are debated. A shared laugh, which the research says only modest things about, is still a good way to start.

Common mix-ups

This isn’t where you settle who owes what. Past hours are never billed to anyone, though you can still say thank you for them. You only fix the setup going forward. A slipped job doesn’t mean its owner failed, either, because the gap might be in the list, or in the owner’s capacity, or in one strange week. And you can’t skip the weekly notes and just do this. The look-back only works if the weekly notes feed it.

A look-back is something you both choose. It looks at the month and the setup, using notes you each kept about your own side. Monitoring is different. It means one person asking for the other’s every hour, checking their phone without agreement, or using the ✗ marks as evidence against them. A partner who simply likes detail, or asks “How did the week go?” isn’t monitoring. If the look-back starts to feel like being checked up on, pause it and talk about how it’s being used before you go on.

zqaxchapterx5xxkinshipqzRaising a grandchild (kinship care)

Many grandparents and other relatives raise a child whose parents can’t, and much of it involves things that keep coming back, such as canceled calls and the hard evenings after contact. A monthly look-back helps. Some repeats you can own. Some, like a parent who misses calls, you can’t, and for those you can own the plan for the evening after. The child may love both the parent who can’t look after them and you, so tell them they don’t have to choose, and don’t run the parent down in front of them. Explain an absent parent in a few true words that fit their age, always with the message that it isn’t their fault. When the parent is your own grown-up child and things are hard between you, keep messages about the child short and civil for the child’s sake, and leave the door open. Look after yourself too. Ask for respite, and find other kinship caregivers.

Maureen, who is sixty-three, is raising her grandson Tyler, who is nine. Her look-back shows that his mom’s Sunday call fell through three times this month. She can’t fix that. So she plans those evenings instead, with hot chocolate and his soccer book, and asks her brother to take Tyler swimming on Sunday afternoons, so there’s something to look forward to that doesn’t depend on a phone ringing.

Words you could use, with the child “Mom loves you. She isn’t well enough to look after you right now, and that’s not your fault.”

How the numbers work

Every number in this book is arithmetic you can do with a pencil. None of them is a test, a diagnosis or a verdict on anyone: they describe a week, a load or a way of sharing work. The weights and cut-offs are this book’s own rules of thumb, not research findings, and you are free to disagree with them. The worked examples are the book’s own, so you can check them against the chapters.

Two habits run through all of them. Scores are rounded to two decimal places before their band is read, so the number you see and the words you see always agree. And nobody’s score is ever worked out for them: if someone’s own answer is missing, the total waits.

The stress tank: How much are you carrying?

You score five things from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very true), about the last day or two: sleep debt, workload elsewhere, unresolved conflict, your physical state, and time pressure today.

tank = (sleep + workload + conflict + body + time) ÷ 20

The answer runs from 0 to 1, and higher means fuller. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 medium, and 0.60 or more high. (The worksheet splits each band in two for its words: very light under 0.15, light, medium on the lighter side under 0.45, medium on the heavier side, high under 0.80, and very high from 0.80.) Your battery is the other side of the same thing, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery. The battery is a picture in words, not a second score.

Worked example Rosa, from Part Two, Chapter III, page 63, scores sleep 3, workload 4, conflict 1, body 2 and time 3.

3 + 4 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 13; 13 ÷ 20 = 0.65, which is in the high band.

Two or more of you: each person scores only themselves, and the shared figure is the plain average of everyone’s own scores. If any one person is at 0.60 or more, treat that as the headline, whatever the average says.

Coming back after a pause (the Calm-Down Kit): if you like numbers, take the score again after a calming round. Under 0.50, go back at the time you named. From 0.50 to 0.59, take another round. At 0.60 or more after two rounds, put it off to a named time. The kit’s line sits at 0.50 on purpose: you don’t need an empty tank to come back, just one less than half full.

Workload balance: how evenly the hours are shared

Two people. Work out each person’s share of the logged hours as a percentage.

balance = 1 − (the difference between the two percentages ÷ 100)

50/50 scores 1.00; one person doing everything scores 0.00. It doesn’t matter which of you does more. Balance ignores the total, which is one reason the stress tank sits beside it.

Worked example Rosemary logs 14 hours and Ken 10 (Part Two, Chapter II, page 44). Out of 24 hours, that is 58.3% and 41.7%.

1 − (58.3 − 41.7) ÷ 100 = 1 − 0.167 = 0.83

Three or more people, or an agreed split. The same idea, said more generally: how much of the week would have to change hands to reach the target, compared with the most that ever could.

each share = that person’s hours ÷ everyone’s hours target = an even share (1 ÷ number of people), or the split you agreed moved = ½ × the sum of every |share − target| most = 1 − the smallest target share balance = 1 − moved ÷ most

Worked example Three partners log 15, 9 and 6 hours (Chapter II, page 44): shares 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20, against a third each.

moved = ½ × (0.167 + 0.033 + 0.133) = 0.167, most = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667, and balance = 1 − 0.167 ÷ 0.667 = 0.75. In hours, 0.167 × 30 = 5 hours a week would change hands for an even split.

With an agreed split, use it as the target: co-parents who agreed 60/40 and log 12 and 8 hours score 1.00.

Ownership: does every job have a name?

ownership = jobs with a named owner ÷ all the jobs on the list

The calculator counts a job as owned when it has one named owner. A backstop, the person who notices if it slipped, is a good habit and has its own column, but the score only needs the owner. The starter jobs on the worksheet don’t count until you give them an owner or change them. Never raise the score by deleting the jobs nobody owns: they are exactly what the score is there to show.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken list 12 regular jobs, and 9 have an owner: 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75.

Who carries more of the list. A score of 1.00 can still mean one person owns everything, so One owner per job also shows each person’s share of the list, with each job weighted by how often it comes round and how big it is:

weight of a job = how often × size how often: daily 5, weekly 1, ongoing 1, monthly 0.25, as needed 0.1 size: small 0.5, medium 1 (the default), big 2 (or, where you gave the hours each time: hours ÷ 0.5) share = the weights of that person’s jobs ÷ the weights of all the jobs

A job you decide together counts the same for each of you; on a list for two homes it shows as “Together” instead.

Worked example One person owns the cooking (daily, medium: 5) and the bills (monthly, medium: 0.25). The other owns the laundry (weekly, big: 2) and the car (as needed, medium: 0.1). Each owns two jobs, but 5.25 ÷ 7.35 = 71% of the list’s weight sits with the first person, and 29% with the second.

Who’s carrying more right now. Who did what flags the busiest person when their share of the logged minutes (or of the work done without being asked) is at least half, and at least 20 points over an even share: 70% or more for two people, 53% or more for three. In the example in the Preface, page 16, one partner did 105 of the 115 minutes that were noticed and handled without anyone asking: 91%, so the sheet says so.

The setup score: can the way you share the work last?

setup score = balance × 0.40 + ownership × 0.35 + (1 − the average tank score) × 0.25

The weights add up to 1, so the score runs from 0 to 1, and here higher means the setup is working better, the opposite of the tank score. The tank is the only part that counts against the score, which is why it enters as 1 − tank. 0.70 or more reads as working well (the “Who does what?” page says “holding”). From 0.40 up to 0.70 it reads as needs a look (“drifting”), and below 0.40 as needs a rethink, together. It is a snapshot of the setup, never a probability and never a verdict on anyone.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken (Chapter II, page 44): balance 0.83, ownership 0.75, and their own tank scores are 0.45 and 0.30, an average of 0.375.

0.83 × 0.40 + 0.75 × 0.35 + (1 − 0.375) × 0.25 = 0.333 + 0.263 + 0.156 = 0.75: working well.

The shortfall is what each part could have earned minus what it did earn: balance 0.40 − 0.333 = 0.067, ownership 0.35 − 0.263 = 0.088, and tank 0.25 − 0.156 = 0.094. The biggest shortfall is the tank, which usually eases outside the chore list (more sleep, a lighter week elsewhere). The calculator’s “what would help most” is the single change with the biggest gain: handing about 2 hours a week from Rosemary to Ken would take balance from 0.83 to 1.00, worth 0.067. Naming one more job’s owner would be worth 0.029, and a tank score lower by 0.10 on average 0.025.

The overall score adds a fourth part, retuning: of the moments that landed badly this week, how many you retuned before answering.

overall = balance × 0.35 + ownership × 0.30 + (1 − tank) × 0.20 + retuning × 0.15 (no rough moments this week: leave retuning out, and divide the first three parts by 0.85)

In Chapter II’s first worked example (balance 0.60, ownership 0.80, an average tank score of 0.40, and 3 of 5 rough moments retuned): 0.21 + 0.24 + 0.12 + 0.09 = 0.66.

The 30-day look-back: what shifted this month?

The look-back splits the last 30 days into four weeks (the first takes the first ten days). For each part you filled in (balance, ownership, the stress tank, and last-minute rescues), it compares the start of the month with the end:

start = the average of weeks 1 and 2 end = the average of weeks 3 and 4 it shifted if end − start is 0.10 or more (balance, ownership, tank), or 1 a week or more (rescues); anything smaller didn’t shift

One next step: a part that moved the wrong way comes first (the biggest move for its size). Otherwise, the first of these that is still true at the end: ownership under 0.70, balance under 0.70, 2 or more rescues a week, or anyone’s tank at 0.60 or more. If none is, keep the same rhythm and look again in 30 days.

Hours to change hands over the month: for each person, the hours they did minus their share of everyone’s hours, added up week by week. Half the sum of those gaps (ignoring the minus signs) is how many hours would change hands to make the month even.

On your own: is your week sustainable?

The “Who does what?” page’s “just me” view adds up the hours your week is spoken for (jobs, the thinking work, care and so on) and compares them with a waking week of about 112 hours (16 waking hours, 7 days).

share = the hours spoken for ÷ 112

Under 50% reads as sustainable, 50% up to 70% as full but workable, and 70% or more as stretched. For example, 50 hours spoken for is 45%, which leaves real room; 80 hours is 71%, a lot to keep up week after week.

All of these are this book’s own ways of putting things into numbers. They have not been tested in research studies, and they shouldn’t be quoted as findings. The free calculators on the website do the arithmetic for you, and each one has a “How is this worked out?” note.

zqglossaryqz

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. More at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Backstop
The second name on a job: someone who notices if it slipped and checks in kindly. Not a supervisor. A job counts as owned once it has an owner; the backstop is a good habit on top.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, how full your stress tank is: a fuller tank means a lower battery. For example: After a poor night and a long workday, your stress tank is nearly full and your battery is low, so a small request feels big.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating. For example: “Load today: heavy. Thank you for the dishes. One small thing: the car. My ask: could you fill it up?”
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately. For example: “How was your day?” “Fine.” Months go by and nobody has asked a real question.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong. For example: Three busy weeks, no real talk, and small things start to feel big.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back. For example: “I need twenty minutes. Can we talk again at eight?”
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover. For example: One person answers a text in a minute; the other answers at the end of the day. Neither is wrong.
Gatekeeping
Holding on to how a home or child-care job must be done, so the other person steps back. Researchers first described it in mothers (“maternal gatekeeping”), but anyone can do it.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
Ledger
A notebook where you write down what happened, so you can look at it calmly later. The book works the same way: see what is really going on, without blame, then change one small thing.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees. For example: Buying the gift is one job. Remembering the birthday, picking the gift and wrapping it are three more.
Mental load
The noticing, planning and remembering behind running a shared life: knowing the shoes are too small, booking the appointment, keeping the list. It is real work, even though nobody sees it.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty. For example: Short night, busy day: the forecast says to keep big talks for tomorrow.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking. For example: Sam owns the recycling: noticing it’s full, taking it out and buying the bags.
Parallel parenting
Co-parenting with as little direct contact as possible: each home runs its own way, and messages stay short, polite and about the children.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade. For example: A pressure of 40 leaves room in the day; 75 means go gently and leave big things for later.
Retune
To retune is to notice you’ve fallen out of step with someone and adjust together: slower, softer, or at a better time. For example: “Can we start that again? I came in too fast.”
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match. For example: You ask “When will you do it?” calmly. They hear pressure. Nobody meant harm, but it crackles.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Unbilled debt
Unbilled debt is the work of a shared life that got done but was never noticed or thanked. For example: You remembered the dentist appointment, and nobody knew there was anything to remember.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day. For example: Some people need a plan in writing. Others think best out loud.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill out on the website. For example: “Who did what” is a week’s log of who did what.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing. For example: “Rest is earned” can feel like plain fact, until you notice it was your family’s rule.

Reading research: a few words in plain English

The chapters often say how sure the research is: “a small study,” “linked with,” “didn’t replicate.” Here is what those words mean, so every note in the book is something you can weigh for yourself.

Correlational (“linked with”)
Two things tend to go together: people who sleep less tend to argue more. It doesn’t show which causes which, or whether a third thing, like a new baby, causes both.
Experiment
Researchers change one thing on purpose, ideally deciding by chance who gets it, and compare. Better at showing cause, though often in a lab, for a short time, with a small group.
Meta-analysis
A study of studies: it pools many studies of the same question. Usually more trustworthy than any one study, but only as good as the studies in it.
Replication
Doing a study again to see if the result holds. When a large team re-ran 100 published psychology studies in 2015, only about a third gave a clear result again, and the effects were on average about half as big. So one study is a start, not the end.
Effect size
How big a difference is, not just whether there is one. A result can be real (“significant”) and still tiny. The book says “small” or “modest” when that is the case.
Who was studied
Much of psychology studies students in Western, educated, well-off countries. A finding may not fit every age, culture or kind of family, and many studies of couples looked only at a mother and a father.
Self-report
People answered questions about themselves. That is often the only way to ask about feelings, but memory and wanting to look good both creep in.
