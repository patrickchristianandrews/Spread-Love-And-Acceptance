Part One: The most important, yourself · Then

Know yourself: then, where your lens came from

This is where the book begins. Part One is about you; Part Two, which opens with its own Preface, is about the people you share a life with; Part Three is about how people work. A one-minute introduction to the whole book.

Why you expect what you expect, and how the rules you picked up growing up still make small decisions for you today.

Five Pillars: This part mostly teaches Pillar III, Read your state first and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person. What are the Five Pillars? (They are five plain ideas the whole program is built on.)

Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed and the kitchen is a mess. Before you’ve had a single thought, you feel it: “Of course. It’s always me.” Nobody said that. But in the house you grew up in, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it. That old rule just made a decision for you.

Or, living alone: Since his wife died, Ray’s house is quiet and nobody will see the dishes. He still can’t sit down until every one is done. Nobody is watching. An old rule from the home he grew up in still is.

  1. You’re not one thing.

    A reaction usually comes from three places at once: how you’re built (your wiring, which stays), what life taught you (patterns, many of which can soften) and what kind of day you’re having (conditions, which pass). This part is about the middle one, the patterns life taught you. Now looks at the other two.

    Dig deeper: the three layers

  2. Your lens came from somewhere.

    Family, school, friends, culture, money at home and what you watched all taught you what to expect: of yourself, of other people, and of how a “good” partner, parent or friend behaves. By adulthood your lens doesn’t feel like a lens. It feels like simply seeing. Where your lens came from walks through it, and you can read one card and stop.

    Dig deeper: the lens idea

  3. Each stage of growing up asks its own question.

    Is the world safe? Can I do it myself? Am I capable? Where do I belong? Can I be close without losing myself? The ages are rough and people move at their own pace. The answers you landed on can become understandings you keep using as an adult.

    Dig deeper: the stages

  4. When something happened matters.

    An experience that touches the question you were answering at the time tends to go deeper. A move at 4 is mostly about routines. A move at 14, in the middle of “Where do I belong?”, can leave a longer feeling of being the outsider. Deeper isn’t permanent, though: people keep learning all their lives.

    Dig deeper: why timing matters

  5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts.

    “I’m only okay when I’m useful.” “My needs are a burden.” “Mistakes mean I’m bad.” A child often can’t know what was going on for the grown-ups. They only know how it felt. As an adult, you’re allowed to look again with the full picture. Looking again doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine, because some things really were not okay.

    Dig deeper: old answers and fairer readings

  6. An old rule can read other people for you.

    If silence at home meant anger, a partner’s quiet evening can feel like trouble is coming. And an expectation nobody said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only miss it: “If you loved me, you’d just know.” “Whoever notices, does it.” Each is somebody’s family rule, not a law of nature. Ask before you conclude: “When you went quiet, what was going on for you?”

    Dig deeper: rules that read others

  7. Write down the rulebook from home.

    Finish these six lines the way your childhood home would have: “In my family, love looked like…” “When someone was upset, we…” “The jobs were split like this…” “Resting was okay when…” “Asking for help meant…” “The unforgivable thing was…” The rulebook page has room to write them. Nothing is saved except on your own device.

    Dig deeper: the six rulebook lines

  8. You get to choose which rules to keep.

    For each line, ask: do I still want this rule? When a reaction feels bigger than the moment, try the one-minute lens check once you’ve settled: Where did I learn this? How old does this feel? Is it true here, with this person, now? What would the adult me choose? Later, you can swap rulebooks with someone you trust and choose shared rules on purpose.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, and choosing together

  9. The past isn’t yours to change. What you carry forward is.

    You couldn’t control the home you grew up in, what other people did, or what you learned before you had all the facts. What is in your hands now is smaller, and stronger: which old rules you keep, how you treat yourself about them, and where you spend your energy today. That includes people. Give your time and care where they’re met with care, and let one-sided effort shrink back to what feels fair. Matching energy with energy isn’t cold. It protects what you have, so you still have something to give.

    Dig deeper: your part, then and now

The ideas, one at a time

Each idea above again, with a real-life example, words you could use and one small step. Read one and stop if you like.

1. You’re not one thing

For example: A grandmother snaps when her grown-up son is late for lunch. Part is her wiring (she likes things on time). Part is an old rule (“late means you don’t care”). And part is that she slept badly.

Words you could use: “Some of this is me being tired, and some of it is an old thing. Give me a minute.”

Try this: Next time you react strongly, ask: is this mostly wiring, something I learned, or just today?

2. Your lens came from somewhere

For example: One friend grew up with big birthday parties, the other with quiet ones. A small dinner feels hurtful to one and perfect to the other.

Words you could use: “In my family this was a big deal. What was it like in yours?”

Try this: Pick one thing you think “everyone” does. Ask one person how their family did it.

3. Each stage asks its own question

For example: Someone who moved schools four times as a teen may still feel like the outsider at every new job.

Words you could use: “I think part of me is still asking whether I belong here.”

Try this: Read the five questions above and notice which one still feels loud. That’s all.

4. When something happened matters

For example: Two adult siblings remember their parents’ divorce differently. One was six and remembers new routines. The other was fifteen and remembers feeling torn in half. Both are true. See Family rifts.

Words you could use: “We were different ages when it happened. It makes sense we remember it differently.”

Try this: Think of one big change from growing up. How old were you, and what were you trying to work out?

5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts

For example: A foster carer who learned that “my needs are a burden” finds it hard to ask for respite (a planned break while someone else cares), even when worn out. See Foster and kinship families.

Words you could use: “An old voice says I shouldn’t ask. The adult me thinks asking is fair.”

Try this: Write one old belief about yourself. Under it, write what you’d tell a friend who believed it.

6. An old rule can read other people for you

For example: A housemate leaves without saying goodbye. If quiet meant anger at home, you may think they’re upset. They may just be late for a shift.

Words you could use: “When you went quiet earlier, I wasn’t sure what it meant. Is everything okay?”

Try this: This week, when you catch yourself guessing what someone’s silence means, ask instead.

7. Write down the rulebook from home

For example: Two partners from different faiths both write “holidays belong to my family,” and finally see why December is hard. See Two faiths, one family.

Words you could use: “Here’s a rule I grew up with. I’m not saying it’s right. I just want you to know it’s there.”

Try this: Finish just one of the six lines today. Leave the others for another day.

8. You get to choose which rules to keep

For example: A team leader sees that “mistakes mean I’m bad” makes them hide problems. New rule: “Mistakes are information. We fix them together.”

Words you could use: “I used to think ___. Now I’m choosing ___.”

Try this: Pick one rule from your list and write the version you would like to live by.

9. What you carry forward is yours

For example: Someone living on their own always plans the meetups with one friend, who never asks back. They stop chasing, keep the door open, and see more of the friends who reply. See Always the one reaching out?

Words you could use: “I’d love to see you. Let me know when works for you.”

Try this: Name one relationship where you give far more than comes back, and give a little less this month. (If you are afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them. Not safe at home? has people to talk to.)

Tuesday morning

Two people get the same text: “Can we talk tonight?” One thinks, “Good, I like knowing ahead.” The other feels their stomach drop, because in the home they grew up in, “we need to talk” meant trouble. Same words. Two different histories.

You, in all your relationships

Your old rules go wherever you go, starting with how you treat yourself.

  • With yourself: The voice you use after a mistake is often an old one.
  • With a partner, or partners: Who notices, who plans, what quiet means. More than two of you means more than two rulebooks. See More than two partners at home.
  • With friends: Who reaches out first, and whether asking feels okay. See Friends.
  • With family: Old roles, like the helper or the peacemaker, switch back on at the door. See For grown-up children and Family rifts.
  • At work: Praised for being useful? You may say yes to everything, then resent it.
  • With housemates: Two rulebooks about mess, noise and guests. See Sharing a room.
  • On your own: After a breakup, old rules can get louder. See On my own.

Looking for your own situation? Help by relationship lists them all.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Ask whether a feeling is the size of this moment or older (Pillar III). Notice what you were praised or scolded for as a child still pulling on you (Pillar V). Holding an old rule in check is real, hidden effort (Pillar I).
  • Between you and others: A learned rule is part of the setup, not a flaw in anyone (Pillar II). Say your rules out loud, and choose shared ones.

In real life

  • Partners: “Of course it’s always me.” You grew up in a house where whoever noticed, did. Naming the old rule out loud, on a calm day, turns a fight about dishes into a talk about who owns what.
  • Family: A parent says, “We should talk.” You brace, because quiet meant anger in the house you grew up in. Knowing where the alarm came from, you ask first: “Is this a good talk or a worried one?”
  • Roommates: One roommate grew up where help was never asked for, only given. The other grew up where nothing happened unless you asked. Neither is rude. They are using two rulebooks.
  • Coworkers: Someone who was praised mostly for being useful says yes to everything, then resents it. Seeing the old rule helps them pause before the next yes.

Common mix-ups

  • “Looking back means blaming my family.” It isn’t about blame. Many of the adults who raised us were working with their own lenses too, and if that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit.
  • “If it started in childhood, I’m stuck with it.” Deeper isn’t permanent. Many old patterns soften with awareness, safety and practice, and some leave a lasting sensitivity you learn to carry and explain.
  • “Every big reaction is an old wound.” Often it’s just a full tank. Sort it first: Chapter III helps tell leftover stress from this-moment stress.
  • “My rulebook is normal, so theirs is wrong.” Each is somebody’s family rule. Two rulebooks in one home are a setup to talk about, not a verdict on either person.
  • “I have to finish this before I go on.” You don’t. You can read one card and stop, and come back another day.
  • “If they won’t look at their rules, there’s no point looking at mine.” There is. Knowing your own rules changes how you react, even if nobody else ever reads a word of this.

Not every home felt calm or kind, so take only what is useful. If looking back feels heavy, you can stop and come back another day, or explore it with a qualified professional, if and when you want that. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared.

Part One: The most important, yourself · Now

Know yourself: now, your wiring, your weather and your words

Why the same words can land differently on different days and in different people, and how to understand today’s you well enough to say what helps.

Five Pillars: This part mostly teaches Pillar III, Read your state first and Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person. What are the Five Pillars? (They are five plain ideas the whole program is built on.)

Sunday, 7:50pm

A friend asks, “Are you okay?” You say, “Fine.” The truth is you can’t tell whether you’re fine, tired or quietly bothered, and you couldn’t explain it to yourself, let alone to them.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part: your temperament, your pace, your senses, how you take in words. You work with it, by building a setup that suits you. Today’s conditions are the passing part: sleep, hunger, load, the room, the time of day. You work around them: eat first, sleep on it, talk tomorrow. Mixing the two up is where a lot of frustration comes from. (Then covered the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    Dig deeper: wiring and conditions

  2. Know how you like to be spoken to.

    Your pace, your tone, how much you need spelled out and what silence means to you all change how words land. Wavelength helps you find words for how you think, talk and listen, with a four-letter Wave Code that is a fun description, not a test. The Wiring Card puts how you take in words on one page you can share before a hard talk, not during one.

    Dig deeper: your wiring and your Wave Code

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Every conversation runs through your own state first. Chapter III goes deep on stress tanks, so this part only adds the self side: a one-minute reading, on your own, before you decide what a hard moment means. Today’s Weather is the quick one, and How much are you carrying? (WP-02) is the fuller one. (“WP” pages are the program’s short worksheets, called workpapers.)

    Dig deeper: reading your state before you speak

  4. Ask where this feeling is coming from.

    Name it (“tight, annoyed, a bit scared”). Then ask: is this the size of the moment, or does it feel older? Which layer is it? And what does it need right now: food, space, a heads-up, reassurance, or a real conversation later? It takes a minute, and it works best once the first wave has passed.

    Dig deeper: a four-step self-check

  5. Plan what settles you on a calm day.

    When you’re wound up is a bad time to work out what helps. The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) is a plan you make while calm: what tends to set you off, two calming steps, and one sentence for stepping away that includes when you’ll be back. It is for you, never for the other person.

    Dig deeper: deciding ahead

  6. Your needs and limits are information, not demands.

    What helps you, what matters to you and what you have left are facts about you. Said kindly, they let other people plan around you: “A heads-up helps me,” “I can take this one, not that one,” “I’m low today, can we talk tomorrow?” WP-01 (Part B) has ready-made kind ways to say no, and WP-09, Say it so it lands, turns a raw reaction into a fact, a feeling and an ask.

    Dig deeper: saying your needs kindly

  7. Being wired differently is not being wrong.

    Some people take in words, noise, change and feelings differently from what most social customs were built around. That is wiring, not a flaw to fix. You can understand it, plan around it and get support for it, if you choose. A mix-up also runs both ways: Wired Differently shows how two differently wired people can each misread the other, with nobody at fault.

    Dig deeper: wiring is not a flaw

  8. Explain yourself without over-sharing.

    Three short sentences cover most moments. “This is how I’m wired. It won’t change, but here’s what helps.” “This is an old alarm. I’m working on it, and here’s what helps in the moment.” “Today’s a low-battery day.” You never owe anyone your history. Share the part that helps them help you, as information and not as a list of rules.

    Dig deeper: scripts, and how much to share

  9. Do your part, and only your part.

    Today you control your words, your effort, your rest, your limits and what you ask for. You don’t control someone else’s mood, reply, pace or choices, and trying to manage them drains you fast. So do your side well, then let go of theirs. And match energy with energy: meet warmth with warmth and effort with effort, and when you keep giving far more than comes back, give a little less until it balances. It protects your battery, and a protected battery is what keeps you kind.

    Dig deeper: your circle of control, and matching energy

The ideas, one at a time

Each idea above again, with a real-life example, words you could use and one small step. Read one and stop if you like.

1. Two layers you can work with

For example: A dad who works nights finds the school-run chatter unbearable at 8am. That isn’t who he is. It’s the hour. He swaps to the afternoon pick-up, and the chatter is fun again. See Different hours.

Words you could use: “It’s not you, it’s the hour. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

Try this: Write one thing that always bothers you (probably wiring) and one that only bothers you when you’re tired (conditions).

2. How you like to be spoken to

For example: A grandmother loves phone calls. Her grandson loves texts. Each thinks the other is being distant. Once they say how they like to talk, they settle on a Sunday call and texts in between.

Words you could use: “I take things in best when ___. How about you?”

Try this: Finish the sentence “I take things in best when…” and keep it for your Wiring Card (a one-page note on how you like to be spoken to).

3. Check your weather first

For example: A long-distance couple notice their hard calls are always at the end of one partner’s late shift. They move serious talks to weekend mornings. See Long distance.

Words you could use: “I’m running on empty tonight. Can we pick this up tomorrow morning?”

Try this: For three mornings, describe your inner weather in one word: sunny, cloudy, stormy or foggy.

4. Where is the feeling coming from?

For example: A coworker says, “Quick question?” and your heart races. Name it: jumpy. Older? Yes, an old boss. Layer: a pattern, plus a busy day. Need: a moment to settle.

Words you could use: “Sure. Give me five minutes and I’ll be right with you.”

Try this: Next time a feeling surprises you, do just the first step: name it in two words.

5. Plan what settles you

For example: A teenager makes a plan with herself. When a row with her mum heats up, she says, “I need ten minutes in my room, then I’ll come back.” And she comes back.

Words you could use: “I’m too wound up to listen well. I need twenty minutes, then I’ll come back.”

Try this: On a calm day, write your one stepping-away sentence, including when you’ll be back.

6. Needs are information

For example: In a home with three partners, Sam needs an hour alone after work. Said once, kindly, it becomes part of the plan instead of a mystery. See More than two partners at home.

Words you could use: “I can do ___. I can’t do ___ this week. Could we ___ instead?”

Try this: Write one need you’ve never said out loud. Practise saying it as a fact, not an apology.

7. Different isn’t wrong

For example: A woman with ADHD keeps forgetting plans with a friend. Instead of “I’m hopeless,” she says what helps: “If you text me the day before, I’ll be there.” See Neurodivergent relationships.

Words you could use: “My brain works a bit differently. Here’s what helps me get it right.”

Try this: Name one setting where you work best (quiet, written down, early, with music) and give yourself it once this week.

8. Explain, without over-sharing

For example: A man with a long-term illness tells a new friend, “Some days I have less energy. If I cancel, it isn’t about you.” He shares what helps, not his medical history. See When one of you is ill.

Words you could use: “Today’s a low-battery day. It isn’t about you.”

Try this: Pick one of the three short sentences above and say it out loud once, on your own.

9. Your part, and only your part

For example: A grown-up daughter keeps trying to stop her father worrying about her. She can’t. She can send a short “Home safe” text after long drives, then let his worry be his. See For grown-up children.

Words you could use: “I can’t stop you worrying, but I can text when I’m home.”

Try this: Write one thing you keep trying to control that isn’t yours. Next to it, write one thing that is. (If you are afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them. Not safe at home? has people to talk to.)

In real life

  • Partners: Sam knows surprise plans make their chest tighten, and says so on a calm day: “A heads-up helps me. It isn’t about you.” Now their partner knows what to try.
  • Family: You need a quiet half hour after a long visit. You say so before you go: “I’ll be good company after a short rest.” It sounds like a plan, not a retreat.
  • Roommates: One roommate needs an hour of quiet after work to recover. The other hears it as being shut out. A one-line note settles it: “Quiet until seven isn’t about you. I’m back after.”
  • Coworkers: Someone thinks best with the agenda a day ahead. They say it once, as how they work and not as an apology: “Could the agenda come the day before?”

Common mix-ups

  • “Knowing myself means finding the right label.” It’s about what helps, not a name. The three layers and a Wave Code are everyday words, not a diagnosis.
  • “‘That’s just how I am’ ends the talk.” It may be true, especially for wiring. It works better as a start: “This is how I’m wired, and here’s what helps.”
  • “If I tell people how I’m wired, they have to manage me.” Telling them helps their words land. You still do your part, and they still have their own needs.
  • “I’m fine” means I’m fine. Sometimes. It can also mean you’ve been tired for so long you stopped noticing. A quick reading each week shows a baseline you’ve gotten used to.
  • “Looking at myself is selfish.” Every conversation runs through your own state. Knowing it is part of being easier to talk with.
  • “If I need something, I have to justify it.” You don’t. “A heads-up helps me” is enough. A reason is a kindness you can offer, not a fee you owe.

The work nobody sees: invisible labor at home

Part Two of the book is about the people you share a life, a home or a job with, and it opens here. Running a life takes a lot of work that nobody sees. This preface helps you make that work visible, to yourself and to anyone you share it with, so you can talk about it fairly and kindly.

Five Pillars: This preface mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load, with help from Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. What are the Five Pillars?

Sunday, 9pm

Someone notices the laundry is still in the washer, the school forms are due on Tuesday, and the birthday card never got mailed. They also notice that nobody else has noticed. The house looks fine. That’s the problem.

Or, living alone: A widower says, “Since my wife died, I notice how many jobs she quietly carried: the birthday cards, the boiler service, knowing who to ring when someone was ill. I never saw them until they stopped getting done.”

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment: none of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you. Over months, it piles up. The book calls this pile unbilled debt. Nobody sends a bill for it. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past.

    For example: Marcus remembers every school form, dentist visit and bit of lunch money for their two kids. His husband, Leo, sees two kids who always seem to have what they need. The work is just out of sight.

    Words you could use: “Can I show you some of the things I keep track of? I’m not asking you to pay anything back. I just want you to see them.”

    Try this: Tonight, write down three things you did today that nobody saw. Don’t do anything with the list yet. Just notice.

    Dig deeper: the work that goes unseen

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, even though nobody can see it.

    For example: In a shared flat, Amira notices the internet deal is ending, compares plans, picks one and checks it went through. Paying took two minutes. The thinking took an evening.

    Words you could use: “Doing it is the quick part. Remembering it and planning it is the part I’d love some help with.”

    Try this: Pick one job at home and split it into four steps: notice, plan, do, check. Who usually does each step?

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen. So the other person isn’t ignoring it. From where they stand, it looks like things take care of themselves.

    For example: Rose minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own doctor visits to fit. Her daughter thinks it all “fits in easily.”

    Words you could use: “I don’t think you’ve been ignoring it. I think it’s been hard to see from where you are.”

    Try this: Ask one person, “What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?” Then just listen, without explaining.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time. Then the talk starts from facts you share, not from two different memories.

    The book calls this shared record a ledger: a plain list of who did what. It’s a way to see, not a bill.

    For example: Two coworkers sharing one job each list a week of tasks. Every client call landed on one person. They don’t argue about last year; they swap Friday calls.

    Words you could use: “I’m not trying to win anything. I’d like us both to be looking at the same list.”

    Try this: Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself: is this to plan next week, or to prove a point? Share it only if the answer is “plan.”

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did, then read the two lists together. That keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, like what the week was like, so you both understand rather than judge.

    For example: Kai, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a home. Each writes only their own list. Reading them together, Sol learns Kai has been calling the landlord every month.

    Words you could use: “I’ve written my side. Would you like to write yours, in your own words, when you’re ready?”

    Try this: Add one line of context to your own list, like “long week at work” or “the kids were ill.” It helps the other person understand, not judge.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what’s happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    For example: Two brothers share their mum’s care. Instead of “you never help,” one says, “There’s some unseen work here we haven’t looked at.” That’s much easier to hear.

    Words you could use: “Let’s look at how it’s set up, not at each other.”

    Try this: Swap one blaming phrase you use, like “you never,” for a plain one, like “this job keeps landing on me.”

    Dig deeper: why the book borrows these words

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool. One week of notes is enough to start. Later come a daily 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying today, a weekly catch-up and a monthly look-back. If you use the monthly look-back later, a few lines a week is all it needs.

    For example: A nurse on nights and a partner on days hardly see each other awake. They start with one week of notes on the fridge. No changes yet.

    Words you could use: “Could we just try one week of notes and see what we notice? We don’t have to change anything yet.”

    Try this: Pick a start day this week and set one reminder on your phone. That’s the whole first step.

    Dig deeper: how the book fits together

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, family, roommates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, but the ideas fit anyone who shares a load.

    For example: A foster carer tracks family visits, social worker calls and school meetings. A teenager in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week. Both count.

    Words you could use: “Whatever we are to each other, we share some work. Could we look at it together?”

    Try this: Name one shared load in your life that isn’t a couple: a family group chat, a shared flat, a team at work.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back toward your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. Why we get out of tune: communication at home

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.” “I know, I’m just asking when.” Four sentences later, nobody is talking about the trash anymore, and neither of you can point to the moment it turned.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. It’s like two radios slightly out of tune: together they make a crackle that neither one was making alone.

    For example: Two housemates. “Is anyone using the oven tonight?” “Why, did I leave a mess?” A plain question was heard as a complaint.

    Words you could use: “I think the words were fine. Something in how we said them got crossed.”

    Try this: After your next small clash, ask yourself: was it the words, or the way they came out?

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on very different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they are close but not quite matched. People who know each other well can still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we’re close to, because we assume they already understand us.

    For example: Two sisters who talk every day clash over holiday plans far more than they ever do with cousins they see once a year. Being close means more overlap.

    Words you could use: “I think I assumed you knew what I meant. Let me say it more clearly.”

    Try this: With someone close, check one meaning today: “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean…?”

    Dig deeper: why close can be noisy

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    How fast you’re talking. The tone, when a simple update is heard as a complaint. And how urgent it feels: “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and next week to the other.

    For example: A business partner texts “Need to talk” between two meetings. The other partner reads it as a crisis and can’t sleep. It was about a printer. A text loses most of the tone, so urgency is easy to misread.

    Words you could use: “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    Try this: Add a time to your next request. Not “at some point,” but “by Friday.”

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you’re tense, you may talk faster and be more likely to hear a pause as a snub. When you’re tired and flat, your replies get slower and shorter. The Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check, helps you guess which one you’re in. It’s a best guess, not a reading.

    For example: A dad gets home from a night shift and answers his teenager with one word. The teen hears “he doesn’t care.” He is just running on empty.

    Words you could use: “I’m running low right now. It’s not about you. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

    Try this: Before a tricky talk, name your state to yourself: calm, revved up or running on empty.

    Dig deeper: how stress shifts your tune

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work fine will still crackle if they’re slightly off. Arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning (getting back in step) will.

    For example: A grandmother tells her grown-up son, “You look tired.” She means care. He hears “you can’t cope.” Neither of them is wrong about what they meant or heard.

    Words you could use: “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Try this: In your next clash, say “we” instead of “you”: “We got crossed there.”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say it out loud: “I think we’re out of sync.” Check how tense you feel. Either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back. Gently slow down, with shorter turns and more pauses. Only then go back to the actual topic.

    For example: Two dorm roommates agree on a pause word, “static,” on a calm day. Mid-argument about noise, one says it. They stop, and come back at 9pm as agreed.

    Words you could use: “I need ten minutes. I’ll come back at half past.”

    Try this: Agree a pause word with someone today, while you are both calm.

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    When you return to the topic, say the fact, how you feel, and what you’d like. “The trash goes out early Wednesday morning. I get anxious when it’s late. Could you take it out tonight?”

    For example: A co-parent texts: “The swim kit wasn’t in the bag. I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?” A fact, a feeling, an ask.

    Words you could use: “The fact is… I feel… Could we…?”

    Try this: Write one fact, feeling and ask sentence about something small, before you need it.

    Dig deeper: going back in

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in (WP-13), can make tiny corrections before small things drift into a big argument. Habit research suggests small routines at a regular time are easier to keep than big ones done rarely. The quiet, easy stretches are the ones that drift most, so keep it going even when nothing seems wrong (see Complacency).

    For example: A long-distance couple in two time zones send one 90-second voice note each morning. It’s tiny, but it keeps them in step.

    Words you could use: “Today I was carrying quite a lot. One thing I liked today was…”

    Try this: Pick a time and a cue, like right after dinner, and try the check-in for three days.

    Dig deeper: staying in tune

Your part, and only your part. You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back. The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

  1. There are two kinds of fair.

    “Did everything get done?” and “Did each of us do what we said we’d do?” Both are reasonable. Friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realizing.

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind judges by outcomes: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy? It’s flexible and easy-going. Done carefully, it counts the cost to whoever covered, but done carelessly, it can hide who is always stepping in.

    Dig deeper: judging by results

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, but it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way.

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. (It can be the same person.) If it’s your job, the question is whether you did it, not whether someone else quietly covered for you. That protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    Dig deeper: agreeing which kind you’ll use

  5. Covering is kind. Say it out loud.

    Helping out when the owner can’t is lovely. Just mention it, so the help is seen: “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Help that stays silent turns into unseen work, and unseen help slowly becomes expected (see Complacency).

    Dig deeper: covering without resentment

  6. Let a comment land before you react.

    When a remark hits harder than it should, say “Give me a second.” The idea is borrowed loosely from brain science, where the brain turns down sounds and sensations automatically, in a fraction of a second. This book’s pause is different: a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. Let the spike pass, then decide whether it needs an answer now, later or not at all.

    Dig deeper: giving a comment time to land

  7. Notice, name, decide.

    Notice it landed hard. Name it kindly: “That hit harder than you probably meant.” Then, once you’ve settled, decide how to reply. Say it so it lands (WP-09) helps with the words.

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    “Was the job done?” is a question about a fact. “Did that comment hurt?” is a question about a feeling. Both matter. Answer them one at a time, not in the same argument.

    Dig deeper: facts and feelings, separately

The monthly look-back: a calm relationship check-in

Once a month, look back for the problems that keep coming back, and fix the setup instead of having the same argument again.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load. What are the Five Pillars?

The third Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth about Tuesday. Or you could notice that Tuesday has now happened three times, and ask what it is about Tuesdays.

  1. Weekly check-ins miss patterns.

    One missed job looks like bad luck. The same job missed week after week is a pattern, and it’s easiest to see when you look at the whole month. The “audit” here is a careful, friendly look back, more like a self-check than an inspection.

    Dig deeper: why once a month

  2. You need four weeks of notes and one list.

    Bring the month’s four weekly notes from WP-01 (the “who did what” log; a few lines a week is enough) and your job list from WP-03 (one owner per job). That’s all. Twenty minutes is usually enough. If either of your stress tanks is high that day (0.60 or more on How much are you carrying? (WP-02)), move it a day or two.

    Dig deeper: getting ready

  3. Mark an ✗ for each week a job slipped.

    A job “slipped” if it was noticed but not done, only done after being raised more than once, or quietly covered by someone who isn’t its owner. Look closely at every job with two or more ✗. Three or four in a month is a real pattern, not just bad luck.

    Dig deeper: gathering the month’s notes

  4. A problem that keeps coming back usually has no real owner.

    It gets patched every week and never fixed. The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The fix is to give the job one owner whose name sticks, or to talk honestly about whether anyone has the time and energy for it.

    Dig deeper: spotting the gaps that keep reopening

  5. Sort each repeat into one of three kinds.

    No clear owner: update the job list. An owner who can’t keep up: have a kind, honest talk. A short-term cause, like illness or travel: note it and move on.

    Dig deeper: sorting each gap

  6. Count the gaps each month.

    Write down how many gaps of each kind you found. If the number of jobs with no clear owner falls over several months, the setup is working; one month can be a normal swing. If it keeps rising, something bigger has changed, and it’s worth a talk.

    Dig deeper: keeping count

  7. Look back even when nothing is slipping.

    A month with no gaps is good news, and it’s also the month most likely to go unchecked. When something has worked for a while, people stop looking, and nothing breaks on the day that happens. Keep the look-back going on quiet months, and add one question: “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?” That is how a look-back guards against complacency.

    Dig deeper: the quiet months

  8. It’s not a review of either person.

    You don’t add up old hours or punish old mistakes. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past. The only question is where the setup isn’t working yet, and what you’ll try next month.

    Dig deeper: what the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

  9. Keep it short and kind.

    Start with something that went well. Talk about the job, not the person. If a gap needs a proper talk, set it for another day using Check-ins. If a talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.

How feelings work: signals, the body, and naming what you feel

What feelings are for, where they start, and why putting a name to one can make it easier to carry. For anyone: on your own, at home, with friends, at work or out in the world.

In short:

  • A feeling is a signal, not a verdict. It says “something here matters to me.” It doesn’t say what to do.
  • Feelings start in the body. A tight chest or a hot face often comes before the word for it.
  • Naming helps. “I feel let down” is easier to hold than a big, nameless rush.
  • Feeling it isn’t doing it. You can let a feeling be there and still choose what you do next.

Thursday, 4:50pm

A message from your manager: “Got a minute tomorrow?” Your stomach drops. By the time you get home you have snapped at the cat, skipped dinner and replayed every mistake from the last month. Nobody has said anything bad. Your body heard “trouble” before you had a single clear thought.

This part of the book is about how people work: you, and everyone around you. We start with feelings, because they are under almost everything else. How we talk, how we see each other and how we handle stress all run through them. This chapter teaches how feelings work first. What to do with them comes second.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear says “something might be a danger.” Anger says “something seems unfair, or in my way.” Sadness says “I lost something that mattered.” A signal is worth listening to. But it can be wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm can go off for burnt toast.

    For example: You feel jealous when a friend makes a new close friend. The jealousy isn’t proof that you’re a bad person, or that your friend is leaving. It is a signal that the friendship matters to you.

    Thoughts you could try: “This feeling is telling me something matters. What is it?” “Is this the fire, or the toast?”

    Try this: Next time a strong feeling arrives, finish this sentence: “This feeling is trying to protect…”

    Dig deeper: what feelings are for

  2. Feelings start in the body.

    Often your body reacts before your mind has a word: a racing heart, a hot face, a heavy chest, a knot in the stomach. Your brain then tries to make sense of it. That’s why being hungry, tired or in pain can feel a lot like being upset. The body is sending a strong signal, and the mind guesses what it means.

    For example: A teenager comes home “furious” at their parent over nothing much. After a sandwich and a sit-down, the fury is mostly gone. Some of it was hunger wearing anger’s coat.

    Thoughts you could try: “Where is this in my body?” “Am I hungry, tired, cold or sore right now?”

    Try this: Three times today, pause and ask: “What does my body feel like right now?” Just notice. No need to change anything.

    Dig deeper: the body and the brain’s best guess

  3. Naming a feeling helps you hold it.

    Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down a little. In brain-scan research by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), simply putting a name to a feeling (“I feel scared”) turned down the brain’s alarm response. A name gives a big, cloudy rush some edges, so you can see it, talk about it and decide what to do.

    For example: Two housemates are tense all evening. One finally says, “I think I’m embarrassed about the noise complaint, and I’ve been acting cross.” The other relaxes. Now they know what’s in the room.

    Words you could use: “I think what I’m feeling is…” “I’m not sure what this is yet. Maybe worried?”

    Try this: Once a day, name one feeling out loud or on paper. A guess is fine. “Something like sad” still counts.

    Dig deeper: why naming works

  4. More words, better tools.

    “Bad” is a very blunt word. “Disappointed,” “left out,” “overwhelmed” and “ashamed” each point to something different, and each needs something different. A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need fancy words. You can use numbers, colors or body words too.

    For example: A dad tells his partner, “I feel bad about my mum.” They talk in circles. Then he says, “Actually, I feel guilty that I haven’t visited, and scared she’s getting worse.” Now the talk has somewhere to go.

    Words you could use: “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a grey day.”

    Try this: When you notice “bad,” try for one more exact word. When you notice “fine,” try for one more honest one.

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the first one you felt. Anger is often a cover for hurt or fear, because anger feels stronger and safer. And we often have feelings about our feelings: ashamed of being sad, scared of being angry. That second layer can hurt more than the first.

    For example: A grandmother is short with her daughter after the grandkids’ visit is cancelled. Under the shortness is hurt. Under the hurt is a fear: “Am I still needed?”

    Thoughts you could try: “What might be under this one?” “It’s okay to feel this. I don’t also have to feel bad for feeling it.”

    Try this: Next time you feel angry, ask gently: “Is there hurt or worry underneath?” You don’t have to say it. Just look.

    Dig deeper: first feelings and feelings about feelings

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise, peak and settle if you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are different. They are like weather (in plain words: a background feeling that lasts hours or days) that colors everything, often without you noticing. A grey mood can make a small thing feel huge, and a good mood can make the same thing feel like nothing.

    For example: A woman living alone has a bad morning and decides “my whole life is going nowhere.” By evening, after a walk and a call with her sister, the same life looks ordinary and okay. The facts didn’t change. Her weather did.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is a wave. It will peak and pass.” “Is this a feeling about now, or a mood coloring everything?”

    Try this: Don’t make big decisions at the top of a wave. Write the decision down, and look at it again tomorrow.

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We pick up each other’s feelings, often without noticing: a tense voice, a sigh, a slammed door. That’s part of how people bond. It also means stress can move round a home or a team like a cold. You can care about someone’s feeling without catching all of it.

    For example: A team leader comes into a meeting rushed and anxious. Within ten minutes the whole team feels behind. The next week, she takes two slow breaths at the door first. The meeting feels different.

    Thoughts you could try: “Is this feeling mine, or did I pick it up?” “I can stay beside their storm without standing in the middle of it.”

    Try this: Notice who you tend to “catch” feelings from. Before your next talk with them, settle yourself for one minute first.

    Dig deeper: how feelings spread

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Many people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private: “Don’t make a fuss,” “Big boys don’t cry,” “We don’t talk about that.” This is common for men and for older people, and in some cultures it is a sign of respect and strength. It isn’t a flaw. And it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely say a feeling show it every day by doing: fixing the tap, driving someone to hospital, turning up. Caring by doing is real caring. Words can be added slowly, at your own pace, a few at a time.

    For example: A retired builder whose wife has died has never talked about feelings in his life. At his grandson’s football match, he finally says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder. Nothing else needs to be said.

    Words you could use: “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    Try this: Pick one sentence above and say it once this week, to one person, or even just out loud on your own. Short is fine. A text counts. So does writing it down.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually comes with an urge: to shout, run, hide, check, fix or text. The feeling isn’t a choice. What you do with the urge is more of one. Pushing a feeling down tends to make it come back stronger, and acting on every urge can hurt people. The middle path is: let the feeling be there, and choose the action.

    For example: A father feels a hot rush of anger when his son breaks a window with a ball. He says, “I’m too cross to talk right now. Let’s sort this out in ten minutes.” The anger was real. The shouting didn’t happen.

    Words you could use: “I’m feeling a lot right now. I’m going to take a minute before I answer.” “I want to reply when I’m calmer.”

    Try this: Pick one urge you often act on too fast, like sending a sharp text. Make a rule: wait ten minutes first.

    Dig deeper: feelings, urges and choices

You notice the knot in your stomach again. This time you name it: “I’m scared I’m in trouble.” You remember it’s a signal, not a verdict, and you ask your manager straight away: “Is tomorrow’s chat about something I should worry about?” “Oh no, it’s about the summer rota.” The knot loosens. In everyday life

  • On your own: No one else is there to notice for you, so a daily one-word check-in with yourself matters more. See On my own.
  • With a partner: “What are you feeling?” can sound like a test. “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is easier to answer.
  • With friends: A friend going quiet may be feeling low, not cross with you. See Friends.
  • Parents and children: Children learn feeling words from us. Naming your own (“I’m frustrated, so I’m taking a breath”) teaches more than any lesson. See For parents.
  • At work: Feelings don’t stay at home. A short “I’m a bit flat today, not upset with anyone” can stop a team guessing. See At work.
  • If you show love by doing: Fixing, driving, cooking and turning up are love. If someone close shows care that way, you can thank them for it in words: “I noticed you cleared the drive. Thank you.”
  • With strangers: The rude person in the queue may be scared, in pain or grieving. You can’t know. You can choose not to catch it.

Common mix-ups

  • “Some feelings are bad.” Feelings aren’t good or bad. Some are painful, and some actions are harmful. The feeling itself is information.
  • “If I feel it, it must be true.” Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid. A feeling is a signal to check, not a fact.
  • “Letting it out always helps.” Shouting or hitting a pillow tends to keep anger going. Naming it, moving, or talking it through calmly helps more.
  • “Calm people don’t feel much.” Many calm people feel a lot. They have just practised letting feelings be there without acting on every one.
  • “I should be able to name it straight away.” Many people find feelings hard to name, and some find it very hard. That’s a difference, not a failing. Body words, numbers and colors count.
  • “Their face tells me what they feel.” Faces are a poor guide. People show the same feeling in different ways. Asking is kinder and more accurate than guessing.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask, don’t assign. “You’re angry” can feel like a label. “You seem upset. Am I reading that right?” leaves room for them to correct you.
  • Make the feeling make sense. “Of course you’re upset, that was a lot” helps more than advice. You can make sense of a feeling without agreeing with every action.
  • Offer choices. “Do you want ideas, or just someone to listen?” Many people only want the second.
  • Accept other ways of saying it. Some people find words hard. A number, a text, a drawing or “I’m at a seven” is a real answer.
  • Don’t make their feeling about you. If their sadness makes you anxious, settle yourself first, so they don’t have to look after your feelings too.

Words you could use: “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”

When to get more help

Everyone has hard days and big feelings. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or another trained professional if:

  • a low, numb, anxious or angry feeling stays most days for two weeks or more;
  • feelings are stopping you from sleeping, eating, working or seeing people;
  • you are using alcohol, drugs or other things to stop feeling;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that’s happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. You deserve help today.

If you feel afraid of someone you live with or are close to, that fear is an important signal. It is not something to “manage” on your own. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.

Stress and the nervous system: the ladder, flooding, rest and recovery

What stress does to your body and your mind, why kind people say things they don’t mean when they are flooded, and the kinds of rest that actually help you recover.

In short:

  • Stress is your body getting ready for a demand. A little helps. Too much, for too long, wears you down.
  • Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty. Each one changes how you think and hear.
  • Flooded means offline. When stress is very high, listening and careful thinking get much harder. Pause, settle, then come back.
  • Rest is a skill. Different kinds of tired need different kinds of rest.

Monday, 7:52am

The bus is late, the kids can’t find a shoe, and your phone buzzes with a work message. Your partner says, “Have you seen my keys?” and you hear yourself snap, “I’m not your secretary!” A second later you think, “Where did that come from?” It came from a body that was already full.

The last chapter looked at feelings. This one looks at the system underneath them: your nervous system, and what happens to it under pressure. Understanding stress won’t make life less busy. It can make your reactions, and other people’s, a lot less mysterious.

  1. Stress is your body getting ready.

    When something needs dealing with, your body gears up: the heart speeds, breathing quickens, muscles tense and stress hormones rise. This is useful. It helps you catch a falling glass, meet a deadline or speak up. The trouble starts when the body stays geared up for too long, with no chance to come back down.

    For example: A nurse handles an emergency calmly and well. Two hours later, at home, she can’t sit still and cries at an advert. Her body is still running the emergency.

    Thoughts you could try: “My body is getting ready for something. That’s its job.” “The emergency is over. I can let my body catch up.”

    Try this: After something stressful, give your body a clear “it’s over” signal: a walk, a shower, a stretch or a song.

    Dig deeper: the stress response

  2. Stress depends on what you have to meet it.

    The same event can be easy for one person and huge for another. Stress grows when a demand looks bigger than what you have to meet it: time, energy, money, help, skills or sleep. That’s why a small thing can tip you over on a bad day, and why giving someone more support can shrink their stress without changing the task at all.

    For example: Two coworkers get the same tight deadline. One has a quiet evening ahead. The other has a sick child at home. Same task. Very different stress.

    Words you could use: “It’s not the task, it’s that I don’t have much left today.” “What would make this feel more doable?”

    Try this: When something feels huge, write two lists: what it asks of you, and what you have. Then ask what could make the second list longer.

    Dig deeper: demands and resources

  3. The ladder: three everyday states.

    The ladder is a picture, not a real ladder. In plain words, it means three states your body moves between during the day. This site uses three plain words for them. Calm and connected: breathing is easy and you can hear hard things. Revved up: fast, tense, and small things feel urgent or personal. Running on empty: flat, slow and short of words. You move up and down all day. The Nervous System Ladder is a thirty-second self-check. It gives a best guess, not a reading.

    For example: A grandfather minding a toddler all afternoon is calm at two, revved up by four and running on empty by six. The same toddler, the same grandfather. The state changed.

    Words you could use: “I’m revved up right now.” “I’m running on empty. Can this wait till morning?”

    Try this: Set three reminders today. Each time, pick a rung: calm, revved up or running on empty. No judging, just noticing.

    Dig deeper: the ladder

  4. When you’re flooded, thinking goes offline.

    At the top of the stress curve, the body is in full alarm. Careful thinking, listening and seeing the other side get much harder. Many people call this being flooded. You might say things you don’t mean, freeze, or only want to get away. It isn’t a character flaw. It’s a body that needs to come down before the talk can go on.

    For example: Two adult siblings start sorting their late father’s things. Within minutes they are shouting about a lamp. Neither cares about the lamp. Both are flooded with grief.

    Words you could use: “I’m too flooded to listen well. Can we stop for twenty minutes and come back at four?”

    Try this: Learn your own flood signs: heat, a racing heart, tunnel vision, a loud inner voice. When you notice two of them, pause.

    Dig deeper: flooding

  5. Stress adds up, like a tank filling.

    Think of a tank (in plain words: how much stress you are already carrying today). Stress from one place spills into the next. Traffic, a hard meeting, a noisy shop and a worrying text can all fill the same tank. By evening, a tiny thing makes it overflow. The last drop gets the blame, but the tank was nearly full already. Small daily hassles count, not just big events.

    For example: A teenager explodes when asked to empty the dishwasher. The dishwasher isn’t the problem. A friendship fell apart at lunch, and the dishwasher was the last drop.

    Words you could use: “My tank was already full when I got home. That wasn’t really about you.” “Rough day? You don’t have to explain.”

    Try this: Before reacting to a small thing in the evening, ask: “How full was I before this happened?” Part Two’s Chapter III has a simple way to tell.

    Dig deeper: why stress adds up

  6. Settle the body first.

    You can’t think your way out of a body in alarm. You can help the body come down, and the thinking often follows. Slow breathing with long out-breaths, moving, cold water on your face, a familiar song, pressure (like a heavy blanket) or simply leaving a noisy room can all help. Different things work for different people.

    For example: A manager feels her heart race before giving hard feedback. She walks round the block once, breathing out slowly. She goes in steadier.

    Thoughts you could try: “Body first, words second.” “What has helped me settle before?”

    Try this: On a calm day, list three things that settle you. Keep the list on your phone. The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) helps you build it.

    Dig deeper: settling the body

  7. Rest isn’t one thing.

    Lying on the sofa scrolling isn’t always rest. Recovery comes in different kinds: sleep, switching off from work in your head, real relaxation, doing something absorbing you enjoy, and feeling in charge of your own time. Different kinds of tired need different kinds of rest. Being with people restores some people and drains others.

    For example: Housemates plan a “relaxing” Saturday party. One loves it and wakes up refreshed. The other needs all of Sunday alone to recover. Both rested. They just needed different things.

    Words you could use: “I need quiet rest tonight, not people rest. It’s not about you.” “What kind of tired are you?”

    Try this: Ask yourself: am I body-tired, people-tired, brain-tired or feelings-tired? Pick a rest that matches.

    Dig deeper: kinds of rest

  8. Sleep and stress feed each other.

    Stress makes sleep harder, and poor sleep makes stress hit harder. After a short or broken night, small things feel bigger, tempers are shorter and people pull away from each other. In a brain-scan study by Yoo and colleagues (2007), one night without sleep made the brain’s alarm centre react more strongly to upsetting pictures. In a daily study by Gordon and Chen (2014), people reported more conflict with their partner after nights of poor sleep. And in experiments by Ben Simon and Walker (2018), people who had missed a night’s sleep kept others at a greater distance and felt lonelier. You can’t always fix sleep, but you can notice when it’s part of the story.

    For example: A nurse coming off three night shifts finds herself in tears over a parking ticket. Her sister says, “You’ve barely slept. Let’s sort it out on Thursday.” On Thursday, it’s just a parking ticket.

    Words you could use: “I slept badly. If I’m short with you today, that’s why, and it’s not about you.” “Can we talk about this after we’ve both slept?”

    Try this: Before a hard conversation, ask: “Have we both slept?” If not, pick another time. At night, try the same few calm steps before bed, in the same order, so your body learns the signal. If poor sleep goes on for weeks, talk to a doctor. Sleep problems are common and often treatable.

    Dig deeper: sleep and stress

  9. Stress is easier to carry when it’s shared.

    People calm each other. A steady voice, a hand on the shoulder or just a quiet person nearby can help a nervous system settle. Saying your state out loud also helps others read you right: “I’m stressed about work” stops a partner or friend from thinking it’s about them. Sharing stress isn’t dumping it. It’s letting someone stand beside you.

    For example: A father tells his ten-year-old, “I’m a bit stressed about money this month. It’s my grown-up job to sort it, and you’re not in trouble.” His son stops tiptoeing round him.

    Words you could use: “I’m stressed, and it’s not about you.” “Do you want help, a hug, or just some space?”

    Try this: Tonight, tell one person your state in one sentence, and ask theirs.

    Dig deeper: stress between people

In everyday life

  • On your own: No one notices you’re running on empty, so build rest in before you need it. See On my own.
  • With a partner: Agree a pause word on a calm day, and that whoever pauses names a time to come back. See One of you wants to talk now, the other needs space.
  • With friends: A friend who cancels again may be overwhelmed, not uninterested. See Friends.

  • Shift work, illness and caring: Some people run on empty for structural reasons. That calls for a different setup, not more effort. See Different hours, When one of you is ill and Caregivers.

Common mix-ups

  • “All stress is bad.” Short bursts of stress help you rise to a challenge. The problem is stress with no recovery.
  • “If I’m calm, I can’t be upset.” Calm means steady, not happy. You can be calm and sad, or calm and firm.
  • “Pausing is avoiding.” A pause with a time to come back is a way to finish the talk well. A pause with no return is avoiding.
  • “Rest is lazy.” Recovery is part of the work. Bodies and minds that never recover tend to break down.
  • “Everyone should be able to handle what I can.” People have different loads, different bodies and different resources. Compare the load, not the person.
  • “Running on empty means they don’t care.” Short replies and flat faces often mean tired, not cold.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be the calm, not the coach. A lower voice, slower words and a steady presence help more than “calm down.”
  • Lighten the load, not just the mood. Taking one task off someone’s list can do more than any pep talk.
  • Ask what kind of help. Practical help, company, a distraction or space? Ask, don’t guess.
  • Don’t debate someone who is flooded. Offer a break and a time to come back. Facts can wait twenty minutes.
  • Notice long stretches. If someone has been running on empty for weeks, gently mention it, and help them find support.

Words you could use: “You seem really stretched. Is there one thing I could take off your plate?” “We don’t have to sort this now. Shall we pick it up after dinner?”

When to get more help

Stress is normal. Constant stress that never lets up is a warning sign. It may be time to see a doctor, counsellor or other trained professional if:

  • you’ve felt exhausted, dread-filled or numb most days for several weeks, even after rest;
  • you can’t sleep, or sleep but never feel rested;
  • you have physical signs that worry you, such as chest pain, constant headaches or stomach trouble (always get chest pain checked straight away);
  • you’re relying on alcohol, drugs or other habits to get through the day;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that is happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line.

If the stress comes from being afraid of someone, such as how they will react, what they will do or whether you are allowed to leave, that is not ordinary stress. Your body may be warning you. Not safe at home? has free, private help.

How we see each other: perception, bias, and the stories we tell

Why two people can watch the same moment and come away with two different stories, and how to see others, and yourself, a little more fairly.

In short:

  • Seeing is a best guess. Your brain fills in a lot of what you think you see and hear.
  • We fill gaps with stories, and the story often feels like a fact.
  • We judge ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions. That gap causes a lot of hurt.
  • Ask, don’t guess. Asking is kinder and more accurate than reading minds.

Wednesday, 12:15pm

You wave at a coworker across the canteen. She looks straight through you and walks off. All afternoon you wonder what you did. At four, she messages: “Sorry I didn’t stop, I’d just had a call about my dad.” She never saw you. The whole story was yours.

The first two chapters of Part Three looked inside: feelings, and the stress that shapes them. This chapter looks outward, at how we see other people. It explains why misunderstandings are so common, even between kind and clever people, and what helps.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think. Your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well and fast. But it means what you see is partly what you expected to see. Two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

    For example: Two parents watch their son at a football match. One sees him “giving up.” The other sees him “being careful with a sore ankle.” Same boy. Different guesses.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is what I see. It might not be all there is.” “What else could this be?”

    Try this: Next time you’re sure what someone meant by a look, think of two other things it could have meant.

    Dig deeper: perception as a best guess

  2. You see what you’re looking for.

    Attention is like a torch in a dark room. What it lights up, you see. What it doesn’t, you can miss, even if it’s right in front of you. If you’re looking for proof that someone doesn’t care, you’ll notice every missed call and none of the little kindnesses.

    For example: A woman decides her new manager “doesn’t like her.” For a month she notices every short email. She misses that he gave her the best project.

    Thoughts you could try: “What might I be missing?” “If I were looking for the opposite, what would I see?”

    Try this: For one week, write down one kind thing a person you’re struggling with does each day. Just notice.

    Dig deeper: the torch of attention

  3. We fill the gaps with stories.

    When we don’t know why someone did something, the mind makes up a reason, fast. A late reply becomes “they’re angry.” A closed door becomes “they don’t want me.” The story arrives feeling like a fact. Memory works this way too: each time we remember something, we rebuild it a little, so two people can honestly remember the same evening differently.

    For example: A friend doesn’t reply for two days. You decide she’s fed up with you. She was on a camping trip with no signal.

    Words you could use: “The story I’m telling myself is that you’re upset with me. Is that right?”

    Try this: When you feel hurt by something someone did, write down the story you’re telling yourself. Then write one other possible story.

    Dig deeper: stories and memory

  4. We judge ourselves by our reasons, and others by their actions.

    When you are late, you know why: the traffic, the phone call, the child who lost a shoe. When someone else is late, you only see that they’re late, and it’s easy to think “they’re careless.” We all tend to blame other people’s character for things that are really about their situation.

    For example: A driver cuts in front of you, and you think “what an idiot.” Next week, rushing to the hospital, you cut someone up yourself, and you know you’re not an idiot.

    Thoughts you could try: “What might be going on for them that I can’t see?” “If I did this, what would my reason be?”

    Try this: When someone annoys you today, think of one situation that would explain it without making them a bad person.

    Dig deeper: actions and reasons

  5. Quick shortcuts can lead us astray.

    The brain uses shortcuts to save time. Many are useful. Some lead to unfair judgments: a first impression that colors everything after it, a bad moment that outweighs ten good ones, or a quick guess about someone based on their age, accent, looks or group. Having these shortcuts doesn’t make you a bad person. Not noticing them can make you an unfair one.

    For example: A housemate decides on day one that the new tenant is “messy” because of one box in the hall. Every bit of mess after that is “proof,” while her own mess goes unseen.

    Thoughts you could try: “Am I looking for proof of what I already think?” “Would I judge this the same if someone I like did it?”

    Try this: Pick one person you’ve made your mind up about. Look for one thing that doesn’t fit your view of them.

    Dig deeper: shortcuts and bias

  6. Everyone feels like the one who sees clearly.

    Most of us feel we see things as they really are, and that anyone who disagrees must be missing something, biased or not thinking straight. The other person feels exactly the same about you. Neither of you can see your own lens, only theirs.

    For example: Two adult siblings argue about how their parents raised them. Each says, “I’m just being realistic.” Both are telling the truth about what they lived through.

    Words you could use: “I think we’re both seeing this from where we stand. Can you tell me how it looks from yours?”

    Try this: In your next disagreement, explain the other person’s view back to them until they say, “Yes, that’s it.” Then give yours.

    Dig deeper: why we each feel like the realist

  7. “Us” and “them” changes what we see.

    People quickly split into groups, even over tiny things. We tend to see our own group as varied and full of individuals, and other groups as “all the same.” We give our own side the benefit of the doubt. Spending real time with people from the other group, as equals, is one of the best ways to soften this.

    For example: At work, the night shift calls the day shift “lazy,” and the day shift calls the night shift “sloppy.” After a joint team day, they start saying names instead of “them.”

    Thoughts you could try: “Would I say this about one of ‘us’?” “Who in that group do I actually know?”

    Try this: Have one real conversation this month with someone from a group you tend to see as “other.” Ask about their day, not their views.

    Dig deeper: us and them

  8. Ask, don’t guess.

    Imagining yourself in someone’s shoes is a kind start. But research suggests it doesn’t make us much more accurate about what they think. Asking does. A simple, curious question beats the cleverest guess. “What was that like for you?” is a better tool than “I know exactly how you feel.”

    For example: A grandmother thinks her teenage grandson hates visiting because he’s on his phone. She asks. He’s messaging a friend who is having a hard time, and he likes being at her house because it’s calm.

    Words you could use: “Can I check what you meant?” “What was going on for you?” “I might have this wrong. How did it look from your side?”

    Try this: Once today, swap a guess for a question.

    Dig deeper: seeing more fairly

Thursday, 12:20pm

You see your coworker in the canteen again. This time you go over: “How’s your dad?” She tells you. You sit together. Yesterday’s story about what you did wrong is gone. It was only ever a guess.

In everyday life

  • On your own: We tell stories about ourselves too. “I always mess things up” is a story built from a few bright memories. Look for the ones it leaves out. See Know your own wiring.
  • With a partner: After years together, we stop checking and start assuming. “I know what you meant” is the most dangerous sentence in a long relationship.
  • With friends: A friend going quiet is rarely about you. Ask before you decide. See Friends.
  • Parents and children: Children are often labelled early: “the difficult one,” “the easy one.” Labels can become lenses that only see what fits. See For parents.
  • At work: Short emails, flat faces and different working styles are easy to misread. See At work.
  • In your community: People from other backgrounds, faiths or generations are easiest to see as “all the same.” See Two faiths, one family.

Common mix-ups

  • “I just see things as they are.” Everyone sees through a lens. Knowing that is where fairness starts.
  • “Being biased means being a bad person.” Everyone uses mental shortcuts. What matters is noticing them and checking.
  • “If I imagine their side hard enough, I’ll understand.” Imagining is a start. Asking is usually more accurate.
  • “My memory is a recording.” Memory is rebuilt each time. Two honest people can remember the same event differently.
  • “Giving the benefit of the doubt means ignoring what happened.” No. It means checking the story before deciding. If something hurtful keeps happening, that matters.
  • “Their face or tone told me what they meant.” Faces and tones vary a lot between people and cultures, and between neurotypes. They are clues, not proof.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Don’t argue with their story first. Make sense of how they feel, then gently ask, “Is there any other way to see it?”
  • Offer information, not verdicts. “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.”
  • Model asking. Let people see you check your own guesses out loud: “I assumed he was annoyed. I’ll ask.”
  • Be careful with labels. Labels for children, coworkers or family members stick, and then shape what everyone sees.
  • Believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone their experience didn’t happen.

Words you could use: “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”

When to get more help

Seeing other people’s side is a strength. It should never mean doubting yourself when you know something is wrong. If someone often tells you that what you saw, heard or remember didn’t happen, that you’re “too sensitive” or “imagining it,” and you find yourself unsure of your own mind, take that seriously. It can be part of controlling behaviour. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? can help, privately.

It may also help to talk to a counsellor or other trained professional if you notice you often feel others are against you, find it hard to trust anyone, or keep getting stuck in the same painful stories about yourself. Those patterns are common, understandable and often change with support.

Part Three · Chapter 4

How communication works: meaning, listening, repair and messages

Talking feels simple, because we do it all day. But every message has to travel from one mind to another, and a lot can change on the way. This chapter explains how that journey works, and what helps a message arrive the way you meant it.

In short:

  • Meaning is made between two people. What you say and what they hear can be different, and nobody has to be wrong.
  • Every message says two things: the words, and what they seem to say about the two of you.
  • Check, don’t guess. A short question saves a long misunderstanding.
  • Mix-ups are normal. Repair is the skill. And small talk is allowed to be short.

Thursday, 8:15am

“Can you call me when you’re free?” Ana meant: no rush, I have a small question about the weekend. Her brother read it on the bus and spent the whole ride wondering who was ill. Nobody did anything wrong. The message just arrived carrying something it was never meant to carry.

This chapter explains how a message travels from one mind to another, what can change on the way, and the small habits that help it arrive: listening, checking, starting soft, repairing, and a few ready-made lines for small talk.

  1. Meaning is made between two people.

    Words don’t carry meaning on their own. The person speaking has a meaning in mind. The person listening builds their own meaning from the words, the tone, the timing and everything they already expect. Most of the time the two match well enough. Sometimes they don’t, and neither person can see the gap from where they stand.

    For example: A manager writes “Let’s chat tomorrow” to a new coworker. She means a friendly catch-up. He spends the evening sure he is in trouble.

    Words you could use: “I think what I meant and what you heard might be two different things. Can we check?”

    Try this: Today, notice one moment when someone took your words differently from how you meant them. Just notice. No blame.

    Dig deeper: how meaning travels

  2. Every message says two things.

    There is what you say, and there is what it says about the two of you. “You’re late” is a fact about time. It can also say “I was worried,” or “I don’t matter to you.” People often argue about the fact when the hurt is in the second layer.

    For example: Two housemates. “Did you use my pan?” is about a pan. Underneath, it may be “Do you respect my things?” Talking only about the pan won’t settle that.

    Words you could use: “I don’t really mind about the pan. I think I wanted to know my stuff matters here.”

    Try this: Next time a small thing stings, ask yourself: what did that seem to say about us?

    Dig deeper: the two layers of a message

  3. Listening is something you do, not something that happens.

    Hearing is automatic. Listening is a choice. Most of us listen partly to reply: to fix, defend, advise or tell our own story. Real listening means following what the other person is trying to say, and letting them know you got it, before you add anything of your own.

    For example: A teenager says school is “pointless.” Her dad starts to explain why it isn’t. She stops talking. Next time, he tries “Pointless how? Tell me about today.” She talks for twenty minutes.

    Words you could use: “So what I’m hearing is... Have I got that right?” “Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?”

    Try this: In one conversation today, wait until the other person has finished, then say back what you heard before you reply.

    Dig deeper: what good listening is

  4. Check, don’t guess.

    Once we know something, it’s hard to imagine not knowing it. So we leave out steps, assume the other person can tell what we mean, and guess what they meant instead of asking. We do this most with people we know well. A short check costs a sentence. A wrong guess can cost a whole evening.

    For example: Two friends plan “something low-key” for a birthday. One pictures a takeaway on the sofa. The other books a quiet restaurant. Both are disappointed, and both thought it was obvious.

    Words you could use: “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean...?” “When you say soon, do you mean today or this week?”

    Try this: Pick one person you know well and check one meaning with them today, even if you feel sure.

    Dig deeper: why we guess, and how to check

  5. Hard things land better when they start soft.

    How a hard talk begins often shapes how it ends. A complaint about one thing (“The bins weren’t taken out”) is easier to hear than a criticism of the person (“You never help”). A simple shape works for most hard things: what happened, how you feel, and what you’d like.

    For example: A woman wants her elderly father to stop driving at night. Instead of “You’re not safe anymore,” she says: “You had two near-misses last month. I lie awake worrying. Could I drive us in the evenings?”

    Words you could use: “What happened was... I felt... Could we...?”

    Try this: Write one fact, feeling and ask sentence about something small that has been bothering you. You don’t have to send it yet.

    Dig deeper: saying hard things kindly

  6. Mix-ups are normal. Repair is the skill.

    Even close, kind people misunderstand each other all the time. What keeps a relationship steady isn’t never getting it wrong. It’s noticing and fixing it: “That came out wrong,” “I think I misread you,” “Can we start again?” Small repairs, made often, build trust.

    For example: At the supermarket, a stranger snaps “Excuse me!” when you block the aisle. You snap back. A second later you add, “Sorry, long day.” They smile: “Me too.” A tiny repair, and the moment is gone.

    Words you could use: “Can I try that again?” “I think I took that the wrong way. What did you mean?”

    Try this: The next time something comes out wrong, repair it within a minute, out loud. Notice how small it can be.

    Dig deeper: how repair works

  7. Messages lose their tone on the way.

    A text or email carries the words but not your face, your voice or your timing. So the reader fills in the tone, and they fill it in with however they feel right now. A tired reader hears a tired, cross message. Short replies, missing greetings and late-night timing all get read as meaning something.

    For example: A group chat for a school fundraiser. One parent replies “Fine.” to a plan. Two others decide she is annoyed. She was typing one-handed while cooking.

    Words you could use: “Quick reply, not a cross one!” “This isn’t urgent. Any time this week is fine.”

    Try this: Before you send your next tricky message, read it out loud in the grumpiest voice you can. If it sounds harsh, add a warm word or a time frame.

    Dig deeper: texts, email and tone

  8. Different styles aren’t wrong styles.

    Some people say exactly what they mean. Others hint and expect you to read between the lines. Some talk fast and overlap; others need a pause to think. Families, cultures and different kinds of minds all shape this. A clash between two styles is usually a translation problem, not a character flaw.

    For example: An autistic employee answers “Is this report ready?” with “No, I need two more days.” Her manager hears blunt. She was simply being clear and honest. Once they talk about it, he finds her clarity a relief.

    Words you could use: “I’m quite direct, so if I sound blunt, I’m not cross.” “It helps me if you say exactly what you need.”

    Try this: Write one sentence about how you like to be spoken to, and share it with one person this week.

    Dig deeper: styles, cultures and minds

  9. Small talk has a job, and it can be short.

    Small talk (“Cold today, isn’t it?” “Busy weekend?”) isn’t really about the weather. Its job is to say “I see you, I’m friendly, we’re okay.” The anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski called this kind of talk “phatic communion” in 1923: talk whose main purpose is bonding, not information. Small moments like this matter more than people expect. Sandstrom and Dunn (2014) found that people who had a short, friendly exchange with a coffee-shop barista felt more belonging and a better mood than people who kept it purely efficient. But small talk is hard work for many people, including many autistic people, shy or anxious people and people speaking a second language. The rules are unwritten, it can feel pointless, and it uses energy. A few ready-made lines, sometimes called social scripts, take the guesswork out. Keeping it short is allowed.

    For example: A dad who dreads the school gate keeps three lines ready: “Morning! How’s your week going?” then “Oh, nice,” then “Right, I’d better get going. See you Friday.” He no longer dreads it, and the other parents think he’s lovely.

    Words you could use: To open: “Morning! How’s your week been?” “How do you know Sam?” To keep it going: “Oh, how was that?” To close: “Lovely to see you. I’d better get on.” “I’ll let you get on. Have a good day.” To opt out kindly: “I’m not great at chit-chat, but it’s really nice to see you.”

    Try this: Write your own three lines: one to open, one follow-up question, one to close. Use them once this week. If you’d rather skip ahead to a real topic, that’s fine too: “Can I ask you about something I’m actually curious about?”

    Dig deeper: small talk and social scripts

Thursday, 9:02am

Ana’s brother rings the moment he’s off the bus. “Who’s ill?” Ana laughs. “Nobody! I just wanted to ask if you’re free on Saturday.” He laughs too, a bit shakily. Next time, Ana texts: “No rush and nothing’s wrong. Quick question about Saturday, call when you’re free.” Six more words. They save him a whole bus ride of worry.

In everyday life

On your own: how you talk to yourself

Communication isn’t only between people. You also send yourself messages all day: “Typical, I always mess this up,” or “That was hard, and I handled it.” The same ideas apply. Notice the tone, check whether the message is fair, and repair it when it’s harsh.

Thoughts you could try: “Is that what I actually mean, or just how it came out?” “What would I say to a friend who did this?”

Try this: Catch one harsh sentence you say to yourself today, and rewrite it in the way you’d speak to someone you like.

With other people

The same few ideas show up everywhere people talk:

  • Partners: “We never go out anymore” sounds like a complaint about evenings. Underneath, it may be “I miss you.” Answering the second layer (“I miss you too, let’s plan something”) goes further than defending the calendar.
  • Parent and young child: “Stop that!” tells a child what not to do, but not what to do instead. “Feet on the floor, please” is easier to follow, and easier to hear.
  • Coworkers: In a meeting, silence is taken as agreement. Later, a quieter colleague says she never agreed. A round of “Anyone see it differently?” catches this.
  • Housemates: A note on the fridge saying “PLEASE clean up” reads as shouting, even if it was written in a hurry. A face-to-face chat, or a kinder note, works better.
  • Grown-up child and parent: “Are you eating properly?” is care in one generation’s language and checking-up in another’s. Both people can say what they mean and what they hear.
  • Strangers and services: On a support call, saying “I’m frustrated, but not with you” often changes how the whole call goes.

Common mix-ups

  • “If they loved me, they’d just know.” Closeness makes us check less, not understand more. Saying it clearly is a kindness, not a failure.
  • “I said it clearly, so it’s their fault they got it wrong.” Meaning is built by both people. You can be clear and still be misheard.
  • “Listening means agreeing.” You can fully understand someone and still see it differently. Understanding comes first; your view can come after.
  • “Being direct is rude.” Directness is a style. It can be warm and direct at the same time.
  • “Repairing means admitting I’m the bad one.” A repair just says “that didn’t go how I wanted.” Either person can make one.
  • “Small talk is fake.” It’s a way of saying “we’re okay with each other.” You’re allowed to keep it short, or to skip to a real topic.
  • “Texts are safer for hard things.” They feel safer to send, but they are easier to misread. For tender things, add warmth, or talk.

If you want to help someone else with this

Maybe a friend keeps having the same argument with their partner, or your teenager feels nobody listens. You can’t communicate for them, but you can help.

  • Listen first. The most useful thing is often to be the person who really hears them, without fixing.
  • Help them separate the two layers. “What was it about? And what did it seem to say about you two?”
  • Offer the simple shape. What happened, how they feel, what they’d like. Let them find their own words.
  • Don’t take sides on the other person’s character. You only hear one side, and they may make up next week.
  • Notice if it’s more than a mix-up. If they seem afraid of the other person, gently share Not safe at home?

Words you could use: “Do you want me to just listen, or help you think about what to say?”

When to get more help

Most mix-ups can be eased with the ideas here. Some situations need more.

  • When talking makes things worse every time, a couples or family counselor can slow conversations down and help both people be heard.
  • When you’re afraid of how someone will react, that isn’t a communication problem. Clearer words are not the fix, and you don’t have to keep trying. Not safe at home? lists free, private help, and signs to look out for may help you name what’s happening.
  • When someone is always “misunderstanding” you in ways that make you doubt your memory or your sense of what happened, talk with someone you trust outside the relationship.
  • When speaking or understanding is hard for you every day, because of anxiety, a hearing or language difference, or how your mind works, a doctor, speech and language therapist or counselor may help. Needing support is not a failing.
  • If you’re overwhelmed right now, start with Upset right now.

Part Three · Chapter 5

Attachment, trust and belonging

Why we need each other, how the bonds we had early on shape what we expect today, and how trust and belonging are built, lost and rebuilt, one small moment at a time.

In short:

  • Belonging is a basic need. Feeling lonely or left out is a signal, like hunger, not a flaw.
  • Early bonds set expectations, not a sentence. What we learned about being cared for can soften and change.
  • Trust grows in small moments. Doing what you said, again and again, matters more than big gestures.
  • You can start small. One friendly exchange, one kept promise, one kind word to yourself.

Sunday, 9:30pm

Kai sent a long message to a friend at lunchtime. It’s been read, but there’s no reply. By evening, Kai has gone from “she’s busy” to “she’s annoyed with me” to “nobody really likes me.” The friend, meanwhile, is at her nephew’s birthday, phone in a coat pocket. One silence, two very different nights.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out, it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That isn’t weakness. It’s a signal, like hunger, telling us to reach for connection.

    For example: Rhea moves to a new city for work. She’s busy and doing well, but by the third month she feels flat and teary on Sundays. Nothing is wrong with her. She’s lonely, and loneliness is a real need asking to be met.

    Thoughts you could try: “Feeling lonely doesn’t mean I’m unlikeable. It means I need connection, like anyone.”

    Try this: This week, reach out to one person, even with a short “thinking of you” message.

    Dig deeper: why we need each other

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children, we learn from the people who cared for us whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder or to stop asking. These lessons are a starting point, not a life sentence. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    For example: A toddler at the park runs off to explore, then runs back to check his grandmother is still on the bench, then runs off again. She is his safe base. Knowing she’s there makes him brave enough to explore.

    Words you could use: (to a child) “I’m right here. Go and look, and come back whenever you want.”

    Try this: Think of one person who was a safe base for you, at any age. What did they do that helped?

    Dig deeper: what attachment is

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close, and need more space. Many of us feel mostly secure, and most of us mix all three. These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they are sliding scales, not boxes.

    For example: When things feel tense, Dev wants to talk it through right now. His wife Lou needs to go quiet first. He reads her quiet as “she’s leaving.” She reads his questions as pressure. Once they see the pattern, they agree: Lou says “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and Dev waits.

    Words you could use: “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet. Let’s talk at eight.”

    Try this: Notice what you do when you feel unsure of someone: reach out more, or pull back? No judgment, just notice.

    Dig deeper: anxious, avoidant and secure

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust doesn’t usually come from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again: doing what you said, turning towards someone when they reach out, being the same person on a hard day as on a good one.

    For example: A new team member says she’ll send the figures by Thursday, and she does. The next week, she flags early that something will be late. By the third month, her manager trusts her completely, not because of one big moment, but dozens of small ones.

    Words you could use: “I said I’d do it by Friday. I’m running late, so here’s where it’s at.”

    Try this: Keep one small promise today, and notice how it feels to be someone who does what they said.

    Dig deeper: how trust grows

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle our nervous system. In one study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. This works both ways: we can be that safe presence for others too, often without saying much.

    For example: After a panic attack, Tom’s friend doesn’t try to fix anything. He sits next to him on the step and says, “Take your time. I’m here.” Tom’s breathing slows.

    Words you could use: “You don’t have to talk. I’ll just sit with you.” “What would help right now: company, quiet, or both?”

    Try this: Notice who helps you feel calmer. Tell them, simply: “I always feel better after seeing you.”

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Groups like teams, families, clubs and communities can offer both. Being left out of a group hurts, even when the group doesn’t matter much to us. Real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    For example: At a new job, Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny and hides that she doesn’t drink. She’s fitting in, and she’s exhausted. At a book club, she says what she really thinks and people ask her to say more. That’s belonging.

    Words you could use: (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it OK if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    Try this: Think of one place where you can be fully yourself. How could you spend a little more time there?

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It returns slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    For example: Two adult sisters fall out after one shares a private story at a family dinner. The apology is a start. What rebuilds trust is the months afterwards, when the story never comes up again and the sister checks before sharing anything.

    Words you could use: “I know sorry isn’t enough. I’ll show you, and you can take whatever time you need.”

    Try this: If you’ve broken someone’s trust, name one specific thing you’ll do differently, and do it.

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can feel out of reach. But belonging starts small: a chat with a neighbour, a regular class, an online group about something you love, a kind word to yourself. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    For example: After his divorce, Martin starts saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall. A few weeks later they talk football every morning. It isn’t a deep friendship, but it makes Mondays easier, and it reminds him he can still connect.

    Words you could use: “I’m new around here. Is there anything you’d recommend?” “I loved what you said in the group. Would you like to get a coffee sometime?”

    Try this: Have one small, friendly exchange with someone you don’t know well this week.

    Dig deeper: building connection

Monday, 8:10am

Kai’s phone buzzes. “Sorry! Phone was in my coat all day at my nephew’s party. Loved your message. Coffee Thursday?” Kai notices how far last night’s story had run: from “she’s busy” all the way to “nobody likes me.” Next time a reply is slow, Kai decides to send a light “No rush, just thinking of you,” and let the silence be just a silence.

In everyday life

  • Partners: One partner texts a lot when apart; the other forgets. Agreeing on one goodnight message keeps both feeling connected.
  • Parents and children: A child who acts up at pick-up may simply be releasing a long day with the safest person they know.
  • Friends: A friend who always cancels may be overwhelmed, not uncaring. Asking “Is it easier if we do something short?” keeps the door open.
  • Coworkers: A team where mistakes can be admitted without blame builds trust faster than one where everyone hides them.
  • Housemates: A weekly shared meal, even a simple one, can turn people who share a kitchen into people who look out for each other.
  • Community: A neighbour who waves every morning is a small, real part of feeling at home.

On your own: belonging with yourself

Belonging isn’t only about other people. It also includes how you treat yourself. If you’re kind to yourself on a bad day, you carry a little safe base wherever you go. People who learn to comfort themselves often find it easier to let others in, too, because they’re less afraid of being let down.

Thoughts you could try: “I’m allowed to need people.” “Today was lonely. That’s hard, and it won’t always be like this.”

Common mix-ups

  • “Needing people means I’m needy.” Needing connection is human. Everyone has this need, even those who hide it well.
  • “My attachment style is fixed for life.” Patterns can change, especially through steady, kind relationships and reflection.
  • “An ‘anxious’ or ‘avoidant’ label explains everything.” Labels can help you notice patterns, but people are more than a type. Use them for curiosity, not judgment.
  • “Jealousy and checking up show how much they love me.” Reaching for reassurance is human. Controlling who someone sees, or checking their phone, is not love and is not an attachment style. See signs to look out for.
  • “If I forgive, I have to trust again straight away.” Forgiving and trusting are different. Trust can return slowly, or not at all, and that’s your call.
  • “Belonging means everyone likes me.” Belonging usually means a few people who know you as you are. That’s enough.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be a steady presence. Reliability matters more than big gestures. Turn up when you said you would.
  • Take loneliness seriously. If someone says they feel alone, don’t rush to “But you have so many friends!” Listen first.
  • Invite, and keep inviting. People who feel left out may say no a few times. A warm, no-pressure invitation still matters.
  • Make room in groups. Notice who is on the edge, and ask their view.
  • Don’t diagnose. Avoid telling someone they’re “so avoidant” or “so clingy.” Ask what would help instead.

Words you could use: “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” “We’re having a few people over on Friday. No pressure, but I’d love you to come.”

Habits and change: why change is hard and how small steps work

Why it’s so hard to change, even when we really want to, and what actually helps: small steps, a kinder setup, patience with slips, and remembering that other people’s change belongs to them.

In short:

  • Change is hard for good reasons. Habits save effort, so the old way is always the easy way.
  • Small and set up beats big and willpower. Make the first step tiny and change the setup around you.
  • Slips are part of change. A missed day is information about the plan, not proof about you.
  • Other people’s change is theirs. You can support it. You can’t force it.

Monday, 7:05am

The plan was a walk before work. The trainers are by the door. But the phone is in your hand, and somehow it’s 7:40 and you’re scrolling with cold coffee. By evening, a quiet voice says: “See? You never stick to anything.” That voice is wrong. You aren’t weak. You’ve met a habit.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    Much of what we do each day runs on autopilot. That saves energy, which is why habits exist. The downside is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one. Change asks your brain to do something effortful, again and again, until the new way becomes the easy one.

    For example: Grace wants to stop snapping at her kids in the morning rush. She means it every night. Every morning, the same rush brings the same snap. She isn’t a bad parent; the morning is a well-worn groove.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is hard because it’s a habit, not because I’m failing.” “Old grooves take time to fill in.”

    Try this: Pick one thing you’d like to change and write: “When does it usually happen? Where? What’s going on just before?”

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger: a time, a place, a feeling, a person, or the thing you just did. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward (relief, comfort, a little buzz) keeps it going. If you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    For example: A man notices he reaches for snacks not when he’s hungry but when he opens his laptop after dinner. The cue is the laptop. He starts making tea first instead.

    Thoughts you could try: “What happened right before I did that?” “What was I getting from it?”

    Try this: For three days, each time the habit happens, jot down the cue: time, place, feeling, or what you were doing.

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring but easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and that’s why they work: they’re easy enough to do on a bad day. Tie the new step to something you already do: “After I put the kettle on, I’ll...” Once it sticks, you can grow it.

    For example: Two housemates want a tidier kitchen. Instead of a big weekly clean they never do, they agree: “After dinner, we each wipe one surface.” It takes two minutes, and the kitchen stays nicer.

    Words you could use: “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I ..., I will ...”

    Try this: Shrink your change until it takes two minutes or less. Write it as “After I ___, I will ___.”

    Dig deeper: why small steps work

  4. Change the setup, not just your willpower.

    Willpower runs low when we’re tired, hungry or stressed. Your surroundings don’t. Make the new habit easier (put it in sight, get things ready the night before) and the old one a little harder (move it out of reach, add a step). Small changes to your setup can do more than any amount of trying harder.

    For example: A teenager wants to sleep better. Instead of “trying not to scroll,” he charges his phone in the kitchen. It works far better than willpower ever did.

    Words you could use: (to yourself or a partner) “What could we change about the setup so this gets easier?”

    Try this: Make one small change to your space today that makes the new habit easier or the old one harder.

    Dig deeper: shaping your setup

  5. Being in two minds is normal.

    Part of you wants to change. Part of you doesn’t, and that part usually has its reasons: the old habit gives comfort, saves effort, or protects you from something. Pushing harder against that part rarely works. Listening to it often does.

    For example: Asif wants to drink less, but he also loves the Friday pub with his team. Instead of “never again,” he asks what Friday gives him: friends, laughing, unwinding. He keeps the Friday, and switches to alcohol-free after the first drink.

    Thoughts you could try: “What does the old habit give me?” “What would make the change worth it to me, not just to others?”

    Try this: Write two lists: “What I like about how things are” and “What I’d like about changing.” Both lists are allowed.

    Dig deeper: readiness and mixed feelings

  6. Slips are part of change.

    Nearly everyone who changes something slips back sometimes. One slip doesn’t undo your progress. What often does is the thought that follows: “I’ve blown it, so why bother?” Treating yourself kindly after a slip makes it more likely you’ll start again, not less.

    For example: After three calm weeks, a dad shouts at his son over homework. That night he says, “I shouted, and I’m sorry. I’m working on it.” The next day he’s back on track. The slip became a repair, not a reason to give up.

    Thoughts you could try: “A slip is a moment, not a verdict.” “What made today harder, and what can I plan for next time?”

    Try this: Decide now what you’ll do after a slip: one kind sentence to yourself, and one small step to start again.

    Dig deeper: slips and starting again

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

    Change done only to please others, or out of shame, is hard to keep. Change that connects to what you care about, the kind of person, parent, friend or colleague you want to be, has deeper roots. Each small action is a vote for that person.

    For example: A woman who wants to “be better at keeping in touch” reframes it: “I’m someone who shows up for my friends.” Sending one voice note a week becomes part of who she is, not a chore.

    Thoughts you could try: “Who am I becoming with this step?” “This matters to me because...”

    Try this: Finish this sentence for your change: “I want to do this because I care about...”

    Dig deeper: values and who you’re becoming

  8. You can support change, but not force it.

    It’s natural to want people we love to change: to drink less, tidy more, look after themselves. But other people’s change belongs to them. Pressure tends to make people dig in. What helps more: saying clearly what you need, asking what would help, and noticing their small steps out loud.

    For example: A coworker keeps missing deadlines. Instead of “You need to be more organized,” his teammate asks, “What would make the handover easier for you?” They set up a shared checklist, and the deadlines improve.

    Words you could use: “I can’t change this for you, but I’d love to help if you want me to.” “I noticed you did that this week. That really helped.”

    Try this: Notice one small positive change someone else made this week, and tell them.

    Dig deeper: change with other people

Two weeks later, 7:05am

The trainers are still by the door, but the phone now charges in the kitchen overnight. The plan has shrunk too: shoes on and out to the end of the road. Some mornings that’s all. Most mornings it turns into a walk. On the day you miss, the voice says “See?” and you answer it: “A missed day is a slip, not a verdict. Tomorrow, the end of the road.”

In everyday life

  • Partners: One partner wants to spend less time on phones in the evening. They agree on a basket for both phones after nine, not a rule for one person.
  • Parents and children: A picture chart of the bedtime steps makes a new routine easier for a young child than reminders ever did.
  • Friends: Two friends who want to move more meet for a Saturday walk. The friendship is the reward that keeps the habit going.
  • Coworkers: A team that keeps forgetting actions starts ending every meeting with “Who does what by when?” It’s a tiny habit with a big effect.
  • Housemates: A shared list on the fridge works better than anyone trying to remember.
  • Big life changes: A move, a new baby or a new job breaks old routines. That’s disruptive, and it’s also a good moment to start new habits.

On your own: a kind plan for change

If you’re working on something alone, put the ideas together in one small plan:

  • One change. Not five. Pick the one that would make the most difference, or the one that feels most doable.
  • One cue. “After I ___, I will ___.”
  • One setup tweak. Make it easier to start.
  • One tired-day version. The smallest version you can still do on a bad day.
  • One kind sentence for slips. Written down, ready to use.

Common mix-ups

  • “If I really wanted it, I’d just do it.” Wanting is only the start. Habits, setup, tiredness and stress all matter.
  • “It takes 21 days to form a habit.” There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range.
  • “One slip means I’ve failed.” Slips are normal. Starting again is the skill.
  • “Being hard on myself will keep me on track.” Harshness tends to make people give up. Kindness makes it easier to try again.
  • “If I nag enough, they’ll change.” Pressure often makes people dig in. Ask, support and notice instead.
  • “Routines mean being rigid.” For many people, especially autistic people, routines are what make life manageable. Change can respect that.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask before advising. “Would ideas help, or would you rather just talk it through?”
  • Help them find their own reasons. “What would be different for you if this changed?”
  • Help with the setup. Offer practical things: a lift to the gym, a shared calendar, a quieter evening.
  • Notice small steps out loud. Encouragement for small wins often helps more than pointing out what’s still missing.
  • Be kind about slips. “That’s OK, tomorrow’s a new go” helps more than “I knew it.”
  • Look after yourself. You can care deeply and still set limits on what you’ll put up with.

Words you could use: “I’m on your side with this. What would help?” “You’ve done this three days running. That’s not nothing.”

When to get more help

  • If a habit is harming your health or life, such as drinking, drugs, gambling, eating or self-harm, please talk to a doctor or a specialist service. Needing help with this is common and nothing to be ashamed of.
  • If you can’t stop even though you’ve tried many times, professional support can make a real difference. It isn’t a sign of weakness.
  • If change feels impossible because you’re exhausted or low most days, that may be burnout or depression. A doctor or counselor can help.
  • If someone is demanding you change in ways that frighten you, or punishing you when you don’t, that isn’t support. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
  • If you’re overwhelmed right now, start with Upset right now.

Self-worth, shame and self-compassion

Why your worth isn’t a score you keep earning, how shame is different from guilt, and how being kind to yourself makes it easier, not harder, to grow.

In short:

  • Your worth isn’t a score. It doesn’t rise and fall with each day’s results.
  • Guilt and shame are different. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.” Guilt is easier to put right.
  • Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off. Being kind to yourself after a mistake makes it easier to own it and grow.
  • Worth is something we can give. How we treat each other teaches people what they are worth.

Thursday, 11:20pm

You sent the wrong file to the whole team. Someone noticed, fixed it, and said “no worries.” Hours later you are still awake, replaying it. The voice in your head isn’t saying “that was a mistake.” It’s saying “you’re useless. They all know it now.”

This chapter is for anyone: on your own, at home, with friends, at work or in your community. It teaches how self-worth and shame work first, then what you can do. Nothing here is a diagnosis. Take what helps and leave the rest.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    Many of us carry a quiet scoreboard: I’m worth something when I’m useful, liked, slim, busy, top of the class or never a bother. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth.” The trouble is that any score can drop. If your worth rises and falls with each day’s results, every bad day feels like a verdict on you.

    For example: A retired teacher feels lost since she stopped working. She’s fine. But for forty years, “I’m needed” was how she knew she mattered.

    Thoughts you could try: “Today went badly. That’s about today, not about my worth.” “I matter on days I achieve nothing.”

    Try this: Finish the line “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times. Those are your scores. Just notice them.

    Dig deeper: worth that doesn’t have to be earned

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points toward fixing it: saying sorry, making it right. Shame is about who you are, and that feels impossible to fix. So shame tends to make people hide, freeze, blame someone else or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is more likely to lead to repair.

    For example: A teenager breaks his mum’s favorite mug. Guilt says, “I should tell her and help pay for a new one.” Shame says, “I ruin everything,” and he hides the pieces in the bin.

    Thoughts you could try: “What exactly did I do? What would make it right?” “I made a mistake. I am not a mistake.”

    Try this: Next time you feel bad about something, ask: is this about what I did, or who I am? If it’s who you are, turn it into one thing you did.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    Shame shows up in the body first: a hot face, eyes down, a sinking stomach, wanting the floor to swallow you. It can also come out sideways, as sudden anger or a cold, closed face. That’s why a small comment can start a big fight: it touched shame, and shame defends.

    For example: A coworker says in a meeting, “Didn’t we already cover this?” His colleague snaps back. Later she realizes the snap wasn’t about the question. She felt stupid in front of everyone.

    Words you could use: “I think I felt embarrassed just then, and it came out sharp. Can I try again?”

    Try this: Learn your own shame signs. Next time you suddenly want to disappear or snap, name it quietly: “That’s shame.”

    Dig deeper: how shame works in the body

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh voice usually started as protection. If you criticize yourself first, maybe nobody else can hurt you. If you’re hard on yourself, maybe you’ll never fail again. It meant well. But a voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, and it is tiring to live with.

    For example: A young dad tells himself “idiot” every time he forgets something for the baby. His own father used that word. The voice is old, borrowed and not his.

    Thoughts you could try: “Thanks for trying to protect me. I can take it from here.” “Whose voice is that, really?”

    Try this: Write down one thing your inner critic says often. Then ask: would I say this to a friend I love? What would I say instead?

    Dig deeper: the critic and the soothing system

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you would treat a good friend who is struggling. One well-known model has three parts: kindness instead of attack, remembering that everyone struggles, and noticing the pain without drowning in it. Studies, such as one by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012), suggest people who are kind to themselves after a failure are often more likely to try to improve, not less.

    For example: A woman fails her driving test for the third time. Instead of “I’m hopeless,” she tries: “This is hard. Lots of people need more goes. What will I practice before the next one?” She books another lesson.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is a hard moment.” “Other people feel this too.” “What do I need right now?”

    Try this: Put a hand on your chest or arm, if that feels okay, and say those three lines slowly to yourself once today.

    Dig deeper: what self-compassion is, and isn’t

  6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

    We all measure ourselves against others. It’s how humans work out where they stand. But feeds full of other people’s best moments make that measuring unfair. Self-esteem that depends on being better than others goes up and down a lot. Self-compassion doesn’t need you to win.

    For example: Two old friends meet up. One has a new house, the other is renting a room. The renter spends the evening quietly ranking herself, and misses that her friend is lonely and wanted to talk.

    Thoughts you could try: “I’m comparing my inside to their outside.” “Their good news doesn’t take anything from me.”

    Try this: Notice one comparison today. Then swap it for a question about the other person: “How are you, really?”

    Dig deeper: comparison and steady self-worth

  7. You can own a mistake without drowning in it.

    Owning a mistake is easier when it doesn’t mean you’re worthless. A simple shape helps: say what you did, say the effect, say what you’ll do now. Then stop. You don’t need to beat yourself up as proof that you’re sorry.

    For example: A housemate forgot to pay her share of the bill, and the internet was cut off. “I forgot to pay. That left you without internet all weekend. I’ve paid it and set a reminder.” Done, without a speech about being a terrible person.

    Words you could use: “I did this. I can see it affected you like this. Here’s what I’m doing about it.”

    Try this: Think of one small thing you still feel bad about. Write the three-part sentence. Decide whether to send it, say it or simply let it rest.

    Dig deeper: owning it, repairing it, letting it rest

  8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they are treated, and adults keep learning it. How we talk to each other matters: “you did a careless thing” leaves room to change; “you’re so careless” doesn’t. Praise for effort and for who someone is, not only for results, helps people feel they matter on bad days too.

    For example: A team lead starts each project review with “what did we learn?” instead of “who got it wrong?” Over time, people own up to problems earlier.

    Words you could use: “I’m glad you’re here, whatever happened today.” “That didn’t work. You’re still welcome here.”

    Try this: Say one thing to someone today that isn’t about their results: “I like how you…” or “I’m glad I know you.”

    Dig deeper: how worth is given and learned

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control how other people judge you, or what you were taught about your worth growing up. You can control how you speak to yourself now, whether you repair what you can, and where you spend your time. Give your energy to people who treat you as someone who matters.   • Parents and children: A child who is told “you’re so naughty” hears who they are. “That was a naughty thing to do” leaves them a way back.
  • Friends: One friend always says yes to everything, then quietly burns out, because saying no feels like risking being dropped.
  • Work: Someone hides a small error for weeks because owning it feels like proving they don’t belong there.
  • Community: A newcomer at a club makes one mistake, feels everyone saw, and never comes back.

On your own: a kinder voice to keep

You don’t need anyone else to start this. You only need to notice how you talk to yourself, and to practice one gentler sentence.

  • Catch the critic’s favorite words: “always,” “useless,” “pathetic,” “should.”
  • Swap one of them for a fact: “I was late twice this week” instead of “I’m hopeless.”
  • Keep a short list of things you did well, or simply did, on hard days. Getting up counts.

Thoughts you could try: “I’m allowed to be a work in progress.” “I can be disappointed in what I did and still be on my own side.”

Common mix-ups

  • “Self-compassion is self-pity.” Self-pity says “only me, poor me.” Self-compassion says “this is hard, and other people go through it too.” It connects you to others.
  • “If I’m kind to myself, I’ll get lazy.” Research suggests the opposite is more common: fear of your own attack makes people avoid trying and hide mistakes.
  • “Self-worth means thinking I’m great.” It doesn’t. It means you matter even on the days you’re not great.
  • “Feeling shame means I’m a bad person.” Shame is a feeling, not a fact. Almost everyone feels it.
  • “Owning a mistake means agreeing I’m worthless.” The opposite. You can only really own a mistake when it doesn’t threaten your whole self.
  • “I should just feel confident.” Confidence comes and goes. Kindness toward yourself is something you can practice on the days it’s gone.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Separate the person from the act. Talk about what happened, never about what kind of person they are.
  • Don’t argue with their shame. “You’re not useless!” can feel like being told they’re wrong to feel it. Try: “It sounds like you feel really low about it. I don’t see you that way.”
  • Make repair easy. Help them find one small thing they can do, so the feeling has somewhere to go.
  • Notice them on ordinary days. Kindness when nothing has gone wrong teaches worth best.

Words you could use: “That sounds like it’s weighing on you. Want to work out one thing that would help?” “You don’t have to earn being cared about.”

When to get more help

Everyone feels shame and self-doubt sometimes. It’s worth talking to a doctor, counselor or therapist if harsh thoughts about yourself are there most days, if they stop you working, eating, sleeping or seeing people, or if you feel you deserve to be hurt. Therapies such as compassion-focused therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy are built to help with exactly this.

If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US, call or text 988, any time. Outside the US, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

If someone close to you keeps telling you you’re worthless, stupid or lucky to have them, that isn’t a self-esteem problem. It can be a way of controlling someone. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? have free, private help.

Loss, grief and big life changes

What grief is, why it comes in waves, how it shows up in the body, and how to carry a loss, or a big change, without having to “get over it.”

In short:

  • Grief is love with nowhere to go. It hurts because the person, place or life mattered.
  • It comes in waves, not stages. A good week and then a bad day is normal, not going backwards.
  • You don’t have to let go. Many people keep a bond with the person they lost, and that’s healthy.
  • Most people find a way through. Support helps, and some losses need more help. Both are normal.

Saturday, 9:05am

Six months on, you’re doing fine. Then, in the supermarket, you see the cereal your dad always bought, and you have to leave your cart in the aisle and sit in the car. Nobody around you knows. It feels like day one again.

This chapter is for anyone who has lost someone or something: a person, a pet, a marriage, a friendship, health, a home, a job, a country or a future you had pictured. It explains how grief works first, then what can help. There is no right way to grieve, and nothing here is a timetable.

  1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone. It isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted, or children leaving home can all bring real grief. The size of the grief usually matches the size of the bond, not what other people think the loss “should” mean.

    For example: A man is surprised how hard he takes his dog’s death. His friends say “it was just a dog.” But that dog walked with him every morning for twelve years.

    Thoughts you could try: “This hurts because it mattered.” “My grief doesn’t need anyone’s permission.”

    Try this: Name one loss you’ve been carrying that you haven’t called grief. Say it to yourself: “I’m grieving…”

    Dig deeper: what grief is, and what counts

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. They came from listening to dying patients, and were never meant as steps everyone follows in order. Most people swing back and forth: some hours deep in the loss, some hours getting on with life. That back-and-forth is healthy. Laughing at a funeral, or having a good day, doesn’t mean you’ve stopped caring.

    For example: A widow feels guilty that she enjoyed lunch with her sister. Then she cries all evening. Both were grief doing its work.

    Thoughts you could try: “A good hour isn’t a betrayal.” “This is a wave. Waves pass.”

    Try this: Let yourself have one small break from grief today, without guilt: a walk, a show, a meal with a friend.

    Dig deeper: waves, and the swing between loss and life

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, poor sleep, no appetite or extra appetite, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there isn’t. That takes time, and it uses energy.

    For example: A teacher whose mother died keeps losing her keys and forgetting meetings. She worries something is wrong with her memory. It’s grief fog, and it eases as the months pass.

    Words you could use: “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

    Try this: Pick three basics to protect this week: one regular meal, a set bedtime, and a short walk outside.

    Dig deeper: the grieving body and brain

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain. Someone is still alive but changed, through dementia, addiction or illness. Someone is gone but not dead, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Some losses aren’t recognized by others: an ex-partner’s death, a pregnancy loss, a friend you met online. Grief without a ceremony, or without other people noticing, can feel very lonely.

    For example: A woman’s father has dementia. He’s still here, and she still visits, but the dad who knew her is fading. She’s grieving someone who hasn’t died.

    Words you could use: “I’m grieving something people might not see as a loss. It is one for me.”

    Try this: If a loss of yours had no ceremony, make a small one: light a candle, write a letter, plant something, visit a place.

    Dig deeper: ambiguous and unrecognized grief

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying. Others grieve by doing: fixing, organizing, working, building something. Neither is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks the other “isn’t grieving” when they are just grieving differently. Culture, faith, age and how your mind works all shape it, too.

    For example: After their son’s death, one parent wants to talk about him every evening. The other spends weekends restoring his old bike. Each feels the other is getting it wrong. Both are grieving him.

    Words you could use: “I think we grieve differently. Can you tell me what helps you, and I’ll tell you what helps me?”

    Try this: Ask someone who shares your loss: “What has helped you most, even a little?” Listen without correcting.

    Dig deeper: grieving styles and families

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said healthy grief meant “moving on” and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently: people often keep a bond with who they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head, and values they carry forward. The relationship changes shape. It doesn’t have to end.

    For example: A teenager wears her grandfather’s watch to exams. She doesn’t believe in luck. It just feels like he’s with her.

    Thoughts you could try: “What would they say about this?” “I carry this part of them with me.”

    Try this: Choose one way to keep a bond: cook their recipe, tell a story about them, or keep one of their habits.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retirement, moving country, a child starting school, coming out, or getting well after a long illness can all bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts: an ending, a confusing in-between time, and a new beginning. The middle part is the hardest, and the easiest to rush.

    For example: A couple moves to a new city for a great job. Six months in, one of them feels low and guilty: “We wanted this.” They also lost friends, a favorite café and a whole way of life.

    Thoughts you could try: “I can be glad about this and still miss what came before.” “I’m in the in-between. It’s supposed to feel unsettled.”

    Try this: For one change you’re going through, write: what ended, what feels unclear, and one small thing that’s new and good.

    Dig deeper: endings, the in-between, beginnings

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that many people, over time, find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, even though they never forget. Being with people who let you talk, or not talk, helps. For some, though, grief stays stuck at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard. That isn’t weakness, and there is specific help for it.

    For example: A year after his wife died, a man still can’t go into their bedroom or see friends. His doctor refers him to a grief counselor. Slowly, things begin to move.

    Words you could use: “It’s been a long time and it isn’t getting any easier. I think I need more help.”

    Try this: Write down two people you could text on a hard day, and one place you could call if those people aren’t enough.

    Dig deeper: resilience, support and stuck grief

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control the loss, how long grief lasts, or how other people react to it. You can control small things: eating something, resting, telling one person how you are, and saying no to what you can’t manage right now. That is enough for a hard season. In everyday life

  • Partners: After a miscarriage, one partner wants to talk and the other goes quiet. Each worries the other doesn’t care.
  • Parents and children: A child laughs and plays the day after a funeral, then cries at bedtime. Both are grief.
  • Friends: A friend stops calling after your diagnosis, not from coldness but because they don’t know what to say.
  • Work: A colleague returns after a death and everyone avoids the subject. She feels her loss has been erased.
  • Community: A neighborhood loses its library, or a town loses its factory, and many people grieve the same thing together.

On your own: carrying it gently

Grief can be lonely, even with people around. Some things help when you’re carrying it alone:

  • Give the day a small shape: one meal, one walk, one person you message.
  • Expect harder days around dates, and plan something gentle for them.
  • Write to who you lost, or about what you lost. Nobody else needs to read it.

Thoughts you could try: “I don’t have to do this fast.” “Today I only need to get to tonight.”

Common mix-ups

  • “There are five stages, in order.” Most people go back and forth. There is no correct order.
  • “After a year you should be over it.” Grief changes over time, but there’s no deadline. Anniversaries and birthdays often bring it back.
  • “If I’m not crying, I’m not grieving.” Some people grieve by doing, thinking or staying busy. That counts.
  • “Moving on means forgetting.” You can build a new life and keep a bond with who you lost.
  • “Good changes shouldn’t make me sad.” Every change ends something. Missing the old life is normal.
  • “I shouldn’t mention their name, it’ll upset them.” Many grieving people say hearing the name is a comfort. Ask.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Show up, and keep showing up. Support often floods in for two weeks and then stops. A text at three months, or on a birthday, can mean a great deal.
  • Offer something specific. “Can I drop off dinner on Thursday?” is easier to say yes to than “let me know if you need anything.”
  • Say the name. Share a memory. Let them talk, or not.
  • Don’t fix or explain. Skip “everything happens for a reason” and “at least…”. Try “I’m so sorry. I’m here.”
  • Comfort in, vent out. If you’re upset too, lean on people further from the loss, not on those closest to it.

Words you could use: “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.” “I was thinking about your mum today. I remember her laugh.” “I’m going to the shop, what can I bring you?”

When to get more help

Grief is not an illness. But it’s worth talking to a doctor or a grief counselor if, after many months, the pain isn’t easing at all, you can’t manage daily life, you feel life has no meaning without the person, or you are drinking or using other things to get through. Many hospices, faith communities and charities offer free bereavement support, including for children.

If you are having thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US, call or text 988, any time. Outside the US, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

If a loss involved violence, or you are grieving a relationship you left because you weren’t safe, Not safe at home? has free, private help, including after you separate.

Minds that work differently

How autistic, ADHD, sensory and learning differences work, why they are differences and not character flaws, and how people with different minds can meet in the middle.

In short:

  • Brains vary, like bodies do. Different isn’t broken, and differences can still be disabling in a world built for one kind of mind.
  • Overload isn’t a choice. Meltdowns, shutdowns and burnout are what happens when a nervous system has had too much.
  • Some things help a lot: less masking, room to stim, time for deep interests, and real rest.
  • Meet in the middle, out loud. Ask what helps, say what helps you, and use the words each person chooses.

Monday, 8:15am

“Can you just put your shoes on?” For the third time. Your child is lying on the hall floor, hands over his ears, because the new socks “feel wrong.” You’re late. He’s not being difficult. To him, the sock seam feels like sandpaper, and the alarm clock is still ringing in his head.

This chapter is for anyone: neurodivergent people, diagnosed or not, and the people who live, work and learn with them. It explains how these minds work first, then what helps. It can’t diagnose anyone, and only you get to choose the labels you use for yourself.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    People’s brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with others. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what is treated as typical. Different isn’t broken. And at the same time, differences can be disabling, especially when the world is built for one kind of mind.

    For example: A man who struggled all through school finds out at 40 that he is dyslexic. He’s relieved: he was never lazy or stupid. His brain just processes written words differently.

    Thoughts you could try: “Maybe this isn’t a flaw. Maybe it’s a difference that needs a different setup.”

    Try this: Think of one thing that’s hard for you that’s easy for others. Ask: is it effort, or is it a different kind of brain?

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally, have deep and focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely, and find predictability calming. Autism isn’t a line from “mild” to “severe.” Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change day to day. Misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    For example: An autistic coworker says, “Your slides have three typos.” She means it as help. Her colleague hears criticism. Neither is wrong; they’re using different rules for what kind means.

    Words you could use: “I like it when people say exactly what they mean. You can be direct with me.” “Do you want my honest view, or just support right now?”

    Try this: In one conversation today, say exactly what you mean, with no hints. Notice whether it makes things easier.

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD often have plenty of attention, but find it hard to point it where they choose. Boring tasks can feel impossible; interesting ones can swallow hours. Time can feel slippery (“now” and “not now”), and feelings can come fast and big. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself, not a lack of effort or care.

    For example: A woman with ADHD forgets her friend’s birthday, then spends six hours making her an amazing handmade card. The forgetting and the focus come from the same brain.

    Words you could use: “I care about this. My brain drops things. A reminder really helps, and it doesn’t annoy me.”

    Try this: Move one thing you keep forgetting out of your head and into the world: an alarm, a note on the door, or a basket by the exit.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise or scratchy clothes. Others need more input: movement, strong flavors, pressure. Overload isn’t being fussy. It’s a body at its limit.

    For example: Two housemates argue about the kitchen radio. One finds silence eerie; the other finds the radio painful. A rule helps: radio on mornings, headphones in the evening.

    Words you could use: “It’s not you. The noise in here is too much for me right now. Can we move somewhere quieter?”

    Try this: Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you, and one that calms you. Make one small change today.

    Dig deeper: senses, overload and needs

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, not a measure of how clever someone is. Many people have a “spiky” profile: very strong in some areas, struggling in others. The right tools, like audiobooks, spoken notes or extra time, can level the ground.

    For example: A teenager with dyspraxia is called clumsy at football. In drama, he writes and directs the school play. Same boy, different task.

    Words you could use: “I learn better when I can listen than when I read. Could you send a voice note instead?”

    Try this: Ask someone you live or work with: “What’s the easiest way for you to take in information?” Then use it.

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences: copying others, forcing eye contact, holding back movements, rehearsing small talk. This is called masking. It can help someone get through a day, but it uses a lot of energy, and over years it can lead to exhaustion, anxiety and burnout.

    For example: A student seems fine all day at college, then comes home and can’t speak for an hour. She’s not being rude. She’s recovering from a day of performing.

    Words you could use: “I need some quiet time after work. It’s how I recharge, not a sign I’m upset with you.”

    Try this: Notice one place where you can drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and often lasts months. Rest, fewer demands and less masking help. Pushing harder usually makes it worse.

    Words you could use: “I’m in burnout. I need fewer demands for a while, not a pep talk.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    Try this: If you think you might be in burnout, list three demands you could drop, delay or share for a month. If it goes on, or you feel hopeless, talk to a doctor.

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much: crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version: going still, losing speech, not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload. What helps is less: less noise, fewer words, fewer questions, more space and safety. Talking can wait.

    Words you could use: (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Try this: On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds, like rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing or fiddling with something. Most people stim a little (think of a jiggling foot). For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping.

    Words you could use: “Rocking helps me think. It doesn’t mean anything’s wrong.” “You don’t need to stop. It’s fine with me.”

    Try this: Notice what your own hands or body do when you’re stressed, focused or happy. That’s your stim.

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds, often autistic and ADHD ones, focus deeply on one thing at a time. That focus can be a gift: real expertise, flow and joy. It also makes switching tasks hard, so being interrupted can feel like being pulled out of water. Warnings before a change, and routines, help a lot.

    For example: A dad calls his daughter to dinner three times and gets more frustrated each time. Then he tries a ten-minute warning, and a five. She comes to the table calm.

    Words you could use: “Dinner is in ten minutes. I’ll come and get you at five to.” “Can you give me a warning before we switch?”

    Try this: Before you interrupt someone who’s focused, give a heads-up and a time. See if it goes more smoothly.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by and engaged with their special interests reported higher wellbeing, though very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing.

    Words you could use: “Can I tell you about this for five minutes? It’s how I unwind.” “Tell me more. What’s the best bit?”

    Try this: Ask someone about their favourite subject, and let them talk for five minutes without steering.

    Dig deeper: special interests and wellbeing

  12. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, explicit words help everyone: say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. Changes made for one person, like quiet spaces, written agendas or flexible hours, often help many others too.

    For example: A team starts sending meeting agendas a day early. It was for one autistic colleague. Soon everyone says meetings are shorter and better.

    Words you could use: “What would make this easier for you?” “Here’s what helps me. What helps you?”

    Try this: Swap one “how I work best” fact with someone today: one thing that helps you, and ask for one that helps them.

    Dig deeper: adjustments that help everyone

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, support and community. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is simple: ask, and use the words the person chooses. And never try to diagnose someone else, especially in an argument.

    For example: During a row, a partner says, “You’re so autistic.” It lands as an insult and ends the talk. Later, he apologizes and asks what his partner actually needs.

    Words you could use: “How do you like to describe yourself?” “I’m wondering if I might be neurodivergent. I’d like to find out more.”

    Try this: If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed, and decide one small next step: reading, talking to someone, or asking a doctor.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis, identity and words

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they are autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties, fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed how they understood their lives. Relief, grief and joy can all come together.

    Words you could use: “I’ve just found out I’m autistic. I’m still working out what it means.” “That explains so much about my life.”

    Try this: If you’ve found out later in life, look for one autistic-led group, book or online space, and give yourself time to make sense of it.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis later in life

Your part, and only your part. You can’t change how your brain, or anyone else’s, is wired. You can learn how yours works, say what helps, and ask others what helps them.   • Parents and children: Behaviour often has a sensory or overload reason. Ask “What’s too much right now?” before “Why are you doing this?”
  • With friends: A friend who cancels at the last minute may be out of energy, not out of care. Offer easy options, like a walk or a message.
  • At work: Written agendas, clear deadlines and quiet spaces help many people, not only neurodivergent ones. See Neurodiversity at work.

Common mix-ups

  • “Everyone’s a bit autistic.” Everyone has some traits in common, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life. The phrase can make real needs sound small.
  • “ADHD just means you can’t sit still.” Many people with ADHD, especially girls and women, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside.
  • “They seem fine to me.” Masking can hide a lot. Seeming fine isn’t the same as being fine.
  • “Adjustments are special treatment.” They remove barriers so people can do what’s expected. Glasses aren’t special treatment either.
  • “Autistic people lack empathy.” Many feel others’ feelings strongly. Differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Believe them about what’s hard, what hurts and what helps, even if it’s different for you.
  • Be clear. Say what you mean, give times, and write down anything important.
  • Ask, don’t assume. Every neurodivergent person is different. “What helps you?” beats any list.
  • Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places aren’t extras.
  • Enjoy their interests. Listening to someone talk about what they love is one of the warmest things you can do.

Words you could use: “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you wonder whether a difference is part of ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, for you or someone you care for, a doctor, school support staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so start early and write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or find a helpline at findahelpline.com.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? has free, private help.

Part Three · Chapter 10

Values, meaning and a good life: what philosophy offers everyday life

What matters to you, how to find it, what to do when two good things pull against each other, and what a few very old ideas can offer an ordinary, busy week.

In short:

  • Values are directions, not finish lines. You never “complete” kindness. You keep turning toward it.
  • Strong feelings point to what you value. Anger often guards fairness; hurt often guards closeness.
  • Meaning comes from ordinary places. People, care and everyday work give most of us most of our meaning.
  • Values live in small acts. One small thing this week, done because it matters, counts.

Sunday, 9pm

The week ahead is full, and none of it feels like yours. You’re not sad, exactly. You just can’t remember the last time you did something because it mattered to you, and not because it was next on the list.

This chapter is about values: the things you care about, the direction you want your life to face, and what makes a life feel good and worth living. It borrows from psychology, and from philosophy, which has been asking “how should I live?” for more than two thousand years. You don’t need to have read any of it. Everything here is in plain words.

  1. Values are directions, not finish lines.

    A goal is something you can tick off: run a 5k, paint the hall, pass the test. A value is a direction you keep walking in: looking after your health, making your home feel cared for, being a fair friend. You never “finish” kindness. That’s why values still work on the days when goals fall apart.

    For example (on your own): Priya planned to go to the gym three times this week and went once. She felt like a failure. Then she asked what the goal was for: “looking after my body, so I have energy for my kids.” She walked for twenty minutes at lunch. The goal slipped. The direction didn’t.

    Thoughts you could try: “What is this goal for? What do I care about underneath it?” “I can’t do the big thing today. What’s one small step in the same direction?”

    Try this: Pick one goal you have. Ask “what is this for?” three times in a row, and write down where you end up.

    Dig deeper: goals and values

  2. Your strong feelings point to what you value.

    You don’t have to invent your values from nothing. They show up in your strongest reactions. Anger often means something you care about has been stepped on. Envy can point to something you want for yourself. Sadness often shows what you love. A feeling is a signal, and a value is often what it’s pointing at.

    For example (a coworker): Sam is furious when a colleague takes credit for the team’s work. Under the anger is a value: fairness, and seeing people’s effort. Once Sam names it, he can talk about fairness in the next meeting, instead of just being angry at one person.

    Words you could use: “I think I’m upset because fairness really matters to me. It isn’t about wanting to win.”

    Try this: Think of the last time you felt strongly, good or bad. Finish this sentence: “That mattered to me because I care about…”

    Dig deeper: finding your values

  3. Two good things can pull in different directions.

    Most hard choices aren’t good against bad. They are good against good: honesty and kindness, freedom and safety, family and rest. Feeling torn doesn’t mean you’re confused or weak. It means you care about more than one thing. Even when you choose well, it’s normal to feel a little sad about the side you didn’t choose.

    For example (a parent and child): A mum cares about being honest and about protecting her eight-year-old. When the family dog is very ill, she doesn’t pick one. She tells the truth, gently, in words a child can hold: “Biscuit is very poorly. The vet is helping, and we’ll look after him together.”

    Thoughts you could try: “Which two good things are pulling at me here?” “Can I honor some of both, even if I can’t have all of either?”

    Try this: Name a choice you feel stuck on. Write the good thing on each side. Notice how it feels to see that you’re torn between two goods, not between right and wrong.

    Dig deeper: when values clash

  4. Put your effort where it can land.

    About two thousand years ago, a teacher called Epictetus, who had been born into slavery, taught that some things are up to us and some aren’t. Your own choices, effort and how you treat people are up to you. Other people’s choices, the weather and the past are not. Worry often goes to the second pile. Values live in the first.

    For example (a partner): Lena wants her partner to enjoy Christmas with her family. She can’t make him enjoy it. She can plan a shorter visit, include a walk he’ll like, and be warm herself. That’s her part, and it’s enough.

    Words you could use: “I can’t make this go well. I can choose how I show up.”

    Try this: Write one worry in two columns: the parts that are up to you, and the parts that aren’t. Pick one small thing from the first column and do it.

    Dig deeper: what is up to us

  5. A good life is more than a good mood.

    Feeling good matters. But many thinkers, from the Greek philosopher Aristotle to psychologists today, describe a second kind of wellbeing: growing, using your strengths, being a good friend, and doing things that matter even when they’re hard. Aristotle called it flourishing. He thought we build it the way we build any skill: by practice, a little at a time.

    For example (a friend): Jade spent a month helping a friend move out of a bad flat. It was tiring and not much fun. But looking back, she says it was one of the best months of her year. It wasn’t pleasant. It was worthwhile.

    Thoughts you could try: “Was today a good day, or a worthwhile day? Both count.”

    Try this: Tonight, write down one thing that felt good today and one thing that felt worthwhile. Notice whether they are the same thing.

    Dig deeper: happiness and flourishing

  6. Meaning comes from ordinary places.

    Researchers who study meaning often describe three parts. Your life makes some sense to you. You are heading somewhere. And your life matters to someone. Most meaning doesn’t come from big moments. It comes from people, from work done with care, and from small roles that others count on.

    For example (in the community): Walter is retired and lives alone since his wife died. His days went quiet. Now he reads to children at the library one morning a week. It gives him a reason to iron a shirt, and people who notice when he isn’t there.

    Thoughts you could try: “Who would notice if I didn’t show up? Who do I show up for?”

    Try this: List three roles you play, big or small: neighbor, sister, the one who waters the office plants. Next to each, write who it matters to.

    Dig deeper: where meaning comes from

  7. Different values aren’t wrong values.

    Two people can share a home, a family or a team and still put the same good things in a different order. One puts safety first; another puts adventure first. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that there are many real goods in life, and they can’t all be ranked on one scale. That leaves room to disagree without either person being bad.

    For example (housemates): Theo values a calm, tidy home. His housemate Mo values an open door and friends dropping by. For months it was “messy” against “uptight.” Once they named the values, it became a plan: open house on Fridays, quiet Sundays.

    Words you could use: “I think we care about different good things here. Can we each say what matters to us about it?”

    Try this: In your next disagreement, ask: “What matters to you about this?” Listen for the value, not just the position.

    Dig deeper: living with different values

  8. Values live in small, everyday acts.

    A good life is built mostly from small things done again and again: how you greet people, whether you keep small promises, how you talk about someone who isn’t in the room. The Chinese teacher Confucius taught that small, everyday courtesies shape who we become. Later thinkers who wrote about care said that being good starts with paying attention to the person in front of you.

    For example (a stranger): At the supermarket, an older man is struggling with the card machine. You wait without sighing, and say “No rush.” It costs forty seconds. It might be the kindest moment of his day.

    Thoughts you could try: “What would the person I want to be do in the next five minutes?” “When I drift from my values, I don’t need to punish myself. I just turn back.”

    Try this: Pick one value. Choose one tiny act that shows it, and do it each day this week.

    Dig deeper: everyday ethics

Your part, and only your part. You can choose your direction, notice when you drift, and turn back without shame. You can’t choose other people’s values for them, and you don’t have to adopt theirs to love them. You can share what matters to you, listen to what matters to them, and look for the places where your directions overlap.

Sunday, 9:20pm

You write three words on the back of an envelope: “people, health, making things.” Then one small thing for this week under each: call Gran, walk on Wednesday, finish the shelf. The week is still full. But now some of it is yours.

In everyday life

  • On your own: Three words on an envelope can be enough. Look at them on Sunday and pick one small thing for each. See Part One, Next.
  • With a partner: You may rank the same values differently, like security and adventure. Say what each of you is protecting before you argue about the plan.
  • Family: Two generations can both value “family” and mean different things by it: being close by, or being free to go. Ask what it means to them.
  • Friends: Friendships often rest on one shared value, like loyalty or fun. Naming it can help when life pulls you apart. See Friends.
  • At work: When a job keeps asking you to act against a value, the strain shows up as tiredness or cynicism. Naming the clash is a first step. See At work.
  • In your community: Volunteering, a faith group or a club can be a place to act on a value with other people.

Common mix-ups

  • “Values are rules.” Rules say what you must do. Values say what you care about. Rules can be handed to you. Values have to be yours to work.
  • “If I really valued it, I’d never fail at it.” Everyone drifts from their values. The value is the direction you turn back to, not proof that you never wandered.
  • “Meaning has to be something big.” For most people, most meaning comes from small, steady things: people, care, ordinary work.
  • “Happiness is the point of life.” Feeling good matters. So does doing things that matter. A worthwhile day can be a tiring one.
  • “My values should match my partner’s, or my family’s.” Close people often share some values and rank others differently. That’s normal, and it can be talked about.
  • “Philosophy is only for clever people.” The old questions are everyone’s questions: what matters, what’s fair, what’s up to me. You’re already asking them.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask, don’t tell. “What matters most to you about this?” helps more than “Here’s what you should care about.”
  • Notice values out loud. “You stayed late to help your friend. Loyalty really matters to you, doesn’t it?” People often see their values for the first time in someone else’s words.
  • With children, name the value when you see it: “You shared, even though you wanted it. That was generous.” It teaches more than a lecture.
  • Don’t use values as a weapon. “I thought you valued family” is a guilt trip, not a question. Ask what family means to them instead.
  • Respect a different order. You can disagree with someone’s choice and still see the good thing they were reaching for.

Words you could use: “What would a good outcome look like to you?” “What do you want to be able to say about how you handled this, a year from now?”

When to get more help

Feeling a bit lost or flat for a while is part of being human. But some signs mean it’s worth talking to someone trained:

  • Life has felt empty or pointless for more than two weeks, and things you used to enjoy don’t reach you. That can be depression, which is common and treatable. A doctor or counselor is a good first step.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be alive. Please talk to someone today. Free, private people to talk to lists crisis lines (in the US, you can call or text 988). If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • Someone in your life is pushing you to give up what matters to you, cutting you off from friends, or making you afraid to disagree. That’s about control, not values. When it’s more than a rough patch can help you think it through.

Some therapies, such as acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), work directly with values. A counselor can help you find yours when everything feels foggy.

Part Three · Chapter 11

Healing after hurt: repair, forgiveness, and when to get help

How hurt heals: why it lingers, how small breaks get mended, what a real apology sounds like, what forgiveness is and isn’t, and when it’s time to get more help.

In short:

  • Hurt is real, and healing takes time. Replaying it doesn’t mean you’re weak. It means it mattered.
  • Repair is what counts. Every close relationship has breaks. A real apology names it, owns it and mends it.
  • Forgiveness isn’t going back. You can forgive without forgetting, excusing or letting someone back in.
  • Safety comes first. You can heal even if they never say sorry, and you never owe anyone a risk to your safety.

Thursday, 2am

It was one sentence, three weeks ago. They’ve probably forgotten it. You haven’t. It plays again, word for word, and you think of all the things you could have said back. You’re tired of carrying it, and you don’t know how to put it down.

Everyone gets hurt, and everyone hurts other people, mostly without meaning to. This chapter explains how hurt works, how relationships mend, and how you can heal, with or without the other person. It’s for small hurts and bigger ones. If someone is hurting you on purpose, or you feel afraid, start with safety first: Not safe at home?

  1. Hurt is real, and healing takes time.

    Being hurt by someone shows that something mattered: the person, your trust, your sense of being respected. The mind replays hurts because it’s trying to understand them and keep you safe. Healing isn’t a straight line. The feeling can come back on an anniversary, or when something reminds you. That doesn’t mean you’ve failed. It means you’re still healing.

    For example (on your own): Months after a friend said something cruel at a party, Leo feels a jolt when he hears that song again. He reminds himself: “That’s an old bruise being pressed, not a new wound.”

    Thoughts you could try: “This hurts because it mattered.” “A feeling coming back doesn’t mean I’m back at the start.”

    Try this: When a hurt replays, notice it, name it (“there’s that memory again”), and gently bring your attention to something in the room you can see or touch.

    Dig deeper: why hurt lingers

  2. Every close relationship has breaks. Repair is what counts.

    No relationship is smooth all the time. People snap, forget, misread and let each other down. Research on parents and babies, on couples and on therapy all points the same way: breaks are normal. What matters most is whether they get mended. A small repair, done soon, often makes a relationship feel safer than if the break had never happened.

    For example (a parent and child): Dad snaps at his son for spilling juice. Twenty minutes later he sits down next to him: “I was grumpy and I shouted. That wasn’t fair. Spills happen. Want to help me make a new one?”

    Words you could use: “Can I have a do-over? That came out badly.” “I didn’t like how I spoke to you earlier. I’m sorry.”

    Try this: Think of a small break this week, with anyone. Go back and repair it with one sentence.

    Dig deeper: breaks and repair

  3. Telling someone they hurt you.

    People can’t mend what they don’t know about. Saying “that hurt” is a risk, but it gives the relationship a chance. It works best when you describe what happened and how it felt, rather than what kind of person they are. Pick a calm moment, not the middle of an argument.

    For example (a friend): Nadia says to her friend: “When you told the group about my breakup before I was ready, I felt exposed. I’d like to be the one who shares my news.” She didn’t call her friend a gossip. She said what happened and what she needs.

    Words you could use: “When… happened, I felt… What I’d like is…” “I’m telling you because I want us to be OK.”

    Try this: Write your sentence before you say it: what happened, how you felt, what you’d like. Keep it to three short lines.

    Dig deeper: saying “that hurt”

  4. A real apology names it, owns it, and mends it.

    “Sorry if you were upset” doesn’t heal much. A real apology says what you did, takes responsibility without excuses, shows you understand how it affected the other person, and offers to put it right or do things differently. Research, such as a study led by Roy Lewicki (2016), suggests owning it and offering repair are the parts that matter most.

    For example (a coworker): Priya presented a shared idea in a meeting as if it were hers. The next day she tells her colleague: “I presented our idea as mine. That wasn’t fair to you. I’ll email the team today and say it was joint work.”

    Words you could use: “I did… That was my doing. I can see it left you feeling… Here’s what I’ll do.”

    Try this: Next time you say sorry, check for the four parts: what I did, it was mine, how it affected you, what I’ll do now. See Apology languages for more.

    Dig deeper: what makes an apology work

  5. Forgiveness isn’t forgetting, excusing or going back.

    Forgiveness is usually described as letting go of resentment and the wish to get even. It’s something that happens inside you. It doesn’t mean saying what happened was OK. It doesn’t mean forgetting. And it doesn’t mean you have to trust the person again or have them in your life. Forgiveness is a choice, often a slow one, and you never owe it on someone else’s timetable.

    For example (a grown-up child and a parent): Maya forgave her father for missing much of her childhood. She still keeps visits short, and she still doesn’t leave her kids alone with him when he drinks. Forgiving him and protecting her family both feel right.

    Thoughts you could try: “I can let go of wanting to get even, and still keep my limits.” “I don’t have to forgive today. I can just stop feeding the anger today.”

    Try this: Write down what forgiving someone would mean to you, and what it would not mean. Seeing the difference can make it feel possible, or show you that you aren’t ready, which is fine too.

    Dig deeper: what forgiveness is

  6. You can heal even if they never say sorry.

    Waiting for an apology that never comes can keep you stuck. The person may be gone, unwilling, or unable to understand. Healing doesn’t have to depend on them. You can make sense of what happened, tell your story to someone safe, and decide what it means for you now.

    For example (an ex-partner): After a hard breakup, Jo writes a letter she never sends. She says everything: what hurt, what she wished, what she’s taking with her. Then she puts it away. Nothing changed for him. Something shifted for her.

    Thoughts you could try: “I don’t need them to understand for my healing to count.”

    Try this: Write about the hurt for fifteen minutes, three or four days in a row. Write about the facts and your feelings. If it ever makes you feel much worse, stop, and talk to someone instead.

    Dig deeper: healing on your own

  7. Trust comes back in small, kept promises.

    After trust is broken, one big apology rarely rebuilds it. Trust grows back slowly, in many small moments where someone does what they said they’d do. If you broke trust, being patient and consistent matters more than a grand gesture. If you were let down, it’s fair to watch what someone does, not just what they say.

    For example (partners): Sam hid a credit card debt from his partner. After it came out, he offered to share his bank statements each month, without being asked. After a year of small, steady openness, his partner says she can breathe again.

    Words you could use: “I know my words aren’t enough. I want to show you, over time.” “I need to see it for a while before I can feel it.”

    Try this: If you’re rebuilding trust, choose one small promise you can keep every week, and keep it.

    Dig deeper: how trust is rebuilt

  8. Going back is a choice. Safety comes first.

    After a hurt, you can mend the relationship, change its shape, or step away. All three can be healthy. If someone keeps hurting you after you’ve told them, if you feel afraid of them, or if they control, threaten or put you down, the work is to protect yourself, not to repair. Counseling together isn’t advised when someone is afraid of their partner.

    For example (housemates): Ellie’s housemate borrowed money twice and didn’t pay it back. Ellie forgave the first time. The second time, she said: “I won’t lend money again. I still want us to get on in the house.” A limit, not a war.

    Words you could use: “I’m not angry any more, and I’m not going back to how it was.” “I need some distance while I work out what I want.”

    Try this: Ask yourself three questions: Is it safe? Has the hurt stopped? Do I want this relationship in my life? Your answers can guide what comes next. If you feel unsafe, see signs to look out for.

    Dig deeper: limits, distance and safety

  9. Look after the hurt part of you.

    Hurt lives in the body as well as the mind: poor sleep, a tight chest, feeling jumpy. Healing goes better with basic care: rest, food, movement, people who are kind to you, and talking to yourself the way you’d talk to a hurting friend. Some people find they grow after something painful. That can happen, but it’s not a test you have to pass.

    For example (a stranger): A man shouted at Ade on the bus, calling him names. For days he felt shaky. He told a friend, went for long walks, and said to himself, “That was about him, not me. I’m safe now.” Slowly, the shakiness faded.

    Thoughts you could try: “This was hard. Anyone would be shaken. What do I need right now?”

    Try this: Pick one small act of care for today: an early night, a walk, a warm drink, a call to someone kind. Calm tools are in Upset right now?

    Dig deeper: healing in body and mind

Your part, and only your part. You can say what hurt, apologize for what you did, and look after your own healing. You can’t make someone apologize, understand or change, and you don’t have to wait for them to start feeling better. Forgiveness, trust and going back are all yours to choose, in your own time.

Saturday, 11am

You’ve written it all down, every word of it. Then a smaller version: “When you said that, I felt small. I’d like you to know.” You haven’t decided whether to send it. But the 2am replays have gone quieter, because now it’s on paper, not just in your head.

In everyday life

  • On your own: Writing the hurt down, every word, then a shorter version, can quieten the replays. You don’t have to send it.
  • With a partner: Small breaks happen daily. A quick “That came out wrong. Can I try again?” mends most of them. See How to apologize so it counts.
  • Family: Old family hurts often need a different talk from new ones, and sometimes more time apart. See Family rifts.
  • Friends: A friend may not know they hurt you. “Can I tell you something that stung? I’d rather say it than drift.” keeps the door open.
  • At work: Keep it to the facts and what you need next time. A short note afterwards helps both of you remember the same thing.
  • After someone has died: You can still heal a hurt with someone who is gone, by writing to them or talking it through with someone you trust. See Grief and later life.

Common mix-ups

  • “If I forgive, I’m saying it was OK.” Forgiving isn’t excusing. You can forgive and still say it was wrong.
  • “If I forgive, I have to let them back in.” Forgiveness and going back are separate choices.
  • “A good relationship never has breaks.” Every close relationship has them. Mending them is what builds safety.
  • “Saying sorry means it’s fixed.” An apology opens the door. Trust comes back through what happens next.
  • “I should be over this by now.” Healing has no deadline. Feelings returning doesn’t mean you’re back at the start.
  • “I can’t heal unless they admit it.” Their apology can help, but your healing can go ahead without it.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Listen before you advise. “That sounds really painful” helps more than “You should just let it go.”
  • Don’t rush forgiveness. Pushing someone to forgive can feel like being told their hurt doesn’t matter.
  • Don’t push them to cut someone off, either. It’s their relationship and their choice, unless they’re in danger.
  • If you caused the hurt, give a real apology and then give them time. Don’t ask them to make you feel better about it.
  • If they mention fear, threats or control, take it seriously and share Not safe at home?

Words you could use: “Do you want to talk it through, or would it help more to take your mind off it?” “Whatever you decide about them, I’m on your side.”

When to get more help

Some hurts are too heavy to carry alone, and getting help is a sign of strength, not weakness. Think about talking to a doctor, counselor or therapist if:

  • More than a month after something frightening, you still have flashbacks, nightmares, feel constantly on edge, or avoid places and people that remind you of it. These can be signs of trauma, and there are well-tested treatments for it, such as trauma-focused talking therapies.
  • The hurt is taking over: you can’t sleep, work or enjoy anything, or you’re drinking or using more to cope.
  • The hurt came from abuse or violence, now or in the past. Free, private people to talk to includes specialist lines.
  • You’re having thoughts of hurting yourself or someone else. Please reach out today: in the US, call or text 988; elsewhere, see crisis lines or call your local emergency number.

If you and someone else both want to mend things, a couples or family counselor can help, as long as nobody is afraid of the other.

Helping others well: support without fixing, boundaries, and not burning out

How to help someone without taking over: asking what they need, listening first, caring without drowning, keeping kind limits, what to do in a crisis, and how to keep going without burning out.

In short:

  • Ask what kind of help they want. “Ideas, or just someone to listen?” saves a lot of misfires.
  • Listening is help. Fixing it for someone can take away their power.
  • Your limits make your help last. Caring from a steady place helps more than drowning in their feelings.
  • In a crisis, stay and bring in help. It’s OK to ask directly about suicide, and to call for help.

Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend is telling you about her awful week. You have five good ideas before she’s finished her first sentence. You share them all. She goes quiet, then says, “Yeah, maybe.” You both hang up feeling a bit worse, and you’re not sure why.

Most of us want to help the people around us. But helping well is a skill, and good intentions don’t always land. This chapter explains how support works, why “fixing” can backfire, and how to care for others in a way you can keep up. It’s for friends, partners, parents, coworkers, carers, neighbors, and anyone who has ever wanted to help and didn’t know how.

  1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in different kinds. Some people want ideas. Some want someone to listen. Some want a practical hand, like a meal or a lift. Some just want company. Giving the wrong kind, even kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard. The simplest fix is to ask.

    For example (a friend): In the scene above, a better start would have been: “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to vent?” She would have said “vent,” and the call would have gone very differently.

    Words you could use: “Do you want ideas, a listening ear, or a hand with something?” “What would help most right now?”

    Try this: Next time someone tells you about a problem, ask what kind of help they want before you give any.

    Dig deeper: kinds of support

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being truly heard is powerful. It helps people calm down, think more clearly, and often find their own answers. Good listening means paying attention, not interrupting, checking you understood, and showing that their feelings make sense, even if you’d feel differently.

    For example (partners): Ravi comes home upset about his boss. Instead of “Just quit,” his partner says: “So he criticized you in front of everyone, after you stayed late all week. No wonder you’re furious.” Ravi’s shoulders drop. Ten minutes later, he has his own plan.

    Words you could use: “So what happened was… Have I got that right?” “That makes sense. Anyone would feel that way.”

    Try this: In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice. Then sum up what you heard.

    Dig deeper: how listening helps

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone, we want to make the problem go away. But doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable. Helping well often means helping someone do it, not doing it for them. It respects that it’s their life and their choice.

    For example (a parent and a grown-up child): Grace’s 24-year-old son is struggling with a job application. She wants to rewrite it. Instead she asks, “Want me to read it and tell you what stands out?” He makes the changes himself, and feels proud when he gets the interview.

    Thoughts you could try: “Is this mine to fix, or theirs to solve with my support?” “What do they want to do here?”

    Try this: Notice the urge to fix. Before acting, ask: “Would you like me to do that, or help you do it?”

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were your own can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion: warm concern from a steadier place. Compassion still cares deeply. It just doesn’t require you to sink with the other person. A steady helper is more useful than a second person drowning.

    For example (housemates): When Abi’s housemate went through a breakup, Abi cried every night with her and couldn’t sleep. After a week she was exhausted. She changed approach: kind and present, but also taking breaks, and reminding herself, “This is her pain. I can care without carrying all of it.”

    Thoughts you could try: “I can be with them in this without making it mine.” “I care, and I’m steady.”

    Try this: After a heavy conversation, do something that brings you back to yourself: a walk, a shower, five slow breaths. It isn’t abandoning them. It’s refilling.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything isn’t kindness in the long run. It leads to resentment, exhaustion and, eventually, not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can give, so your yes means something. A kind no, with a suggestion of what you can do, is still help.

    For example (siblings): Kwame’s sister keeps asking to borrow money. He loves her and he’s running out. He says: “I can’t lend money any more. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you’d like.”

    Words you could use: “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    Try this: Write down one thing you’re giving that you can’t keep giving. Plan a kinder, smaller version you could offer instead.

    Dig deeper: boundaries in helping

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot, like carers, parents, nurses, teachers and the friend everyone calls, can burn out. Signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, feeling numb or cynical about the people you help, getting ill more often, or dreading the next call. It doesn’t mean you’re a bad helper. It means you need help too.

    For example (a carer): Helen has looked after her husband since his stroke. She snaps at him, then feels terrible. Her doctor says it sounds like carer strain. A local carers’ group and a few hours of respite each week give her back a little of herself, and she’s gentler at home.

    Thoughts you could try: “Looking after myself is part of looking after them.”

    Try this: Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love.

    Dig deeper: burnout and refilling

  7. In a crisis, stay with them and bring in help.

    If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s OK to ask them directly. Research, such as a review led by Tim Dazzi (2014), suggests asking about suicide doesn’t put the idea in someone’s head; it can bring relief. You don’t have to be their therapist. Your job is to listen, stay with them, and help them reach trained support.

    For example (a coworker): A colleague says, “Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me.” Jordan says gently: “I’m really glad you told me. Are you thinking about ending your life?” She says she’s had thoughts. Jordan stays with her and they call a crisis line together.

    Words you could use: “Are you thinking about suicide?” “I’m glad you told me. You don’t have to go through this alone. Let’s call someone together.”

    Try this: Save a crisis number in your phone now, so you have it if you ever need it. In the US, call or text 988. Other countries are listed in free, private people to talk to. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

    Dig deeper: helping in a crisis

  8. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something go wrong, each person can assume someone else will act. Psychologists call this the bystander effect. Knowing about it helps you break it. You don’t have to be a hero. Small, safe actions count: asking “Are you OK?”, getting staff, or calling for help.

    For example (a stranger): On a train, a man is making a young woman uncomfortable. Dee doesn’t confront him. She sits next to the woman and asks loudly, “Hi! Is this the train to Leeds?” The woman joins in the chat, and the man moves away.

    Words you could use: “Are you OK? Do you need anything?” To someone else nearby: “Can you get the driver? I’ll stay here.”

    Try this: Think of one safe thing you could do if you saw someone in trouble in a public place: distract, get help, or check in afterward. Having a plan makes it easier to act.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  9. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways. People who are good at giving help are often bad at receiving it. But letting others help is a gift to them, too: most people like being useful to someone they care about. Relationships where only one person ever helps tend to feel uneven over time.

    For example (on your own, as a new parent): Mo kept saying, “We’re fine, thanks,” to every offer after the baby came. When she finally said yes to a neighbor’s offer of a meal, the neighbor was delighted, and they became friends.

    Words you could use: “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

    Try this: Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage without it.

    Dig deeper: receiving help

Your part, and only your part. You can offer help, listen well, and be honest about your limits. You can’t make someone accept help, take your advice or get better on your timetable. Their life and their choices are theirs. If someone is in danger, your part is to bring in help, not to carry it alone.

The next Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend calls again. This time you say, “Rough week? Do you want ideas, or just to talk it out?” She laughs and says, “Just talk.” You listen. At the end, she says, “I think I know what I’m going to do.” You didn’t give her a single idea, and it was the most helpful call you’ve had in months.

In everyday life

  • On your own: Helping yourself counts too. Ask yourself the same question: “Do I need ideas right now, or just some rest?”
  • With a partner: “Do you want me to help, or just to hear it?” works at home as well as anywhere.
  • Family: Caring for a parent or a child with extra needs is long-haul help. Share the load early. See Caring for someone you love.
  • Friends: The friend everyone calls needs someone to call too. Notice who that might be.
  • At work: Covering for a struggling coworker is kind for a week. Longer than that, it needs a real conversation with the team. See At work.
  • With strangers: In an emergency, point to one person: “You in the blue coat, please call for help.” A crowd often waits for someone else.

Common mix-ups

  • “Helping means solving the problem.” Often, helping means listening, being there, or helping someone find their own answer.
  • “If I really cared, I’d feel everything they feel.” Caring from a steady place helps more and lasts longer.
  • “Good helpers never say no.” Good helpers last because they know their limits.
  • “Asking about suicide might give them the idea.” Research suggests it doesn’t, and asking can help someone open up.
  • “I’ll make it worse if I step in.” There are small, safe ways to help. Doing nothing often feels worse for the person in trouble.
  • “I should be able to manage without help.” Everyone needs help sometimes. Receiving it is part of a healthy relationship.

If you want to help someone else with this

This whole chapter is about helping others, so here’s help for the helpers around you:

  • Notice the helpers. The carer, the friend everyone calls, the coworker who always covers. Ask them how they are, and mean it.
  • Offer specific help. “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.”
  • Give them permission to rest. “You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this.”
  • If a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love or suggest they talk to a doctor.

Words you could use: “You look after everyone. Who looks after you?” “I’d love to do something for you this week. What would help?”

  • If you’re burned out as a helper or carer, talk to your doctor, a carers’ organization or a counselor. Respite and support are not luxuries.
  • If helping someone is becoming unsafe for you, step back and bring in professionals. You can still care from a safer distance.

Anger: what it’s for and what to do with it

What anger is for, what to do in the hot minutes, how to repair afterwards, and how to tell when anger has turned into harm. For anyone, in any kind of relationship, or on your own.

In short:

  • Anger is a signal that something seems unfair, blocked or threatening. The feeling is allowed. What you do with it is the part you choose.
  • The first minutes are about the body. Slow down, breathe out long, and step away with a time to come back.
  • Letting rip doesn’t empty anger. Shouting tends to feed it. Calming the body, then putting it into words and repairing any sharp moments, helps more.
  • Anger that frightens, controls or hurts someone has become harm. That needs more help, and safety comes first.

Tuesday, 6:10pm

Your brother texts: “Can’t take Mum to the hospital on Thursday after all. Sorry!!” It’s the third time this month. Your jaw sets and your thumbs start typing fast: “TYPICAL. You never…” Your heart is pounding, your face is hot, and the message is nearly sent.

Many of us were taught anger was rude or dangerous, or grew up around anger that was frightening. But anger itself is a normal human feeling, and it often points at something real. This chapter explains how anger works first, and what to do with it second.

  1. Anger is a signal about something that matters.

    Anger usually says “That’s not fair,” “You’re in my way” or “Someone crossed a line.” It pushes us towards a problem, to change it. It can also land on the wrong person, or be far bigger than the problem.

    For example: A woman keeps finding her lunch gone from the office fridge. Her anger tells her a line has been crossed, and gets her to raise it.

    Thoughts you could try: “What is this anger trying to protect?” “Is this about fairness, a blocked plan, or feeling unsafe?”

    Try this: Next time you feel angry, finish this sentence privately: “I’m angry because something I care about, which is ___, seems ___.”

    Dig deeper: what anger is for

  2. Anger often guards a softer feeling.

    Anger can arrive first because it feels stronger and safer than what sits under it: hurt, fear, shame, worry or plain tiredness. In Chapter 1 we called this layers. Finding the layer underneath often shows what you actually need.

    For example: A man snaps at his teenage daughter for coming home an hour late. Under the anger is a cold hour of fear: “What if something happened?” When he says that part, she hears it, and apologizes.

    Words you could use: “I’m angry, and I think underneath I was really scared.” “I came across cross. I think I’m actually hurt.”

    Try this: After the next angry moment, when you’re calm, ask: “Was there hurt, fear or tiredness under it?”

    Dig deeper: what anger can cover

  3. Anger rises in the body fast.

    A hot face, a tight jaw, a pounding heart, a louder voice, fists or shoulders tensing. This is the body getting ready for a fight, and it can happen in seconds. When anger is high, careful thinking and listening get much harder. Chapter 2 calls this being flooded: so stirred up that the thinking part of you goes quiet for a while.

    For example: Arguing about the bins, a woman notices her hands shaking and realises she can’t hear a word her housemate says. That’s her sign to stop.

    Thoughts you could try: “My body is ready for a fight. That doesn’t mean I have to have one.” “Where is this in my body?”

    Try this: Learn your own early signs. Write down two or three, like “jaw tight” or “talking faster.” They are your cue to slow down.

    Dig deeper: anger in the body

  4. In the hot minutes: slow the body first.

    When anger is high, settle your body before you try to sort anything out. Simple steps help: stop talking or typing, breathe out slowly (longer out than in), unclench your hands, and if you can, move away for a while. A large review of 154 studies by Kjærvik and Bushman (2024) found that activities which calm the body, like slow breathing, relaxation and mindfulness, reduced anger, while ones that rev you up showed little or no benefit.

    For example: A dad feels his temper rising while helping with homework. He says, “I’m getting frustrated, and that’s not your fault. I’m going to get a glass of water and come back in five minutes.” He does. The homework gets done.

    Words you could use: “I’m too angry to talk well right now. I’ll come back at eight.” “I need ten minutes. I’m not leaving the conversation, I’m coming back to it.”

    Try this: Make a three-step plan on a calm day: your early sign, the words you’ll say, and where you’ll go for ten minutes. Upset right now? has steps for the hottest moments.

    Dig deeper: what to do in the moment

  5. Letting rip doesn’t empty it.

    Many people believe anger is like steam: let it out, by shouting, slamming or hitting a pillow, and it will be gone. Research doesn’t support this. In a well-known study by Bushman (2002), angry people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them ended up angrier, not calmer. So does going over and over what happened in your head. Anger tends to fade faster when the body is calmed and the mind has something else to do for a while.

    For example: After a row with a coworker, a man spends his lunch telling three people how awful she is. By the afternoon he’s angrier than ever. Next time, he takes a walk instead, and by two o’clock he can think clearly.

    Thoughts you could try: “Going over this again is feeding it.” “I can come back to this later, when I’m calmer.”

    Try this: If you notice yourself replaying an argument, set a timer for twenty minutes and do something absorbing. Come back to the problem after.

    Dig deeper: venting and going over it

  6. Step back and look from the outside.

    Once the body has settled a little, it helps to look at the moment as if you were watching from across the room. What happened? What might the other person have been dealing with? In experiments by Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman (2012), people who looked back on an annoying moment “like a fly on the wall,” rather than reliving it, felt less angry and acted less aggressively.

    For example: Furious that a friend cancelled again, a woman imagines telling the story to a kind stranger. Told that way, she remembers her friend is caring for a sick parent. The anger softens into worry.

    Thoughts you could try: “If I were watching this from the side, what would I see?” “What else might be going on for them?”

    Try this: Write two lines about the moment, starting with your own name instead of “I”: “Sam got angry because…”

    Dig deeper: stepping back

  7. Say it, don’t throw it.

    Anger is often a fair message sent in a hurtful way. When you are calmer, you can still say you were angry, and why, without attacking. A simple shape helps: what happened (the facts), how you felt, and what you’re asking for.

    For example: Instead of “You always let me down,” a woman says to her sister: “When you cancelled Mum’s appointment again, I felt really angry and alone with it. Can we make a plan for who takes her, for the next three months?”

    Words you could use: “I was angry about what happened on Monday. Can I tell you why?” “When ___ happened, I felt ___. What I’m asking for is ___.”

    Try this: Write your angry message, then rewrite it in the three-part shape before you send anything. See Chapter 4 for more on saying hard things.

    Dig deeper: saying anger in words

  8. After anger, repair.

    Everyone gets angry, and most of us say something sharp sometimes. What matters a great deal is what happens next. A short, real repair (naming what you did, without “but,” and saying what you’ll do differently) helps rebuild trust.

    For example: A grandmother shouts at her grandson for breaking a vase, and he cries. An hour later she sits with him: “I shouted, and that was scary. The vase isn’t as important as you. I’m sorry.” He hugs her.

    Words you could use: “I was sharp with you earlier. That wasn’t fair, and I’m sorry.” “I’m still upset about what happened, and I also shouldn’t have spoken to you that way.”

    Try this: Think of one recent moment when your anger landed on someone. Is there a short repair you could still make? Chapter 11 has more on apologies that land.

    Dig deeper: repair after anger

  9. When anger becomes harm.

    Feeling angry is human. But some things done in anger are harm, whatever the feeling behind them: hitting, pushing, throwing things at or near someone, breaking their things, threatening, shouting so someone is scared, or using anger to control what another person does, wears, spends or who they see. People walking on eggshells around you is a sign too. Anger is never a reason someone deserves to be hurt.

    For example: A man realises his partner and children go quiet when he comes home in a bad mood. He doesn’t hit anyone, but they are afraid of him. He talks to his doctor and joins an anger course.

    Words you could use: “I think my anger is scaring people I love. I want help with it.” (If it’s happening to you) “I’m frightened of how they get when they’re angry.”

    Try this: If this sounds like you, see help with your own anger. If you’re afraid of someone else’s anger, Not safe at home? lists free, private help.

    Dig deeper: when anger becomes harm

Your part, and only your part. You can learn your early signs, settle your body, say what made you angry in words, and repair when you get it wrong. You can’t make someone else calm down, and you don’t have to accept being shouted at, threatened or controlled. If someone else’s anger frightens you, getting safe matters more than working it out.   • you have hit, pushed, thrown things, broken things or threatened anyone;
  • anger is hurting your work, your friendships or your health;
  • you are using alcohol or drugs to cope with angry feelings.

Part Three · Chapter 14

Personality, temperament and change across life

What personality is, where temperament comes in, how people change across their lives, and how to live and work with people who are made differently from you, without putting anyone in a box.

In short:

  • Traits are dials, not boxes. Everyone sits somewhere along each one, and most people are near the middle.
  • Temperament is a starting point. Babies already differ in how active, cautious or easily upset they are. What happens next matters too.
  • People keep changing. On average, people grow steadier and more organised as they get older, and you can change on purpose, slowly.
  • Different isn’t wrong. Most clashes between personalities are about fit, not fault.

Saturday, 3:00pm

It’s your aunt’s seventieth birthday. You’re standing quietly by the cake when she says, loud enough for everyone: “Oh, you were always the shy one!” Everyone laughs. You smile, but something stings. You’re forty-two. You teach thirty children every day. Are you still “the shy one”? Were you ever?

We all describe people by their personality: “She’s so laid-back,” “He’s a worrier,” “They’re the organised one.” Descriptions like these can turn into boxes. This chapter looks at how personality actually works, what stays the same, and what changes.

  1. Traits are dials, not boxes.

    Personality is your usual pattern of thinking, feeling and acting. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”: openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organised and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are). The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them. Each one is a dial, not a switch. Most people sit somewhere in the middle of most dials.

    For example: Two friends take a quiz. One gets “introvert,” the other “extravert.” When they look at their actual scores, they are only a few points apart, both near the middle.

    Thoughts you could try: “I’m more of this than that, some of the time.” “A label is a summary, not a whole person.”

    Try this: For each of the five dials, mark where you think you sit, from one to ten. Then ask: where would a close friend put you?

    Dig deeper: the five dials

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality we can see from very early on. Some babies are very active, some calm; some settle easily into new routines, others take time; some react strongly to noise or change. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences, and the idea of “goodness of fit”: how well a child’s temperament fits what the people and places around them expect.

    For example: A slow-to-warm-up toddler cries at every new playgroup. Her mum stops pushing her to join in straight away and lets her watch from her lap for ten minutes first. By the third week, she joins in on her own.

    Words you could use: “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made, not a flaw.”

    Try this: Think about what you were like as a small child, from what family say or what you remember. What seems the same now? What seems different?

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality is a pattern, not a rule. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, organised in their job and messy at home, patient with children and short with call centres. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across many situations, not what they will do in this one.

    For example: A manager thinks a new colleague is “cold.” At the team’s charity football match, the same colleague is chatty, funny and warm. The office was the thing that was quiet, not him.

    Thoughts you could try: “I’ve only seen them in one setting.” “Where am I most myself, and where am I least?”

    Try this: Before deciding what someone is “like,” try to see them in one different setting, or ask them about one.

    Dig deeper: person and situation

  4. Introvert and extravert is about energy, not liking people.

    People who are more introverted often enjoy people very much, but find busy, noisy social time uses up energy, and need quiet to recover. People who are more extraverted often feel energised by company and busy places. Most of us are a mix (sometimes called ambivert). Neither is better. They just need different things to feel at their best.

    For example: After a wedding, one partner wants to go on to the after-party; the other wants bed and silence. They agree: one goes for an hour, the other goes home, and they have breakfast together.

    Words you could use: “I loved tonight, and I’m out of social energy. It’s not about you.” “Shall we go together, and I’ll head home at ten?”

    Try this: Notice what refills you after a busy week: company, quiet, or a bit of both. Plan one of those this weekend.

    Dig deeper: introversion and extraversion

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable, but it isn’t fixed. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organised, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness kept growing right across the lifespan. Big life events, roles and relationships all play a part.

    For example: A man who was a nervous, disorganised student becomes a calm, steady nurse in his forties. His friends from school barely recognise him. He says, “Life kept asking me to cope, and slowly I learned how.”

    Thoughts you could try: “Who I was at fifteen isn’t who I have to be now.” “People I knew years ago may have changed too.”

    Try this: Write down one way you have changed in the last ten years. What helped it happen?

    Dig deeper: how personality changes with age

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    People who want to change a trait can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways, again and again. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended, over sixteen weeks, to change in the direction they wanted. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially towards more emotional steadiness.

    For example: A woman who wants to be less shy at work makes one small plan: say one thing in each team meeting. After two months, speaking up feels ordinary.

    Thoughts you could try: “I don’t have to become a different person. I can practise one different thing.”

    Try this: Pick one small action that fits who you’d like to be more of, like “reply to messages the same day” or “say hello first.” Do it three times this week. See Chapter 6 on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    There isn’t a “good” personality. Each trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are kind and easy to be with, and may find it hard to say no. Very conscientious people are reliable, and may struggle to rest. People high in openness love new things, and may get bored with routine. Knowing your mix helps you use the gifts and plan for the costs.

    For example: A highly organised woman feels irritated by her relaxed housemate. Then the boiler breaks, and the housemate is the one who stays calm and finds a plumber at 10pm. She starts to see his calm as a strength.

    Thoughts you could try: “What is the gift in this trait, and what is the cost?” “What they do that annoys me might be the flip side of something I value.”

    Try this: Pick one trait of yours that people sometimes complain about. Write down one time it helped.

    Dig deeper: gifts and costs

  8. Differences are about fit, not fault.

    When two people with different personalities share a home, a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one, a planner and someone spontaneous, a talker and someone who needs quiet: neither is wrong. What helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it. Personality is also different from neurodivergence and from mental health conditions. If something feels bigger than personality, Chapter 9 may help.

    For example: A couple fight about holidays: one wants every hour planned, the other wants to wander. They agree on one plan for the morning and a free afternoon. They both enjoy it more.

    Words you could use: “I think we’re just made differently here. What would work for both of us?” “I’m a planner and you’re not. Can we plan half and leave half?”

    Try this: Think of one person you clash with. Name the difference without blame: “I like ___; they like ___.” Then think of one small arrangement that would suit you both.

    Dig deeper: fit, not fault

Your part, and only your part. You can get to know your own mix, look after what you need, and practise one small change if you want to. You can’t change someone else’s personality, and you don’t have to change yours to be loved. If you’re always the one bending to fit, it’s fair to say so. Know your own wiring

Saturday, 5:30pm

Later, as people are leaving, you sit down next to your aunt. “I was the shy one, wasn’t I,” you say. “I still like quiet. These days I stand in front of thirty children every morning.” She laughs. “Well, I was a terror at your age, and look at me now: I do flower arranging.” You both laugh. You were never just “the shy one.” Neither of you is who you were at ten, and that’s fine.

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you plan a life that suits you: how much company, how much quiet, how much routine, how much new. “I’m just not a people person” might be true, or an old label worth testing.

  • With a partner: Different traits are normal. A shared plan (“I’ll handle bills; you handle holidays”) often works better than trying to change each other. See When we keep having the same fight.
  • With friends: A quiet friend who doesn’t text much may still care a great deal. Ask what kind of contact suits them. See Friends.
  • Parents and children: A child’s temperament isn’t a sign of good or bad parenting. Adjusting to fit them is a skill, not giving in. See For parents.
  • Family: Family roles (“the clever one,” “the difficult one”) can stick long after they stop fitting. You’re allowed to grow out of them.
  • At work: Teams need a mix. Ask “How do you like to work?” rather than guessing from a quiz. See At work.

Common mix-ups

  • “You’re either an introvert or an extravert.” Most people are somewhere in the middle, and it can shift with setting and mood.
  • “Personality is set by adulthood.” It keeps changing, slowly, across the whole of life.
  • “Some personalities are better.” Every trait has gifts and costs. What matters is fit and how you use it.
  • “Online type quizzes tell you who you are.” Many popular quizzes sort people into a handful of types. Real people are spread along each dial, not sorted into groups. Use quizzes for fun and conversation, not as a label.
  • “They’re just difficult.” “Difficult” often means “different from me.” It can also hide stress, illness or a need nobody has asked about.
  • “Changing would mean being fake.” Practising something new feels unnatural at first. Over time, it becomes part of you.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Drop the old labels. Notice if you still describe someone the way they were years ago, and look for who they are now.
  • Ask, don’t type. “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” beats guessing from their personality.
  • Notice growth out loud. “You handled that so calmly” can help someone see a change they hadn’t noticed.
  • Don’t use personality as a weapon. “You’re so uptight” or “You’re just lazy” closes doors. Describe the action, not the person.

Words you could use: “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits any more.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”

When to get more help

Personality differences are normal and don’t need fixing. It may be worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or psychologist if:

  • a pattern in how you feel, think or relate to people keeps causing you or others real distress, across many parts of life;
  • you feel very unsure who you are, or your moods swing so much it’s hard to cope;
  • worry, low mood or fear of people is stopping you from living the life you want.

Please don’t diagnose yourself or anyone else with a “personality disorder” from what you read online. Those are clinical terms that only a qualified professional can use, after a proper assessment.

A personality trait never excuses controlling, frightening or hurtful behaviour. If you’re afraid of someone close to you, Not safe at home? lists free, private help.

Part Three · Chapter 15

Closeness and love: being known, at every age

How closeness grows, what it means to feel truly known, how love changes over the years, and how it looks in every kind of relationship, at every age, including for people on their own.

In short:

  • Love comes in many forms. Friends, family, partners, chosen family and the love you give yourself are all real love.
  • Closeness grows from being known. You share something real, and the other person responds with understanding and care.
  • Love changes over time. Early excitement often settles, and long love can stay warm, close and even exciting.
  • Love is something you do, in small ways, every day. It never includes fear or control.

Friday, 10:30pm

You and Sam have shared a home for eleven years. Tonight you’re both on the sofa, both on your phones. You realise you can’t remember the last time you talked about anything except bills, the boiler and whose turn it is to cook. You love Sam. You just feel, oddly, a little alone in the same room.

This chapter isn’t only about romance. It’s about closeness in all its forms: what helps people feel near to each other, why it can fade, and how it can grow again. It builds on Chapter 5, on attachment and trust.

  1. Love comes in many forms.

    The ancient Greeks had several words for love: for friends, for family, for romantic partners and for strangers. Today people love in many ways too: lifelong friendships, partners of any gender, people with more than one partner, chosen family, people who are asexual or aromantic and build deep closeness without romance or sex, and people on their own who pour love into friends, family, pets, work and community. No form is the “real” one. A life can be full of love without a partner in it.

    For example: A woman in her fifties who has never wanted a partner says her closest relationship is with her best friend of thirty years. They’ve been through two house moves, one cancer scare and countless Sunday phone calls. “That’s my great love story,” she says.

    Thoughts you could try: “Who are the people I love, in all the forms it takes?” “The love I have counts, even if it doesn’t look like a film.”

    Try this: Write down three people (or more) you love, of any kind. Next to each, write one way you show it.

    Dig deeper: the many forms of love

  2. Closeness grows from being known.

    Intimacy isn’t only about touch or romance. At its heart is being known. Psychologists Reis and Shaver described it as a back-and-forth: one person shares something real about themselves, and the other responds in a way that leaves them feeling understood, accepted and cared for. In a diary study by Laurenceau, Barrett and Pietromonaco (1998), feeling that the other person really responded was a big part of what made everyday conversations feel intimate.

    For example: Two brothers who talk mainly about football are driving to their dad’s care home. One says, “I find these visits really hard.” The other says, “Me too. I always feel guilty on the way home.” They’ve never felt closer.

    Words you could use: “Can I tell you something I haven’t said out loud before?” “Thank you for telling me. That makes sense.”

    Try this: Share one small, true thing with someone you care about: a worry, a hope, or something that made you happy this week. Then notice how they respond.

    Dig deeper: intimacy as being known

  3. Closeness is built a step at a time.

    People usually get close by sharing a little more, and a little more, when it feels safe. In a well-known study by Aron and colleagues (1997), pairs of strangers who took turns asking gradually more personal questions (now known as the “36 questions”) felt closer after 45 minutes than pairs who made small talk. The same idea works in friendships and long relationships: closeness grows when both people take small, matched steps.

    For example: Two new neighbours start with weather and parking. Over a few months, it becomes “How’s your mum doing?” and later, “Can I tell you what’s really been going on?” Neither rushed it.

    Words you could use: “What’s something you’re looking forward to?” “What’s been on your mind lately?” “What was the best part of your week?”

    Try this: Ask someone one question a little deeper than usual. If they answer, share something of your own back.

    Dig deeper: building closeness step by step

  4. Feeling understood matters most.

    What makes someone feel loved is often less about grand gestures and more about feeling understood. Researchers call this responsiveness: noticing, understanding and caring about what matters to the other person. It shows in small moments, like how you react to good news. Gable and colleagues (2004) found that people whose partners usually met their good news with real interest and warmth felt closer and more satisfied together.

    For example: A teenager says, “I got picked for the band!” Her grandad stops what he’s doing, smiles, and asks, “What instrument? When’s the first concert?” She talks for twenty minutes.

    Words you could use: “Tell me everything!” “That sounds like it really mattered to you.” “What was that like?”

    Try this: The next time someone shares good news, put your phone down and ask two follow-up questions.

    Dig deeper: responsiveness

  5. Old maps show up in love.

    The patterns from Chapter 5 often come out most strongly with the people we love most. Hazan and Shaver (1987) suggested that adult love uses the same attachment system as childhood bonds. When closeness feels shaky, some people reach harder for reassurance; others pull back to feel safe. Neither is bad. Both are ways of protecting something precious.

    For example: When his partner goes quiet, Leo texts five times in an hour. His partner, who goes quiet to calm down, feels crowded. They agree: “I need an hour, and I’ll message you at eight.” Leo can wait for a known time.

    Words you could use: “I’m feeling unsure of us right now. Can you tell me we’re okay?” “I need some space, and I’m coming back. Let’s talk at eight.”

    Try this: Notice what you do when closeness feels shaky: reach out, pull back, or both. Tell someone you trust what helps you feel safe again.

    Dig deeper: attachment in adult love

  6. Love changes over time.

    Early love is often intense, with racing thoughts and a lot of longing. Over years, it usually settles into something calmer and deeper. That isn’t failure. A review by Acevedo and Aron (2009) found that some people in long relationships still report strong romantic love, without the anxious, obsessive edge of early love, and that this was linked to being happier together. In experiments by Aron and colleagues (2000), couples who tried a new, mildly challenging activity together felt better about their relationship afterwards than couples given a dull task.

    For example: After twenty-five years, a couple take a pottery class. They’re both terrible at it and laugh all evening. On the way home, one says, “I feel like we’re on a date.”

    Thoughts you could try: “Calm isn’t the same as cold.” “What could we try that’s new for both of us?”

    Try this: Plan one small new thing with someone you love: a new walk, a new recipe, a new place. New for both of you matters more than big.

    Dig deeper: how love changes

  7. Love at every age.

    Love looks different across a life. Teenagers’ first loves can be huge and real. Adults juggle love with work, children and caring for parents. Many older people choose to spend their time with fewer, closer people. Carstensen and colleagues (1999) linked this to feeling that time is more limited, which makes the most meaningful relationships matter more. People find new love at seventy and eighty. People lose great loves and carry them always. All of it counts.

    For example: A widower of seventy-eight meets a woman at his walking group. His children are surprised. He says, “I loved your mother for fifty years. I still do. And I’m allowed to be happy now.”

    Thoughts you could try: “It’s never too late to be close to someone.” “Missing someone is a form of love too.”

    Try this: Reach out to someone from another generation this week: an older relative, a younger neighbour or a colleague. Ask them about love in their life, if it feels right.

    Dig deeper: love across the lifespan

  8. Love is something you do.

    Feelings of love come and go. The actions of love can be steady: turning towards someone when they speak, keeping small promises, saying thank you, saying sorry, making room for who they are. Physical closeness, like a hug or holding hands, matters a lot to some people and less to others, and sex is part of closeness for some relationships and not others. What matters is that both people feel comfortable and free to say yes or no. Love never includes fear, threats or control.

    For example: A friend notices her friend has been quiet since his divorce. She doesn’t say “I love you,” but every Thursday she texts, “Walk?” and turns up. That is love too.

    Words you could use: “What makes you feel most loved by me?” “Is this okay for you?” “I noticed you’ve been quiet. I’m here.”

    Try this: Ask someone you love, “What’s one small thing that makes you feel cared for?” Then do it this week.

    Dig deeper: love as action

Your part, and only your part. You can share a little of yourself, listen well, turn towards the people you love and keep small promises. You can’t make someone love you, or love you in the way you want. If closeness keeps hurting you, or you feel afraid, that is not a failure of love on your part. Not safe at home?

This time you leave both phones in the kitchen. You make two cups of tea and ask, “What’s something you’ve been thinking about lately that you haven’t told me?” Sam is quiet for a moment, then talks about their dad getting older, and how scared they are of the phone ringing. You listen, and share your own worry about work. It’s midnight before you notice the time. Nothing big was fixed. But you both feel known again.

In everyday life

On your own

Being single, widowed, divorced or happily alone doesn’t mean a life without love. Friendships, family, community and the way you treat yourself are all places where love lives. If you want a partner and don’t have one, that longing is real and worth being gentle with. See On my own and Grief and later life.

  • Partners: Most partners drift into talking only about jobs and bills. One real question a week can bring you back. See Turning toward and Check-ins.
  • More than two partners: Closeness with each person needs its own time and attention. See More than two.
  • Friends: Friendship is love too, and it needs tending like any other. See Friends.
  • Family: Parents, children, siblings and grandparents often love each other deeply and say it rarely. A short “I’m glad you’re my sister” can mean a lot.
  • Long distance: Being known can still happen across miles, through small, regular check-ins. See Long distance.

Common mix-ups

  • “Real love means a romantic partner.” Friendship, family and chosen family are real love.
  • “If the spark fades, love is over.” Early intensity usually settles. Many long relationships stay warm, close and lively.
  • “If they loved me, they’d just know what I need.” Nobody reads minds. Saying what helps is a gift, not a failure.
  • “Jealousy proves love.” Feeling jealous is human. Checking, controlling or punishing is not love. See signs to look out for.
  • “Intimacy means sex.” Intimacy means being known. It can include physical closeness, or not.
  • “Love should be effortless.” Love usually takes small, steady effort, and that effort is part of what love is.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Respect their kind of love. Don’t assume everyone wants a partner, children or one kind of relationship.
  • Be a safe person to tell things to. Listen without judging, and keep what they share private.
  • After a loss or breakup, keep showing up. Many people get lots of support for two weeks and then very little.
  • If you’re worried about someone’s relationship, stay close and kind rather than criticising their partner. If they seem afraid, gently share Not safe at home?

Words you could use: “I’m always glad to hear from you.” “You don’t have to explain your relationship to me. I just want you to be happy and safe.”

When to get more help

  • If you feel very lonely or low most days for two weeks or more, talk to a doctor or counsellor.
  • If closeness feels frightening, or you keep ending up in relationships that hurt you, a counsellor or therapist can help you understand why.
  • Couples, family or relationship counselling can help when you feel stuck, distant or keep having the same fight.
  • If someone you love controls you, frightens you, checks your phone, cuts you off from friends or pressures you into anything you don’t want, that isn’t love. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
  • If you’re in danger now, call your local emergency number. Upset right now? can help you take the first step.
