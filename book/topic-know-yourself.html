Part One, Then: where your lens came from

A reaction usually comes from three places at once: how you’re built (your wiring), what life taught you (your patterns), and what kind of day you’re having. Then is about the middle one, the patterns, because that is the layer that can soften.

Family, school, friends, culture and money at home all taught you what to expect. By the time you’re grown, your lens doesn’t feel like a lens. It feels like simply seeing. Some old answers, like “I’m only okay when I’m useful,” were learned as a child, without all the facts. You’re allowed to look again with the full picture. Looking again doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine.

  • Write the rulebook from home. Finish six lines the way your childhood home would have, such as “In my family, love looked like…” and “Resting was okay when….” Then ask of each one: do I still want this rule?
  • Try the one-minute lens check when a reaction feels bigger than the moment, once you’ve settled: Where did I learn this? How old does this feel? Is it true here, now? What would the adult me choose?

The past isn’t yours to change. What you carry forward is: which rules you keep, and how you talk to yourself about them. Go gently. You can read one part, stop, and come back another day.

When something happened matters as much as what happened. A move at age four, a parent’s illness at nine, or a family split at fifteen each shape different things, because each age is busy learning something different: whether people are reliable, whether you’re capable, who you are. You don’t need to remember it all. Noticing “this feels about eight years old” is often enough to loosen its grip.

After a parent’s funeral

A woman in her forties notices she’s doing every job for the funeral and the house clearance, and resenting her two brothers for it. Writing her rulebook, she finds the line “The eldest girl keeps everyone together.” Nobody ever said it, but everyone lived by it. She decides she can keep the caring and drop the rule, and asks each brother to own one job. See adult siblings and family rifts.

Words you could use (to yourself): “That’s an old rule, not a fact.” “I learned that when I was small. I can look again now.” “I can keep the love and change the rule.”

Try this: Write one rule from home on a card. Underneath, write “keep,” “change” or “not sure yet.” All three are fine answers.

An old rule can read other people for you. If silence at home meant anger, someone’s quiet evening can feel like trouble is coming. Ask before you decide what it means: “When you went quiet, what was going on for you?”

An expectation nobody said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only miss it. “If you loved me, you’d just know.” “Whoever notices, does it.” Each is somebody’s family rule, not a law of nature. When two people follow two rulebooks, each sure theirs is normal, resentment can grow without anyone doing anything wrong.

  • When you’re ready, and only then, swap rulebooks on a calm day, with curiosity and not correction.
  • Choose shared rules on purpose: who owns which job, how upset looks in each of you, and what helps. It isn’t about whose family did it right.
  • Say it lightly (“an old rule just went off”), never as a verdict (“you’re just like your mother”). Don’t guess at someone else’s history to explain them. Let them tell you.

How two rulebooks meet in different relationships:

  • A couple from two faiths or cultures: one family shows love by feeding everyone, the other by giving space. Neither is cold or pushy. Name both, then choose what your home does. See two faiths, one family.
  • Grandparents and grown-up children: the rules you raised your children by may not be the rules they choose for theirs. Asking “What are your rules for bedtime?” respects their home. See for grandparents and for grown-up children.
  • A foster or kinship child may carry rules like “adults leave” or “food runs out.” Their behavior can make sense once you see the rule. See foster and kinship families.
  • Roommates from different homes: one grew up with “guests are always welcome,” another with “ask first.” A two-minute talk in week one saves months of quiet annoyance. See sharing a room.

Words you could use: “I think my family’s rule just went off. Can I tell you what it was?” “What did this look like in your house growing up?” “Neither of our families was wrong. What do we want here?”

Part One, Now: your wiring, your weather and your words

Today is made of two layers. Your wiring is the lasting part: your temperament, your pace, your senses, how you take in words. You work with it, by building a setup that suits you. Today’s conditions pass: sleep, hunger, load, the room, the time of day. You work around them: eat first, sleep on it, talk tomorrow. Mixing the two up causes a lot of frustration.

Take a one-minute reading of your weather on ordinary days, so it feels familiar by the time you need it. A tank that has stayed full for a long time can start to feel normal, and “I’m fine” can come to mean “I stopped noticing.”

When a feeling is bigger than you expected, try four steps once the first wave has passed: name it, ask whether it’s the size of this moment or feels older, ask which layer it is, and ask what it needs (food, rest, space, a heads-up, or a real talk later). If words are hard to find, start with where you feel it in your body.

Being wired differently is not being wrong. Masking, or holding an old alarm down, takes real energy even when nobody sees it, and it counts as part of your load. You don’t need a label to use any of this. Your part is your words, your effort, your rest and your limits. Other people’s moods and choices are theirs.

A Monday at work

A man who is autistic, though few at work know it, gets home and can’t face talking for an hour. His partner used to read this as rejection. When he tracks it, he sees it’s every day after open-plan office days, and almost never after working from home. That’s wiring meeting conditions. He books a quiet room for two afternoons a week and tells his partner, “Give me an hour after office days. It isn’t about you.” See neurodivergent relationships.

Words you could use (to yourself): “Is this me, or is this today?” “This is a low-battery day, not a bad-person day.” “Holding it together all day counts as work.”

Try this: For three days, write one line each evening: “Today I was… because…” Look at the “because” part at the end. Is it mostly wiring, or mostly conditions?

Your needs and limits are information, not demands. Said kindly and early, they let people plan around you instead of guessing: “A heads-up helps me. It isn’t about you.” “I’m low today. Can we talk tomorrow?”

Share how you’re wired on a calm day, not in the middle of a hard talk. The style quiz in How you connect gives you a four-letter Wave Code, a fun description and not a test. The “How I like to be spoken to” card puts how you take in words on one page. Offer it as information, invite theirs, and treat the places you differ as the best spots for a short talk.

  • Explain without over-sharing: “This is how I’m wired, and here’s what helps.” “This is an old alarm. I’m working on it.” “Today’s a low-battery day.” You never owe anyone your history.
  • Never fill out a card or a code for someone else, and don’t label them. Ask “What was that like for you?” and let them answer.
  • Match energy with energy: warmth for warmth, effort for effort. If you keep giving far more than comes back, give a little less until it balances. Match care, never volume. In someone’s hard season, match what they can give right now.

Sharing how you’re wired works in very different relationships:

  • With a manager or coworker: “I take in instructions best in writing. Could you send a quick summary after our calls?” That’s a working style, not a confession. See at work.
  • With a teen: ask what helps them instead of guessing. “Do you want ideas or just someone to listen?” See teens.
  • With a partner who keeps different hours: “After a night shift I need quiet before I can talk.” See different hours.
  • With a friend you see less now: “I’m slow at texting, but I love it when you call.” See friends.

Words you could use: “I wrote this so you don’t have to guess. Would you read it when you have a minute?” “What helps you when you’re overloaded? I’d rather ask than assume.”

Part One, Next: who you are becoming

Start with what matters to you. Write two or three plain words, like calm, steady or close to my family. They are a direction, not a score.

  • Pick one small goal, said as something you will do: “Walk for ten minutes after dinner,” not “get healthier.”
  • When it slips, ask what’s in the way: your wiring, an old rule like “rest has to be earned,” or today’s conditions. Then change the arrangement, never yourself.
  • Make the step tiny and tie it to something you already do. Decide ahead with an if-then, and keep a smaller backup for the tired day.
  • Look back kindly once a month for ten minutes: What went well? What kept slipping? Was the gap in the plan, in how much I had, or just a one-off week? Don’t add up old misses.

A missed day is information about the plan, not about you. Rest is part of the plan, not a prize for finishing.

Knowing yourself is what makes a goal fit. Someone who is wired for mornings will keep a 6am habit that a night owl will drop by Wednesday. Someone carrying an old rule like “I have to do it perfectly or not at all” will do better with a goal that is deliberately small. The goal isn’t to become a different person. It’s to build a plan that suits the person you are.

The house goes quiet

After their youngest leaves for university, a mother writes “close to my family” and “curious” as what matters. Her goal: one new class this semester, and one call with each grown child a week, kept short so it doesn’t feel like checking up. See kids grown and gone.

Tell one person what you’re trying and what would help: “I’m trying to walk after dinner. Could you ask me about it on Sunday?” It’s information, not a demand. They can say no, and you don’t need their agreement to start.

  • Ask for what you want, not for what they did wrong.
  • Keep goals inside your own circle. “They’ll start helping” is a wish. “I’ll ask once, kindly, by Friday” is a plan.
  • If you’d like company, invite it: “I’m trying this. Would you try it with me?” Yes, not yet and no are all fair answers.
  • Spend more energy on the people who give back and less on what only takes. When someone close is in a rough season, you can choose to carry a little more for a while, and check at your monthly look-back that it’s still a choice.

Two people can grow in different directions at once, and that’s normal. A partner who retires while the other still works, or a couple where one is coming out, will each be changing. Name what each of you is working on, and ask how you can support each other without taking it over. See retired together and coming out as an adult.

Words you could use: “Here’s what I’m working on this month. What are you working on?” “I don’t need you to fix it. I’d just love you to ask.”

Chapter III: full tanks and leftover stress

If you’re already tired or stretched from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction. That’s human. The useful question is: how much of this feeling is about the thing in front of me, and how much did I bring in with me? “I’m upset about the dishes” and “I’m exhausted, and the dishes are what I noticed” are two different problems. Some leftovers are much older, which is where Part One’s Then helps.

Three everyday states are worth knowing: calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty. They are plain words for a best guess, not a medical reading.

A short stress check scores five things from 0 to 4: sleep, work elsewhere, any unsettled argument, how your body feels, and time pressure today. Add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is light, and 0.60 or more is high. A reading each week, not only on bad days, shows a baseline you may have got used to.

A full tank explains a reaction. It doesn’t excuse it. Fill it out about yourself only.

An example: a man who has just come home after six months working away is surprised by how irritated he is that the kitchen has been rearranged. When he checks, his tank is high: jet lag, a new routine, and feeling like a guest in his own home. The cupboards are just where the feeling landed. See coming home after time apart.

Words you could use (to yourself): “I’m upset, and I’m also exhausted. Which one is this?”

Each person scores only themselves. You can’t tell how full someone else’s tank is. Only they can say.

  • Before a hard talk, say your tank and your angle: “My stress tank’s about half full today, and I’m mostly seeing this from the time-pressure angle.” It works best before you start, not in the middle.
  • Two people can see the same moment from different angles, such as tiredness, history, the family you grew up in, or stress from outside. Ask which angle they’re seeing from instead of guessing.
  • If your reaction was bigger than the moment, a short repair helps: “Sorry, that was more than the question deserved. I’m carrying more than usual today.”
  • Never use “You’re obviously stressed” to brush off what someone said.

This works between partners, between a parent and a teen, among three partners, and between colleagues before a hard meeting. In a family that is caring for someone who is ill, each person’s tank may be full for different reasons, and saying so helps everyone be a little gentler.

Words you could use: “Before we start, I’m about three-quarters full today. How about you?”

Chapter I: your own pace and tone

Three things change how you come across, often without you noticing: your pace (how fast you talk and reply), your tone (whether an update sounds like a complaint) and your urgency (what “soon” means to you).

Stress changes all three. When you’re tense, you may talk faster and read an ordinary pause as a snub. When you’re tired and low, your replies get slower and shorter.

  • Learn your early signs of drifting: a tight chest, a clipped reply, wanting to win.
  • Learn how you take in words best, such as in writing or with time to think.
  • When you drift, step out, settle and come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission to retune yourself.

An example: a business owner realizes that her “quick question” messages at 9pm feel urgent to her staff, though she means them for the next morning. That’s her urgency setting, not theirs. She starts scheduling messages to arrive at 9am.

Two kind people can each say something reasonable and still clash, because their pace, tone or urgency doesn’t match. People who are close check their meaning less, because they assume they’re already understood. Nobody has to be wrong. It’s a tuning problem, not a moral failing.

  • Say how you take in words once, plainly: “I hear things better in writing.”
  • When someone says “when you get a chance,” kindly ask, “By when would be good?”
  • Notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. Retuning can start with one person: slow your own pace and lower your own voice.

Pace and urgency differences are common between a grandparent and a parent, between a gamer partner and a planner partner, and between business partners who work at different speeds. See gaming and time together.

Words you could use: “When I say ‘soon,’ I mean today. What does it mean for you?”

Knowing yourself through big changes

Big changes can shake your sense of who you are: a breakup, coming out, a new baby, retirement, children leaving home, an illness, or moving somewhere new. The same three layers still help. Your wiring mostly stays. Your patterns may be tested. Your conditions change a lot, often for months.

  • Expect your battery to run lower for a while, even when the change is a happy one.
  • Old rules often go off loudly in new situations: “I should cope alone,” “I mustn’t be a burden.” Notice them, and look again.
  • Write what matters to you again. It may have changed, and that’s allowed.

Pages for some of these: on my own, coming out as an adult, new baby, kids grown and gone, retired together and when one of you is ill.

When one person changes, everyone around them adjusts. A partner coming home from deployment, a sibling coming out, a parent who has just retired, a roommate whose hours have changed: each brings a new version of someone you thought you knew. Ask, don’t assume. “What’s different for you now?” is often the kindest question.

Words you could use: “I think I’ve been treating you like the old you. What’s different now?” “I’m still working out who I am in this. Can you be patient with me?”

Part Three: worth, minds and values

Part Three goes underneath who you are. Chapter 7 explains that worth isn’t a score you earn, how shame (“I am bad”) differs from guilt (“I did something bad”), and how a kinder inner voice helps you own a mistake instead of hiding from it. Chapter 9 explains minds that work differently, such as autism, ADHD and sensory differences, as differences, not failures. Chapter 10 helps you find your values and use them as a compass, not a test.

An example: you forget a friend’s birthday and the voice in your head says “you’re a terrible friend.” Chapter 7 suggests a different sentence: “I forgot, and I care. I’ll send a message today.” It’s just as honest, with less shame and something to do.

Thoughts you could try: “I made a mistake. I am not a mistake.” “What would I say to a friend who did this?”

Knowing yourself makes it easier to tell others what helps. Chapter 9 explains the double empathy idea: when two people with different kinds of minds misread each other, the gap runs both ways, so both can meet in the middle. Chapter 10 helps two people with different values see what each one is protecting, before deciding anything.

An example: one partner needs quiet after work, and the other wants to talk right away. Neither is being rude. Naming it as a difference in wiring turns a nightly hurt into a plan.

Words you could use: “This isn’t about you. My brain needs a quiet start. Can we talk at seven?”
