Part Two: Between us · Chapter V

The monthly look-back: a calm relationship check-in

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Once a month, look back for the problems that keep coming back, and fix the setup instead of having the same argument again.

The third Tuesday in a row

The bins again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth you had last Tuesday, word for word. You know your lines. So do they.

Or you could notice that this is the third Tuesday running, and wonder what it is about Tuesdays.

  1. Weekly check-ins miss patterns.

    Four flatmates had an overflowing bathroom bin every week, and every week it seemed like bad luck, until they looked at the month together and saw it had happened every single week since September. One missed job looks like bad luck. The same job missed week after week is a pattern, and patterns are easiest to see when you look at a whole month at once.

    In my old job the step that got skipped more than any other was going back afterwards to check that the fix had actually held, and skipping it is exactly how the same problem turns up again three years later. This chapter is that step, made small. A careful, friendly glance over the month, closer to a self-check than an inspection. Choose a monthly date now, the last Sunday, say, and put it in everyone’s calendar.

    Dig deeper: why once a month

  2. You need four weeks of notes and one list.

    Bring the month’s four weekly notes from Who did what, where a few lines a week is enough, and your job list from One owner per job. Twenty minutes is usually plenty. If either of your stress tanks is high that day, at 0.60 or more on How much are you carrying?, move it a day or two.

    Kat and Nathan work different shifts and keep their weekly notes in one shared note on their phones, mostly typed at red lights. They do the look-back on the one evening a month they’re both off, with a takeaway from the Nepalese place. It has become, slightly to their embarrassment, the evening of the month they look forward to most.

    Dig deeper: getting ready

  3. Mark an ✗ for each week a job slipped.

    A job slipped if it was noticed and not done, only done after being raised more than once, or quietly covered by someone who isn’t its owner. Look closely at every job with two or more ✗, because three or four in a month is a real pattern.

    Two foster carers found the reading log had been missed three weeks out of four. Both of them had an explanation ready. They marked the ✗ in pencil first, together, before either of them said anything at all, which is much harder than it sounds and much the best order. Then the explanations, which turned out to be the same one.

    Dig deeper: gathering the month’s notes

  4. A problem that keeps coming back usually has no real owner.

    That kind of problem gets patched every week and never fixed. The owner is the person who does the job from start to finish, so the fix is to give the job one owner whose name sticks, or to talk honestly about whether anyone has the time and energy for it.

    In a house with three partners, “whoever’s free” did the online grocery order. It was late three times this month. With nobody owning it, everybody assumed someone else had it in hand. One Friday there was no milk at all in the house, and four jars of mustard.

    Dig deeper: spotting the gaps that keep reopening

  5. Sort each repeat into one of three kinds.

    If a job has no clear owner, update the job list. If its owner can’t keep up, have a kind, honest talk. If there was a short-term cause, like a bout of flu, note it and move on.

    The homework folder kept coming back unsigned. Two separated parents looked at it at the look-back and went through the three kinds, out loud, quite carefully, because last time they’d argued. Was nobody sure which home signs it? Was one of them swamped? Or was it just the week she had flu? It was the first one. It had been the first one since September (see Co-parenting).

    Dig deeper: sorting each gap

  6. Count the gaps each month.

    Write down how many gaps of each kind you found. If the number of jobs with no clear owner falls over several months, the setup is working, and a single month can be a normal wobble. If it keeps going up, something bigger has changed and it’s worth a talk. Two business partners write their monthly count on a whiteboard in the back office, next to the fire-drill notice. In March four jobs had no clear owner. By June, one did, and they argue quite cheerfully about whose fault that one is.

    Dig deeper: keeping count

  7. Look back even when nothing is slipping.

    A month with no gaps is good news. It’s also the month most likely to go unchecked. When something has worked for a while, people stop looking, and nothing breaks on the day that happens. Keep the look-back going in quiet months and add one question, “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?”, which is how a look-back guards against complacency.

    The children have left home. Nothing whatsoever is slipping. The look-back takes eleven minutes. At the end Dave says, quietly, looking at his tea, that they don’t really talk at dinner any more. Jan had noticed it too. No list would have shown it.

    Dig deeper: the quiet months

  8. It’s not a review of either person.

    You don’t add up old hours or punish old mistakes. You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled. The only question is where the setup isn’t working yet and what you’ll try next month. After a month in hospital, Yusuf came home to a long list of what had slipped while he was away. His partner had been dreading showing it to him. He read it, and then he said thank you for the bits that hadn’t slipped, which was most of them, and they set up the month ahead. That was all.

    Dig deeper: what the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

  9. Keep it short and kind.

    Start with something that went well and talk about the job instead of the person. If a gap needs a proper talk, set it for another day using Check-ins. If a talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and whoever paused names a time to come back. A mum and two teenagers hold their look-back over pizza on the last Friday. Each says one good thing first (the younger one’s is always “the pizza”). One gap gets a new owner. It’s over in fifteen minutes and nobody has stormed off, which in that house is a result.

    Dig deeper: how to talk about a pattern

Your part, and only your part. In the look-back you can only fix the gaps you own, and offer help with the rest. You can’t make someone keep a rhythm. Keep the rhythms you can keep, notice which ones are kept in return, and let your effort follow: more where it’s shared, less where it isn’t, so the rhythm doesn’t run on one person’s energy. The last Sunday of the month

Two mugs of tea. Four weeks of notes. Twenty minutes, roughly. You go down the list together. Most weeks were fine. Two jobs keep coming up, the recycling and the call to the landlord about the window that won’t shut. You give each of them a name. You finish your tea while it’s still warm, which hasn’t happened on a Sunday in months.

A look-back, three months in

Sam and Alex’s bins slipped three weeks running because Alex works late on Tuesdays. After the trial swap they make it official, so Sam owns the bins and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, which keeps the hours even. The second month is clean. In the third, the grocery order slips twice, both times in a week when Alex’s mum was in hospital, so they mark it as a one-off and leave the list alone. What actually changes in month three is that Alex mentions, almost in passing, while stacking the plates, that he has stopped enjoying their Saturday mornings. No list had shown that. They agree to talk about it properly on Thursday, and Sam spends most of the week worrying about it.

You could say “I don’t think anyone dropped this. I think nobody ever picked it up.”

Common mix-ups

This isn’t where you settle who owes what. Past hours are never billed to anyone, though you can still say thank you for them. You only fix the setup going forward. A slipped job doesn’t mean its owner failed, either, because the gap might be in the list, or in the owner’s capacity, or in one strange week. And you can’t skip the weekly notes and just do this. The look-back only works if the weekly notes feed it.

A note on safety: These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If looking back together ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A look-back is something you both choose. If one person demands logs of the other’s every hour, checks their phone without agreement, or uses the ✗ marks to punish, that is control, not a look-back, and it is not your fault. See Not safe at home? and normal limits and control.

Starting out: before you move in

If you are new together and about to share a home, the tools work best now, before habits set, at about one step a week. In the first week, each of you writes down the rulebook from the home you grew up in and then you swap, with curiosity. In the second, talk about money, starting with your money stories and moving on to the split (Chapter IV). In the third, write your first job list, with one owner per job and the thinking jobs included (One owner per job), and in the fourth, make a pause plan on a calm day. In the fifth, each name three things that matter most to you about a home (Part Three, Chapter 10). After the move, log one ordinary week once two weeks have passed, and do your first short look-back after four. None of it is a test. The point is that neither of you has to guess.

Niamh and Tariq, eight months together, find that their families meant very different things by “tidy”. They agree on the kitchen every night and the rest on Sunday, and on Tariq’s mother never being told about the rest.

Dig deeper: the plan, week by week

Raising a grandchild (kinship care)

Many grandparents and other relatives raise a child whose parents can’t, and much of it involves things that keep coming back, such as cancelled calls and the hard evenings after contact. A monthly look-back helps. Some repeats you can own. Some, like a parent who misses calls, you can’t, and for those you can own the plan for the evening after. The child may love both the parent who can’t look after them and you, so tell them they don’t have to choose, and don’t run the parent down in front of them. Explain an absent parent in short, true words that fit their age, always with the message that it isn’t their fault. When the parent is your own grown-up child and things are hard between you, keep messages about the child short and civil for the child’s sake, and leave the door open. Look after yourself too. Ask for respite, and find other kinship carers. In England and Wales, the charity Kinship (kinship.org.uk) can help.

Maureen, who is sixty-three, is raising her grandson Tyler, who is nine. Her look-back shows that his mum’s Sunday call fell through three times this month. She can’t fix that. So she plans those evenings instead, with hot chocolate and his football annual, and asks her brother to take Tyler swimming on Sunday afternoons, so there’s something to look forward to that doesn’t depend on a phone ringing.

Words you could use, with the child “Mum loves you. She isn’t well enough to look after you right now, and that’s not your fault.”

On your own: a monthly look at yourself

Once a month, look back at your own month kindly, more like a weather report than a scorecard. What filled your battery and what drained it? Which of your limits held and which slipped? Choose one small thing to keep and one to change. Rhiannon lives on her own after a divorce, and her look-back showed the same ✗ three weeks running. She’d skipped her Thursday swim to answer work emails, every week, telling herself it was just this once. So she moved the emails to Friday morning, and kept the swim (see On my own).

To begin, put a relaxed twenty-minute date in the calendar once a month and keep your weekly notes somewhere you can both find them. At the look-back, start by naming one thing that went well, and end by writing down how many gaps of each kind you found, so that over the next few months you can see whether the number is falling. What keeps coming back? is the workpaper for that conversation.
