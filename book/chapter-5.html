The monthly look-back: a calm relationship check-in

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

Once a month, look back for the problems that keep coming back, and fix the setup instead of having the same argument again.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load. What are the Five Pillars?

The third Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth about Tuesday. Or you could notice that Tuesday has now happened three times, and ask what it is about Tuesdays.

  1. Weekly check-ins miss patterns.

    One missed job looks like bad luck. The same job missed week after week is a pattern, and it’s easiest to see when you look at the whole month. The “audit” here is a careful, friendly look back, more like a self-check than an inspection.

    Dig deeper: why once a month

  2. You need four weeks of notes and one list.

    Bring the month’s four weekly notes from WP-01 (the “who did what” log; a few lines a week is enough) and your job list from WP-03 (one owner per job). That’s all. Twenty minutes is usually enough. If either of your stress tanks is high that day (0.60 or more on How much are you carrying? (WP-02)), move it a day or two.

    Dig deeper: getting ready

  3. Mark an ✗ for each week a job slipped.

    A job “slipped” if it was noticed but not done, only done after being raised more than once, or quietly covered by someone who isn’t its owner. Look closely at every job with two or more ✗. Three or four in a month is a real pattern, not just bad luck.

    Dig deeper: gathering the month’s notes

  4. A problem that keeps coming back usually has no real owner.

    It gets patched every week and never fixed. The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The fix is to give the job one owner whose name sticks, or to talk honestly about whether anyone has the time and energy for it.

    Dig deeper: spotting the gaps that keep reopening

  5. Sort each repeat into one of three kinds.

    No clear owner: update the job list. An owner who can’t keep up: have a kind, honest talk. A short-term cause, like illness or travel: note it and move on.

    Dig deeper: sorting each gap

  6. Count the gaps each month.

    Write down how many gaps of each kind you found. If the number of jobs with no clear owner falls over several months, the setup is working; one month can be a normal swing. If it keeps rising, something bigger has changed, and it’s worth a talk.

    Dig deeper: keeping count

  7. Look back even when nothing is slipping.

    A month with no gaps is good news, and it’s also the month most likely to go unchecked. When something has worked for a while, people stop looking, and nothing breaks on the day that happens. Keep the look-back going on quiet months, and add one question: “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?” That is how a look-back guards against complacency.

    Dig deeper: the quiet months

  8. It’s not a review of either person.

    You don’t add up old hours or punish old mistakes. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past. The only question is where the setup isn’t working yet, and what you’ll try next month.

    Dig deeper: what the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

  9. Keep it short and kind.

    Start with something that went well. Talk about the job, not the person. If a gap needs a proper talk, set it for another day using Check-ins. If a talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.

    Dig deeper: how to talk about a pattern

Each idea in everyday life

The cards above say what each idea means. Here each one gets a real-life example, words you could say, and one small step.

1. Weekly check-ins miss patterns

For example: Four flatmates. Each week the bathroom bin overflowed, and each week it looked like bad luck. Looking at the whole month showed it happened every single week.

Words you could use: “Can we look at the whole month, not just this week?”

Try this: Pick a monthly date now, like the last Sunday, and put it in both calendars.

2. Four weeks of notes and one list

For example: A couple on different shifts keep their weekly notes in one shared note on their phones. They do the look-back on the one evening they’re both off. See Different hours.

Words you could use: “I’m running on empty today. Can we move the look-back to Thursday?”

Try this: Choose one place where both of you can find the weekly notes.

3. Mark an ✗ when a job slipped

For example: Two foster carers look back. The reading log was missed three weeks out of four. Three ✗ means it’s a pattern, not a bad week. See Foster and kinship families.

Words you could use: “This isn’t about who. It’s just which jobs slipped.”

Try this: Mark the ✗ in pencil, together, before anyone explains anything.

4. A repeat usually has no real owner

For example: Three partners share a home. “Whoever’s free” does the grocery order, and it was late three times. Nobody owned it, so everybody assumed. See More than two.

Words you could use: “Whose name is next to this one? If nobody’s, shall we pick?”

Try this: For each job with two or more ✗, check that one name is written next to it.

5. Sort each repeat

For example: Separated parents look at the missed homework folder. Was nobody sure which home signs it (no owner)? Is one parent swamped (can’t keep up)? Or was it the week she had flu (a one-off)? See Co-parenting.

Words you could use: “Is this a list problem, a too-much problem, or just a bad week?”

Try this: Write one of the three kinds next to every repeat before you decide what to do.

6. Count the gaps

For example: Two business partners write their monthly count on a whiteboard in the back office. In March, four jobs had no clear owner. By June, one did.

Words you could use: “Let’s just write the numbers down and see how they look in three months.”

Try this: Write this month’s three numbers on the back page of your notes.

7. Look back on quiet months too

For example: The kids have left home and nothing is slipping. At the look-back, one of them says, “We don’t really talk at dinner anymore.” Nothing was broken. Something was drifting. See Kids grown and gone.

Words you could use: “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?”

Try this: Keep the date on a quiet month, and ask just that one question.

8. Not a review of either person

For example: After a month in hospital, one partner comes home to a long list of what slipped. They thank each other for what each carried instead, and fix only next month. See When one of you is ill.

Words you could use: “I see how much you carried. Let’s just set up next month.”

Try this: If you notice yourself adding up past hours, stop and say one thank-you instead.

9. Short and kind

For example: A mum and two teens hold their look-back over pizza. Each says one good thing first. One gap gets a new owner. It’s over in fifteen minutes. See Teens.

Words you could use: “This needs a proper talk. Can we do it Thursday, not now?”

Try this: Set a timer for twenty minutes, and stop when it rings.

Your part, and only your part. In the look-back you can only fix the gaps you own, and offer help with the rest. You can’t make someone keep a rhythm. Keep the rhythms you can keep, notice which ones are kept in return, and let your effort follow: more where it’s shared, less where it isn’t, so the rhythm doesn’t run on one person’s energy. The last Sunday of the month

Two mugs of tea, four weeks of notes and twenty minutes. You go down the list. Most weeks went fine. Two jobs keep coming up. That’s the whole meeting, and it’s enough.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Look at your own routines as a setup you can adjust. Spot the easy defaults, like “I’ll just do it,” that let a gap quietly reopen.
  • Between you and others: Look at the month together, give repeating gaps a clear owner, and keep count so jobs don’t drift back to whoever notices first.

In real life

  • Partners: Sam and Alex’s trash slipped three weeks in a row, because Alex works late on Tuesdays. After a trial swap, they make it official: Sam owns the trash, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, so the hours stay even.
  • Family: Three siblings keep forgetting to call their dad’s landlord about repairs. It turns out nobody ever agreed who would.
  • Roommates: The recycling overflows every other week. Everyone thought someone else was taking it down.
  • Coworkers: Meeting notes go missing twice a month. Giving note-taking a named owner fixes it by the next month.
  • Long distance: A couple in two cities do the look-back by video. Their repeat is “who calls first,” so they give it an owner per weekday. See Long distance.
  • Friends: Two friends notice their monthly dinner has been cancelled three times. Nobody owned booking it. See Friends.
  • Grandparents and parents: School pick-ups by grandma slipped twice, both on days she wasn’t told about. The gap was the message, not grandma. See Grandparents.

Words you could use: “I don’t think anyone dropped this. I think nobody ever picked it up.”

Common mix-ups

  • “This is where we settle who owes what.” Past hours are never billed to anyone, though you can still say thank you for them. You only fix the setup going forward.
  • “If a job slipped, the owner failed.” The gap might be in the list, in the owner’s capacity, or just a one-off week.
  • “One bad week means we should change everything.” Don’t make a lasting change because of a short-term cause.
  • “We can skip the weekly checks and just do this.” The monthly look-back only works if the weekly notes feed it.
  • “More gaps this month means we’re failing.” One month can be a normal swing, or it can mean life changed. That’s a reason to talk, not to worry.
  • “The look-back is for the person who keeps the notes.” It works best when both bring their notes, and both talk.

A note on safety: These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A look-back is something you both choose. If one person demands logs of the other’s every hour, checks their phone, or uses the ✗ marks to punish, that is control, not a look-back, and it is not your fault. See Not safe at home? and normal limits and control.

On your own: a monthly look at yourself

Once a month, look back at your own month, kindly. Not a scorecard, more like a weather report.

  • What filled your battery this month, and what drained it?
  • Which of your limits held, and which slipped?
  • Choose one small thing to keep, and one to change next month.

For example: Rosa lives on her own after a divorce. Her monthly look-back shows the same ✗ three weeks running: she skipped her Thursday swim to answer work emails. She moves the emails, not the swim. See On my own.

Words you could use (to yourself): “Not a scorecard. Just this month’s weather.”

Try this: On the last day of this month, write three lines: one thing that filled you, one that drained you, one thing to change.
