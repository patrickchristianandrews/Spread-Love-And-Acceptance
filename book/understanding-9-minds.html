Part Three · Chapter 9

Minds that work differently

Paperback textRead the expanded version

How autistic, ADHD, sensory and learning differences work, why none of them is a character flaw, and how people with different minds can meet in the middle.

In the paperbacks: Book 1, Know Yourself Kindly, Chapter 7.

At a glance

  • Brains vary, like bodies do. A different mind is not a broken one, though differences can still be disabling in a world built for one kind of mind.
  • Nobody chooses overload. Meltdowns, shutdowns and burnout are what happens when a nervous system has had too much.
  • Less masking, room to stim, time for deep interests and real rest help a great deal.
  • Meet in the middle, out loud. Ask what helps, say what helps you, and use the words each person chooses.

Monday, 8:15am

“Shoes, please, just put them on,” you say, for the third time.

Your son is lying on the hall floor with his hands over his ears. The new socks are wrong. From where you’re standing, they are socks. From where he is, the seam across his toes is sandpaper, and the alarm clock upstairs that nobody has switched off is going straight through the middle of his head. You are already late. So is he, in a way you can’t see.

This chapter is for neurodivergent people, diagnosed or not, and for the people who live and work with them. It can’t diagnose anyone, and only you get to choose the words you use for yourself.

Two tools elsewhere rest on it. The “How I like to be spoken to” card (Part One, Now) works because different minds need different input, and saying things plainly helps almost everyone. One owner per job and the written job list (Part Two, Chapter II) move memory out of anyone’s head and onto paper, which helps ADHD and autistic minds most and everybody a bit.

In quick strokes, a child on the hall floor with his hands over his ears is three things at once. On the surface, a fuss about socks. Underneath, a sensory system turned up high. Underneath that, a morning with no room left in it. The roots are state and setting, and quiet incentives, what the setup rewards. The chapter then takes the ways minds differ one at a time.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    Brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with people. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what gets treated as typical. A different mind isn’t a broken one. Differences can still be disabling, though, especially in a world built for one kind of mind, and that is just as true.

    Gary found out he was dyslexic at forty, and he sat in the car outside the assessment center for twenty minutes afterward with the report on the passenger seat, not reading it, obviously. What he mostly felt was relief. For thirty years he had half believed the teachers who wrote “lazy” in red pen.

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally. Many have deep, focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely than others do, and find predictability calming. Autism doesn’t run along one line from “mild” to “severe.” Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change from day to day, and the misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    The story that got lost in the middle In an experiment, a story was passed along chains of eight people. All-autistic chains kept as much detail as all-non-autistic ones, while mixed chains lost detail faster and reported less rapport, which supports the idea that the difficulty sits between two styles rather than inside one of them.

    Crompton and colleagues (2020) · Moderate: a careful experiment; modest sample, one task

    “Your slides have three typos,” Kate told her colleague, kindly. “One on slide four and two on the summary.” It was exactly what she would have wanted to be told. He went quiet for the rest of the afternoon and she had no idea why until a mutual friend explained it to her at a bar. They were using two different sets of rules for what kindness sounds like, and neither set is wrong, though it would help if they came with subtitles. If you’re the direct one, it can help to say so up front, something like “I like it when people say exactly what they mean, so you can be direct with me.”

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    Many people with ADHD can focus very hard on things that interest them, and the hard bit is steering and holding attention when a task doesn’t. Boring tasks feel like pushing a car uphill, interesting ones swallow whole evenings, and time is slippery (there’s “now” and there’s “not now” and not much in between). Feelings often arrive fast and big, which is common in ADHD, though not everyone has it. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself. Effort and caring don’t come into it.

    Saffy forgot her best friend’s birthday. Completely. Not a text. Then she spent six hours making a card with pressed flowers from the yard, glued on one at a time with tweezers, and posted it four days late with a note that said “I KNOW.” The forgetting and the six hours came out of the same brain. If something keeps falling out of yours, move it into the world. A labeled alarm. A note on the door. A basket by the front door for the keys.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise, or a label in a sweater. Others need more input, like movement or strong flavors or firm pressure. Overload is a body at its limit, which is a very different thing from being fussy.

    Two roommates argued about the kitchen radio, because one found silence creepy and the other found the radio at breakfast physically painful. They settled on the radio until nine and headphones after, which has lasted two years.

    Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you and one that calms you, and change one small thing today.

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, and none of them measures how clever someone is. Plenty of people have a “spiky” profile, very strong in some areas and struggling in others. The right tools, audiobooks or extra time on tests or a reader for the long letters, can level things a good deal.

    Every Wednesday afternoon on the field, fourteen-year-old Ewan is called clumsy, usually by a gym teacher who should know better. The same semester he wrote and directed the school play, which had a revolving set made out of a bike wheel. Ask the people you live or work with how they find it easiest to take things in, and then actually use it.

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences. They copy other people, force eye contact, hold back movements, rehearse small talk in the car. This is called masking. It can get someone through a day. It also uses a great deal of energy, and over years it can wear a person down into exhaustion and burnout. For a long time the cost can be invisible even to the person paying it, which is how a mask works.

    Style, not substance Across three studies, non-autistic people formed poorer first impressions of autistic people from a few seconds of video and were less keen to talk to them. Given only a transcript of what was said, the bias disappeared. That goes a long way to explaining why people mask.

    Sasson and colleagues (2017) · Moderate: three studies with consistent results

    Mia seems completely fine at college, chatty in the cafeteria and helpful in class and laughing in all the right places. She gets home at four fifteen, goes straight up to her room, and can’t say a word to anyone for an hour. Her dad used to knock and ask what was wrong, and now he leaves a cup of tea outside the door and goes away.

    Notice one place where you could drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and it often lasts months. Rest helps, and so do fewer demands and less masking. Pushing harder usually makes it worse. If you think you might be there, list three demands you could drop or hand to someone else for a month.

    Words you could use “I’m in burnout. What I need for a while is fewer demands.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much, and it can look like crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version: going still, losing speech, not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload.

    For a lot of people words go first. What helps is less of everything. Less noise, fewer words, no questions, more space. Talking can wait, sometimes until the next day. On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Ways to say it (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds. Rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing, fiddling with something. Most people stim a little. (Watch any meeting and count the jiggling feet.) For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping. It’s worth noticing what your own hands do when you’re stressed or focused or happy, because that’s yours, and nobody needs to train it out of you.

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds go deep into one thing at a time, often autistic and ADHD ones. That focus can be a real strength, because it builds expertise and brings a great deal of joy, and it also makes switching hard. Being interrupted can feel like being yanked out of water.

    “Dinner!” Nothing. “Tilly, dinner!” Still nothing. Third time, louder. The fish sticks are going cold and Dominic is getting really frustrated. His daughter is upstairs building a castle and has reached a delicate stage with the drawbridge. The next night he tries a ten-minute warning, then a five. She comes down on time, still talking about the drawbridge, and tells him at length what the problem with it is. He doesn’t entirely follow, but he eats his fish sticks while they’re still warm, which is the main thing. The warning, for the record, goes “Dinner in ten minutes, I’ll come and get you in five minutes,” shouted up the stairs, and it works about four nights out of five.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are often a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by their special interests reported higher well-being, though very intense engagement went with lower well-being.

    Interests and well-being In a large Dutch survey of autistic adults, about two in three had a special interest, and being more motivated by it went with higher well-being. Very intense engagement went the other way. The joy is real, and so, some weeks, is the need for an off switch.

    Grove and colleagues (2018) · Moderate: a large survey, self-report, at one point in time

    Ask someone about their favorite subject and let them talk for five minutes without steering. It costs you five minutes. It’s one of the warmest things you can do for anyone.

  12. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, plain words help everyone. Say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. And changes made for one person (a quiet space, an agenda, flexible hours) usually help plenty of other people too.

    A team started sending meeting agendas a day early, for one autistic colleague. Within a month most of them said meetings had gotten shorter. Two people who had never said a word in a meeting started saying things.

    Today, swap one “how I work best” fact with someone. Tell them one thing that helps you and ask for one that helps them. The website can bend a bit to your mind too, with Read your way (bigger text, calmer pages, fewer surprises).

    Dig deeper: adjustments that help everyone

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, and sometimes support and a community too. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person” and others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is to ask, use the words the person chooses, and never try to diagnose somebody else, least of all in an argument.

    The fight was about the dishes. It was going badly. “You’re so autistic,” Danny said, and the conversation ended right there, as it deserved to. In the morning, over toast that neither of them was eating, he said sorry, properly, and then asked what his partner actually needed from him when the kitchen was a mess. It was the conversation they should have had the night before.

    If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed and pick one small next step, like talking it over with someone you trust.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis, identity and words

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they’re autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties or fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed the way they understood their own lives. Relief and grief and something like joy can all turn up together, on the same afternoon. If that’s you, look for one autistic-led group or online space, and give yourself time.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis later in life

  15. Getting things started and finished is a skill of its own.

    Planning, starting, switching, remembering what you were doing and keeping track of time are called executive function. Many autistic and ADHD people find some of these harder, and harder on some days than others. A review of 235 studies by Demetriou and colleagues (2018) found differences in executive function in autism on average, with a lot of variation between people. The brain’s self-management system is working differently. Laziness doesn’t come into it.

    Siân wrote a forty-page report for her board in a week, and it was excellent. In those same weeks, she could not mail one package. It sat on the hall table while she walked past it forty times. Then she put it by the front door with a yellow sticky note on top (“MAIL ME”) and set an alarm for Tuesday at 8:10, and on Tuesday at 8:11 it went. See routines that help and Wired differently.

    Dig deeper: executive function in daily life

  16. When demands feel like a threat.

    Some people, often autistic, feel strong anxiety when asked or expected to do things, even things they want to do. Avoiding the demand can look like refusing, joking, changing the subject or a meltdown. The profile was first described as “pathological demand avoidance,” or PDA, by Newson and colleagues (2003).

    It isn’t in the main diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it. A review by Kildahl and colleagues (2021) found the evidence limited and mostly based on parent reports. Many people who identify with it prefer the phrase “persistent drive for autonomy.” Honestly, I’d hold this one loosely until the research settles. What helps tends to be the same whatever it’s called. Less pressure. Requests that feel like invitations. “Put your coat on now” gets a flat no from Reuben every morning. “It’s cold out. Coat or sweater, your choice?” gets the coat, usually.

    Dig deeper: demand avoidance, carefully

  17. Not recognizing faces isn’t rudeness.

    Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, means finding it hard to recognize faces, sometimes even close friends seen out of context. It can be something you’re born with. Many autistic people also find remembering faces harder, as a review by Weigelt and colleagues (2012) found. People who don’t recognize you may be relying on your voice, your hair, your coat or where they usually see you.

    Clive walked straight past his neighbor in the supermarket without a flicker, and she went home feeling snubbed and told her sister about it on the phone. When he explained, a week later and very embarrassed, that he hadn’t known her without her spaniel, she laughed. Now she says “Hi Clive, it’s Jo from number twelve” every time, even when the spaniel is right there.

    Words to try “I’m bad with faces, so please say who you are when you say hi.” (to someone else) “Hi, it’s Jo from next door.”

    Dig deeper: face blindness

  18. Not speaking isn’t the same as having nothing to say.

    Some autistic people speak rarely, sometimes or not at all, and some lose speech when stressed. Many communicate through AAC (augmentative and alternative communication), which can mean typing, letter boards, picture cards, apps or sign. Using AAC doesn’t stop speech developing: a review by Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) found no sign that it held speech back (and some that it may help), though most of the studies were small. The safest place to start is to presume competence, which means assuming the person understands and has something to say, and giving them the time and the tools to say it.

    At Sunday lunch at her grandma’s, fifteen-year-old Ruby types on her tablet. Her uncle used to talk about her across the table as if she wasn’t there (“Does she want gravy?”). Now he asks her, and waits, a bit longer than he’d like, while she types. Her answer was “Yes. Obviously.” Wait longer than feels natural, which will feel very long and isn’t.

    Dig deeper: non-speaking people and AAC

  19. Telling friends and dates is your choice.

    Whether to tell someone you’re autistic or ADHD, and when, is up to you. Telling can bring understanding. In a study by Sasson and Morrison (2019), people formed better first impressions of autistic adults when they knew they were autistic, especially if they knew more about autism.

    People don’t always respond well, though, and interviews by Au-Yeung and colleagues (2025) describe autistic adults weighing hope for support against fear of not being believed. Many share a little at a time, starting with what helps and leaving the label for later. Second date, outside a bar with a line. “I’m autistic,” Nina said, “so I’m quite direct, and I’d rather walk than sit in there.” Her date looked at the bar, then the line, then back at her. “Oh, thank God,” he said. They walked along the canal as far as the lock and back.

    Words you could use “I’m autistic. For me that means…” “I won’t always pick up hints, so please just tell me.” (if someone shares with you) “Thanks for telling me. What helps?”

    Dig deeper: disclosure in friendships and dating

  20. Finding your people.

    Lots of autistic people describe meeting other autistic people as a turning point, because at last they don’t have to translate or explain or mask. One survey (Cooper, Smith and Russell, 2017) found that autistic people who felt more connected to other autistic people had higher self-esteem and, through that, less anxiety and depression. It shows a link, not proof of cause. Being autistic can be an identity and a community as well as a diagnosis.

    After his diagnosis at fifty-three, Bernard joined an online group of autistic adults who love old maps, specifically the USGS kind with hand-drawn hachures. The first evening he talked to them for three hours about contour lines. When he logged off he had more energy than when he’d started, which hadn’t happened after a conversation in years.

    Dig deeper: autistic community and identity

Your part, and only your part. You can’t change how your brain, or anyone else’s, is wired. You can learn how yours works and say what helps, and ask others what helps them. Meeting in the middle means both people move a little. If you’re always the one adjusting, it’s fair to say so. More in Your wiring, weather and words

You stop asking. You sit down on the hall floor next to him, which is cold, and say, “Socks feel wrong?” He nods without opening his eyes. You go upstairs, switch the alarm clock off, dig the old seamless pair out of the laundry basket. Two minutes later his shoes are on. Two ways to read this

Headphones at dinner, two ways

Some brains filter background sound without trying, so a busy kitchen is just company. Others take in every clatter, voice and hum at once, and filtering it by hand uses up the attention they wanted for the people. If you filter easily, headphones at the table can look like shutting everyone out, and for the other person they’re how they stay at the table at all. If you hear everything, it may help to say so: “The headphones mean I can stay. Talk to me and I’ll take one out.”

Where the fields meet. Holistic therapies start with the body before the argument, which fits overload well: less noise, fewer words, settle first and talk later. Some of these practices are well studied and some are debated, so only the plain everyday parts are used here. The root underneath is quiet incentives, because a setup that only rewards masking will get a lot of masking.

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing how your mind works helps you build a life that fits it, and spot burnout before it’s on top of you (see Get to know your wiring). Partners can swap “how I work best” notes about plans, messages, touch and time alone. With children, behavior very often has a sensory or overload reason, so “What’s too much right now?” is worth asking before “Why are you doing this?” A friend who cancels at the last minute may just be out of energy, and offering something easier, like a walk, keeps the door open. At work, written agendas, clear deadlines and quiet spaces help far more people than the ones they were made for.

If you have ADHD and share a home, make the chore tools lighter. Log three days instead of seven, with a tally on the fridge. Own a few jobs completely, each with a labeled alarm, instead of helping a bit with everything. Forgetting is a setup problem to fix together. More in the expanded version.

Common mix-ups

“Everyone’s a bit autistic” is probably the commonest, and it makes real needs sound small. Everyone shares some traits, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life. ADHD doesn’t just mean not sitting still. Many people with it, girls and women especially, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside. “They seem fine to me” overlooks how much masking can hide, and seeming fine can cost someone their whole evening. Adjustments aren’t special treatment; they remove a barrier, the way glasses do. And the idea that autistic people lack empathy is too simple. Many feel other people’s feelings very strongly, and the differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

Believe them about what’s hard and what helps, even when it’s different for you. Be clear, which means saying what you mean, giving times and writing down anything important. Ask what helps, because every neurodivergent person is different and their answer will beat any list, this one included. Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places matter more than they look. And enjoy their interests with them, if you can.

Ways to say it “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”
