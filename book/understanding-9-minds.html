Minds that work differently

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

How autistic, ADHD, sensory and learning differences work, why they are differences and not character flaws, and how people with different minds can meet in the middle.

Monday, 8:15am

“Can you just put your shoes on?” For the third time. Your child is lying on the hall floor, hands over his ears, because the new socks “feel wrong.” You’re late. He’s not being difficult. To him, the sock seam feels like sandpaper, and the alarm clock is still ringing in his head.

This chapter is for anyone: people who are autistic, have ADHD, dyslexia or other differences (whether diagnosed or not), and the people who live, work and learn with them. It explains how these minds work first, then what helps. It can’t tell you whether you or anyone else has a diagnosis. Only a qualified professional can do that, and only you get to decide what labels you use for yourself.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    People’s brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with others. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what is treated as typical. Different isn’t broken. And at the same time, differences can be disabling, especially when the world is built for one kind of mind.

    For example: A man who struggled all through school finds out at 40 that he is dyslexic. He’s relieved: he was never lazy or stupid. His brain just processes written words differently.

    Thoughts you could try: “Maybe this isn’t a flaw. Maybe it’s a difference that needs a different setup.”

    Try this: Think of one thing that’s hard for you that’s easy for others. Ask: is it effort, or is it a different kind of brain?

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally, have deep and focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely, and find predictability calming. Autism isn’t a line from “mild” to “severe.” Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change day to day. Misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    For example: An autistic coworker says, “Your slides have three typos.” She means it as help. Her colleague hears criticism. Neither is wrong; they’re using different rules for what kind means.

    Words you could use: “I like it when people say exactly what they mean. You can be direct with me.” “Do you want my honest view, or just support right now?”

    Try this: In one conversation today, say exactly what you mean, with no hints. Notice whether it makes things easier.

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD often have plenty of attention, but find it hard to point it where they choose. Boring tasks can feel impossible; interesting ones can swallow hours. Time can feel slippery (“now” and “not now”), and feelings can come fast and big. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself, not a lack of effort or care.

    For example: A woman with ADHD forgets her friend’s birthday, then spends six hours making her an amazing handmade card. The forgetting and the focus come from the same brain.

    Words you could use: “I care about this. My brain drops things. A reminder really helps, and it doesn’t annoy me.”

    Try this: Move one thing you keep forgetting out of your head and into the world: an alarm, a note on the door, or a basket by the exit.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise or scratchy clothes. Others need more input: movement, strong flavors, pressure. Overload isn’t being fussy. It’s a body at its limit.

    For example: Two housemates argue about the kitchen radio. One finds silence eerie; the other finds the radio painful. A rule helps: radio on mornings, headphones in the evening.

    Words you could use: “It’s not you. The noise in here is too much for me right now. Can we move somewhere quieter?”

    Try this: Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you, and one that calms you. Make one small change today.

    Dig deeper: senses, overload and needs

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, not a measure of how clever someone is. Many people have a “spiky” profile: very strong in some areas, struggling in others. The right tools, like audiobooks, spoken notes or extra time, can level the ground.

    For example: A teenager with dyspraxia is called clumsy at football. In drama, he writes and directs the school play. Same boy, different task.

    Words you could use: “I learn better when I can listen than when I read. Could you send a voice note instead?”

    Try this: Ask someone you live or work with: “What’s the easiest way for you to take in information?” Then use it.

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences: copying others, forcing eye contact, holding back movements, rehearsing small talk. This is called masking. It can help someone get through a day, but it uses a lot of energy, and over years it can lead to exhaustion, anxiety and burnout.

    For example: A student seems fine all day at college, then comes home and can’t speak for an hour. She’s not being rude. She’s recovering from a day of performing.

    Words you could use: “I need some quiet time after work. It’s how I recharge, not a sign I’m upset with you.”

    Try this: Notice one place where you can drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds, often autistic and ADHD ones, focus deeply on one thing at a time. That focus can be a gift: real expertise, flow and joy. It also makes switching tasks hard, so being interrupted can feel like being pulled out of water. Warnings before a change, and routines, help a lot.

    For example: A dad calls his daughter to dinner three times and gets more frustrated each time. Then he tries a ten-minute warning, and a five. She comes to the table calm.

    Words you could use: “Dinner is in ten minutes. I’ll come and get you at five to.” “Can you give me a warning before we switch?”

    Try this: Before you interrupt someone who’s focused, give a heads-up and a time. See if it goes more smoothly.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  8. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, explicit words help everyone: say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. Changes made for one person, like quiet spaces, written agendas or flexible hours, often help many others too.

    For example: A team starts sending meeting agendas a day early. It was for one autistic colleague. Soon everyone says meetings are shorter and better.

    Words you could use: “What would make this easier for you?” “Here’s what helps me. What helps you?”

    Try this: Swap one “how I work best” fact with someone today: one thing that helps you, and ask for one that helps them.

    Dig deeper: adjustments that help everyone

  9. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, support and community. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is simple: ask, and use the words the person chooses. And never try to diagnose someone else, especially in an argument.

    For example: During a row, a partner says, “You’re so autistic.” It lands as an insult and ends the talk. Later, he apologizes and asks what his partner actually needs.

    Words you could use: “How do you like to describe yourself?” “I’m wondering if I might be neurodivergent. I’d like to find out more.”

    Try this: If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed, and decide one small next step: reading, talking to someone, or asking a doctor.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis, identity and words

Your part, and only your part. You can’t change how your brain, or anyone else’s, is wired. You can learn how yours works, say what helps, and ask others what helps them. Meeting in the middle means both people move a little. If you’re always the one adjusting, it’s fair to say so. More in Part One: who you are today

Common mix-ups

  • “Everyone’s a bit autistic.” Everyone has some traits in common, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life. The phrase can make real needs sound small.
  • “ADHD just means you can’t sit still.” Many people with ADHD, especially girls and women, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside.
  • “They can focus on games, so they could focus on homework.” Interest and challenge switch attention on. That isn’t a choice in the way it looks.
  • “They seem fine to me.” Masking can hide a lot. Seeming fine isn’t the same as being fine.
  • “Adjustments are special treatment.” They remove barriers so people can do what’s expected. Glasses aren’t special treatment either.
  • “Autistic people lack empathy.” Many feel others’ feelings strongly. Differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Believe them about what’s hard, what hurts and what helps, even if it’s different for you.
  • Be clear. Say what you mean, give times, and write down anything important.
  • Ask, don’t assume. Every neurodivergent person is different. “What helps you?” beats any list.
  • Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places aren’t extras.
  • Enjoy their interests. Listening to someone talk about what they love is one of the warmest things you can do.

Words you could use: “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you, your child or someone you care for has ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, and you wonder if a difference might be part of it, a doctor, school special educational needs staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so it can help to start early and to write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or find a helpline at findahelpline.com.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? has free, private help.
