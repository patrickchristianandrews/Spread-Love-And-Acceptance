Part Three · Chapter 14

Personality, temperament and change across life

Paperback textRead the expanded version

What personality is, where temperament comes in, how people change across their lives, and how to live and work with people who are made differently from you, without putting anyone in a box.

In the paperbacks: Book 1, Know Yourself Kindly, Chapter 6.

At a glance

  • Traits work like sliders. Everyone sits somewhere along each one, and most people are near the middle.
  • Temperament is a starting point. Babies already differ in how active, cautious or easily upset they are, and what happens next matters too.
  • People keep changing. On average people grow steadier and more organized as they get older, and you can change on purpose, slowly.
  • Most clashes between personalities are about fit, and nobody has to be at fault.

Saturday, 3:00pm

Your aunt’s seventieth, in the party room above the golf club. You’re standing by the cake, minding your own business, when she tells the whole room, “Oh, she was always the shy one!”

Everybody laughs, and you laugh along with them, and something stings.

You’re forty-two. Every weekday at 8:40 you stand in front of thirty third graders and get them to sit down, which takes some doing. So are you still the shy one? Were you ever?

We describe each other by personality all the time, as laid-back, or a worrier, or the organized one, and descriptions like those have a way of setting hard. This chapter is about how personality actually works, what stays put and what moves. Two tools elsewhere build on it. The “How I like to be spoken to” card and the style quiz (Part One, Now) describe how you lean on a few sliders, which is more use than knowing which box somebody once put you in. And One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) arranges the jobs around people’s differences, so nobody has to turn into someone else to share a house.

Quickly, one “difficult” colleague has three layers. On the surface, behavior you don’t like. Underneath, a trait, a slider set differently from yours. Underneath that, a situation that suits one setting and not the other. The roots are limited capacity and ownership and structure. Slowly now, slider by slider.

  1. Traits work like sliders.

    Personality is the usual pattern in how you think and feel and act. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”: openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organized and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are).

    The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them well. Each runs from low to high, and most people sit near the middle of most of them. That’s the bit online quizzes leave out. Two friends did one on the train to Santa Cruz and were told, very confidently, that one was an introvert and the other an extravert. Then they looked at the actual numbers. Four points apart, both right in the middle. They spent the rest of the journey arguing about which of them was more middle.

    If you’re curious, mark where you think you sit on each slider from one to ten, and ask a close friend where they’d put you. The gap is often the interesting bit.

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality you can see very early on. Some babies are on the go from the moment they wake. Some are calm. Some take to a new routine right away and some need a week. Some flinch at every loud noise. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences and the idea of “goodness of fit,” meaning how well a child’s temperament suits what the people and places around them expect.

    Sensitive isn’t the same as shy Elaine and Arthur Aron described a trait they called sensory-processing sensitivity, taking things in deeply, and found it was partly separate from introversion. Researchers still argue about its exact shape. Still, the child hanging back may be taking everything in rather than shrinking from it.

    Aron and Aron (1997) · Moderate: a widely used measure; the trait’s structure is still debated

    Isla is two and cried at every new preschool group. Her mom tried coaxing her into the circle, then bribing her, then (once, quietly) begging. In the end she just sat at the edge of the church basement with Isla on her lap, feeling a bit silly, while everyone else did “The Wheels on the Bus.” It smelled of orange drink and floor wax. Week three, Isla climbed down on her own, walked to the sand table, and didn’t look back. Her mom, slightly put out, had a cookie.

    You could say “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made.”

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality describes a pattern, and patterns have exceptions. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, endlessly patient with toddlers and very short with call centers. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across lots of situations and a good deal less about the one in front of you.

    Anita had decided her new colleague was cold. Polite, competent, cold. Then came the fundraiser soccer game, and there he was in a borrowed bib two sizes too small, shouting encouragement at a goalkeeper who was letting in everything. On Monday Anita sat looking around the open-plan office with its gray partitions and humming lights and wondered how much of what she’d seen was the room.

    Dig deeper: person and situation

  4. Introvert and extravert is about energy, not liking people.

    People who are more introverted often like people a lot. What they find is that busy, noisy company uses energy up, and they need quiet afterward to get it back. People who are more extraverted tend to be topped up by company. Most of us are a mix (sometimes called an ambivert), and every point on the slider just needs different things to be at its best.

    Happy now, tired later Forty-eight people tracked for twelve days felt better while acting outgoing and more tired about three hours later, introverts and extraverts alike. It’s a small study, and the authors say so.

    Leikas and Ilmarinen (2017) · Early: a small study (48 people); the authors asked for it to be repeated

    Eleven o’clock at a wedding in a barn outside Asheville. Fraser wants the after-party in the local bar. Luke wants bed and silence and possibly never to hear “Mr. Brightside” again. They’ve had this exact standoff at four previous weddings. This time Fraser goes for an hour and Luke walks back to the hotel along the lane with his shoes in his hand, and they meet at breakfast, one of them hoarse and the other one smug, both of them happy. What Luke said, for once, was “I loved tonight, and I’m out of social energy,” which he’d practiced on the walk from the car.

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable. It also moves. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organized, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness went on growing right across the lifespan. Big life events play a part, and so do the roles people take on and who they love.

    At school Tunde lost his keys about once every two weeks and once turned up to a math exam on the wrong day. He’s a charge nurse now, forty-six, the calm one people look for when it goes wrong at three in the morning. At the reunion his old friends kept staring at him from across the banquet room, and eventually one of them came over with two beers and said, “What happened to you?” He thought about it. “Life kept asking me to cope,” he said, “and eventually I learned how.”

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    If you want to change a trait you can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways over and over. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended to move in the direction they wanted over sixteen weeks. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially toward more emotional steadiness.

    Noor wanted to be less quiet at work. She made a rule. One thing in every team meeting, even if it was only “I agree with Neil.” The first two weeks her heart thudded so hard she was sure people could see it through her sweater. By the end of the second month it had become ordinary, and somewhere in the third month she forgot it had ever been a rule. Pick one small thing that fits who you’d like to be more of, and do it three times this week. Chapter 6 has more on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    There’s no good personality. Every trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are lovely to be around and may find “no” almost impossible to say. Very conscientious people are the ones you’d leave your keys with, and they can’t always stop. People high in openness love anything new and can be bored to tears by routine.

    For most of a year Alys was irritated by her roommate. His sneakers in the hall. His “it’ll be fine.” Then the water heater died on the coldest night in January, and while Alys stood in the kitchen in her coat reading the manual, he called around until he found a plumber who’d come out at ten o’clock, and then he heated soup on the stove and passed around every blanket in the apartment. “It’ll be fine,” he said, and it was, apart from the pot that boiled over.

    A one-page card, “How I like to be spoken to,” turns a trait people complain about into plain information about what helps.

    Professor Puddles

    Every trait has gifts and costs. Writing down what helps you on a one-page card turns a “flaw” into useful information.

  8. Differences are usually about fit.

    When two people with different personalities share a home or a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one can both be right in their own way, and so can a planner and someone spontaneous, or a talker and someone who needs quiet. What helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it, without either of you being the problem. Personality is also different from neurodivergence and from mental health conditions. If something feels bigger than personality, Chapter 9 may help.

    A couple argues about every single vacation, because one wants every hour planned and the other wants to wander about and see what happens. This year they plan the mornings and leave the afternoons free, and they still argue about the mornings. I’d count that as progress.

    Dig deeper: fit, not fault

Your part, and only your part. You can get to know your own mix, look after what you need, and practice one small change if you want to. You can’t change someone else’s personality, and you don’t have to change yours to be loved. If you’re always the one bending to fit, it’s fair to say so. More in Know your own wiring, free online.

Saturday, 5:30pm

People are drifting off with slices of cake in paper towels. You think about sitting down next to your aunt and telling her about the thirty third graders. You don’t. You help stack the plates. On the drive home you’re still turning it over, half annoyed with her and half with yourself, and somewhere around the beltway you start drafting what you’ll say at Christmas if she does it again. It’s quite a good line.

Two ways to read this

The same lunch, two ways

For some people a new lunch is a small pleasure, and the same sandwich every day sounds dull. For others every new food is a small gamble on taste, texture and how it sits, and a known lunch is one decision they never have to make. If you like variety, the same-lunch person isn’t stuck. They’re saving their energy for the rest of the day. If you like sameness, the variety person isn’t restless. Trying something new is part of what makes lunch a break for them.

Where the fields meet. Economics treats attention as a budget, and personality is partly about where the budget goes and how fast it runs out. Business has a related habit: match each job to the person whose strengths suit it, and give it one owner, so nobody has to pretend to be someone else to get it done.

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you build a life that suits you, with about the right amount of company and quiet. “I’m just not a people person” might be true. It might also be an old label that deserves testing at least once. With a partner, a shared plan (one does the bills, the other does vacations) usually goes better than trying to change each other. A quiet friend who hardly texts may still care a great deal, so ask what kind of contact suits them. A child’s temperament says nothing about good or bad parenting, and adjusting to fit them is a skill you pick up. And family roles like “the clever one” or “the difficult one” can hang on long after they stop fitting. You’re allowed to grow out of them, whether or not your aunt has noticed.

Narcissistic, toxic and other labels people reach for.

Words from clinics have drifted into everyday arguments. Narcissistic personality disorder is a clinical diagnosis, and a diagnosis is a complicated judgment that a short chapter, a test or a stranger on the internet can’t make. It describes a long-lasting pattern across most of a person’s life, not a bad week or a bad breakup. “Toxic” isn’t a clinical word at all.

For something to be a personality pattern of this kind, these are usually all present:

  • it shows up across many years and most settings, at home, at work and with friends,
  • it includes a constant need for admiration and very little room for other people’s feelings,
  • it doesn’t change when the person is calm, rested and happy,
  • it causes real, lasting harm to the person or to the people around them.

Not enough on its own:

  • confidence, ambition or self-promotion at work,
  • one selfish choice, or a defensive reaction to criticism,
  • talking about yourself a lot when you’re nervous,
  • someone you’ve fallen out with.

Describing what happened helps more than a label. “You talked over me three times tonight” can be answered. A diagnosis thrown at someone in an argument usually ends the conversation, and it’s often wrong. “Toxic” works the same way. It names no specific behavior and no effect over time, and a hard week, a single conflict or a bad day isn’t enough to earn it.

Common mix-ups

You aren’t simply an introvert or an extravert. Most people are somewhere in the middle, and where you sit shifts with the setting and your mood. Personality isn’t set in stone by thirty, either. It keeps moving slowly all through life. Online type quizzes are good for fun and for starting a conversation, and that includes the website’s own Wave Code, which shows four leanings on four sliders, but they sort people into a handful of types when real people are spread along each slider. “They’re just difficult” often means “they’re different from me,” and it can hide stress or illness or a need nobody has asked about. And practicing something new isn’t being fake. It feels odd at first, like new shoes, and then it’s yours.

If you want to help someone else with this

Drop the old labels, and notice if you still describe someone the way they were twenty years ago. Ask instead of guessing. “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” will tell you more than any theory. Say it when you see someone grow, because “You handled that so calmly” can show a person a change they hadn’t spotted. And describe what happened, not who someone is. “You’re so uptight” shuts doors that a plain description would leave open.

Words to try “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits anymore.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”
