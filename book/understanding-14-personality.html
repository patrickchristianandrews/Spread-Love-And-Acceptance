Personality, temperament and change across life

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What personality is, where temperament comes in, how people change across their lives, and how to live and work with people who are made differently from you, without putting anyone in a box.

In short

  • Traits work like dials. Everyone sits somewhere along each one, and most people are near the middle.
  • Temperament is a starting point. Babies already differ in how active, cautious or easily upset they are, and what happens next matters too.
  • People keep changing. On average people grow steadier and more organised as they get older, and you can change on purpose, slowly.
  • Most clashes between personalities are about fit, and nobody has to be at fault.

Saturday, 3:00pm

It’s your aunt’s seventieth in the function room above the cricket club, and you’re standing quietly by the cake when she announces, loud enough for the whole room, that you were always the shy one. Everyone laughs. You smile along while something stings. You’re forty-two. You stand in front of thirty children every weekday morning at twenty to nine. So are you still the shy one? Were you ever?

We describe each other by personality all the time (laid-back, a worrier, the organised one) and descriptions like those have a way of hardening into boxes. This chapter is about how personality actually works, and what stays put and what moves. Two tools elsewhere build on it: the “How to talk to me” card and the style quiz (Part One, Now) describe how you lean on a few dials, which is more use than knowing which box someone put you in, and One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) arranges the jobs around people’s differences so nobody has to become someone else to share a home.

  1. Traits work like dials.

    Personality is your usual pattern of thinking, feeling and acting. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”: openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organised and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are).

    The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them well. Each one runs from low to high, and most people sit somewhere near the middle of most of them. That’s the bit the online quizzes tend to leave out. Two friends do one on a train and get told, with great confidence, that one is an introvert and the other an extravert; when they look at the actual scores, they’re four points apart and both near the middle. If you’re curious, mark where you think you sit on each dial from one to ten, then ask where a close friend would put you. The gap is often the interesting part.

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality you can see from very early on. Some babies are very active and some are calm. Some settle easily into new routines and some take their time. Some react strongly to noise or change. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences and the idea of “goodness of fit”, meaning how well a child’s temperament fits what the people and places around them expect of it.

    A toddler who is slow to warm up cries at every new playgroup. Her mum stops trying to coax her into the circle and lets her watch from her lap for ten minutes instead, a bit awkwardly, in a church hall that smells of squash. By the third week the little girl climbs down on her own and goes to the sand table.

    You could say “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made.”

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality describes a pattern, and patterns have exceptions. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, organised in their job and chaotic at home, endlessly patient with toddlers and very short with call centres. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across lots of situations. It tells you much less about this one.

    A manager decides that a new colleague is cold. Then at the team’s charity football match she watches the same colleague in a borrowed bib, chatty and funny and shouting encouragement at the goalkeeper, and on Monday she sits looking at the open-plan office wondering how much of what she’d seen was the room. Before you decide what someone is “like”, try to see them somewhere else, or at least ask them about somewhere else.

    Dig deeper: person and situation

  4. Introvert and extravert is about energy, not liking people.

    People who are more introverted often like people very much. What they find is that busy, noisy social time uses up energy, and they need quiet afterwards to get it back. People who are more extraverted tend to be topped up by company and busy places. Most of us are a mix, sometimes called an ambivert, and every point on the dial just needs different things to feel at its best.

    At about eleven at a wedding in a barn near Hexham, one partner wants to go on to the after-party in the village pub and the other wants bed and silence. So one goes for an hour and the other walks back to the hotel, and they meet at breakfast, one of them a bit hoarse, both of them happy.

    Words you could use “I loved tonight, and I’m out of social energy.” “Shall we go together, and I’ll head home at ten?”

    Dig deeper: introversion and extraversion

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable. It also moves. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organised, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness went on growing right across the lifespan. Big life events, roles and relationships all play a part.

    A man who was a nervous, disorganised student, the one who lost his keys every fortnight, is a calm, steady nurse in his forties, and his old school friends barely recognise him at the reunion. When they ask, he says that life kept asking him to cope and he slowly learned how. Write down one way you’ve changed in the last ten years, and what helped it happen.

    Thoughts you could try “Who I was at fifteen isn’t who I have to be now.” “People I knew years ago may have changed too.”

    Dig deeper: how personality changes with age

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    If you want to change a trait, you can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways again and again. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended to move in the direction they wanted over sixteen weeks. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially towards more emotional steadiness.

    A woman who wants to be less shy at work makes one small plan: say one thing in each team meeting, even if it’s only agreeing with someone. For the first fortnight her heart pounds every time. After two months speaking up has become ordinary, and she has started to forget it was ever a plan. Pick one small action that fits who you’d like to be more of, like replying to messages the same day or saying hello first, and do it three times this week. Chapter 6 has more on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    There’s no good personality. Each trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are kind and easy to be with, and may find no almost impossible to say. Very conscientious people are the ones you’d trust with your keys, and they may struggle to rest. People high in openness love new things and can be bored rigid by routine.

    A very organised woman spends most of a year irritated by her relaxed housemate, his trainers in the hall and his “it’ll be fine”. Then the boiler packs in on the coldest night in January, and he’s the one who stays calm, finds an emergency plumber at ten o’clock and makes everyone hot water bottles from the kettle. Pick one trait of yours that people sometimes complain about, and write down a time it helped.

    Things to tell yourself “What is the gift in this trait, and what is the cost?” “What they do that annoys me might be the flip side of something I value.”

    Dig deeper: gifts and costs

  8. Differences are usually about fit.

    When two people with different personalities share a home or a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one can both be right in their own way, and so can a planner and someone spontaneous, or a talker and someone who needs quiet. What helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it, without either of you being the problem. Personality is also different from neurodivergence and from mental health conditions; if something feels bigger than personality, Chapter 9 may help.

    A couple argue about every single holiday, because one wants every hour planned and the other wants to wander about and see what happens. This year they plan the mornings and leave the afternoons free. They still argue about the mornings. I’d count that as progress.

    Ways to say it “I think we’re just made differently here. What would work for both of us?” “I’m a planner and you’re not. Can we plan half and leave half?”

    Dig deeper: fit, not fault

Your part, and only your part. You can get to know your own mix, look after what you need, and practise one small change if you want to. You can’t change someone else’s personality, and you don’t have to change yours to be loved. If you’re always the one bending to fit, it’s fair to say so. Know your own wiring

Saturday, 5:30pm

As people start to drift off with foil-wrapped slices of cake, you think about sitting down next to your aunt and telling her about the thirty children. In the end you help clear the plates and say nothing. On the drive home you’re still turning it over, half annoyed with her and half with yourself, and you wonder whether she’ll say it again at Christmas, and what you would like to say back if she does.

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you build a life that suits you, with the right amount of company and quiet, routine and novelty. “I’m just not a people person” might be true, or it might be an old label worth testing at least once. With a partner, a shared plan (one of you does the bills, the other the holidays) usually works better than trying to change each other. A quiet friend who hardly texts may still care a great deal, so ask what kind of contact suits them. A child’s temperament says nothing about good or bad parenting, and adjusting to fit them is a skill, not a defeat. And family roles like “the clever one” or “the difficult one” can stick long after they stop fitting. You’re allowed to grow out of them, even if your aunt hasn’t noticed.

Common mix-ups

You aren’t simply an introvert or an extravert. Most people are somewhere in the middle, and where you sit can shift with the setting and your mood. Personality isn’t set in stone by adulthood either; it keeps moving slowly across the whole of life. Online type quizzes are good for fun and conversation, and that includes the website’s own Wave Code, which shows four leanings on four dials, but they sort people into a handful of types when real people are spread along each dial. “They’re just difficult” often means “they’re different from me”, and it can hide stress or illness or a need nobody has asked about. And practising something new isn’t being fake. It feels unnatural at first, the way new shoes do, and becomes part of you over time.

If you want to help someone else with this

Drop the old labels. Notice if you still describe someone the way they were years ago, and look for who they are now. Ask instead of guessing: “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” will tell you more than any theory about their personality. Notice growth out loud, because “You handled that so calmly” can show someone a change they hadn’t spotted in themselves. And describe the action rather than the person. “You’re so uptight” or “You’re just lazy” closes doors that a plain description of what happened would leave open.

Words to try “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits any more.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”

When to get more help

Personality differences are normal and don’t need fixing. It may be worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or psychologist if:

  • a pattern in how you feel, think or relate to people keeps causing you or others real distress, across many parts of life;
  • you feel very unsure who you are, or your moods swing so much it’s hard to cope;
  • worry, low mood or fear of people is stopping you from living the life you want.

Please don’t diagnose yourself or anyone else with a “personality disorder” from what you read online. Those are clinical terms that only a qualified professional can use, after a proper assessment.

A personality trait never excuses controlling, frightening or hurtful behaviour. If you’re afraid of someone close to you, Not safe at home? lists free, private help.

This chapter is general education, not a diagnosis or therapy.
