Habits and change: why change is hard and how small steps work

Paperback textRead the expanded version

Why it’s so hard to change, even when we really want to, and what actually helps: small steps, a kinder setup, patience with slips, and remembering that other people’s change belongs to them.

In the paperbacks: Book 2, Heal and Steady, Chapter 6.

At a glance

  • Change is hard for good reasons. Habits save effort, so the old way is always the easy way.
  • A small step and a kind setup outlast willpower. Make the first step tiny and change what is around you.
  • Slips are part of change. A missed day tells you something about the plan and nothing about your character.
  • Other people’s change is theirs. You can support it, though you can’t force it.

Monday, 7:05am

The plan was a walk before work. The sneakers are by the door. Somehow the phone is in your hand instead, and at 7:40 you are watching a man in Finland restore a rusty ax, next to a coffee that went cold twenty minutes ago. You don’t even like axes.

By the evening a quiet voice is telling you that you never stick to anything. What you actually ran into this morning was a habit with about six years of practice behind it. It won, as it usually does for the first few rounds.

This chapter is why several tools in these books are so small. One small step a week (Part One, Next) starts smaller than feels useful and decides ahead of time. The setup score and One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) change the setup instead of asking anyone for more willpower. And What keeps coming back? (Part Two, Chapter V) treats a repeat as information about the plan, because slips are an ordinary part of changing anything.

Quickly: a phone in your hand at 7:40 is the surface. Underneath, a habit with years of practice behind it. Underneath that, a setup that quietly makes the old way easier. The roots are feedback loops and quiet incentives. Then the chapter slows down and takes them one by one.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    A lot of what we do each day runs on autopilot. That saves energy, and it’s the whole point of a habit. The catch is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one, so changing asks your brain to do something effortful again and again, for weeks, until the new way becomes the easy one.

    Every night Jodie decides she will not snap at the children in the morning, and she means it. Every morning, somewhere between the lost reading book and the toast going cold and Lily’s hair, she snaps. The morning has worn a groove deeper than her good intentions, which says something about mornings and not much about her as a mother.

    Pick one thing you’d like to change, and write down when it usually happens, where, and what’s going on just before.

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger, which might be a time or a place or a feeling or a person, or simply the thing you’ve just done. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward (relief, comfort, a small buzz) keeps it going. Once you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    Stale popcorn In one field experiment, people with a strong movie-popcorn habit ate stale popcorn about as much as fresh, but only in a movie theater. Somewhere else, they didn’t. The setting drove the habit more than taste or good intentions did, rather like Nigel’s laptop.

    Neal and colleagues (2011) · Moderate: two field experiments, in line with wider habit research

    Nigel noticed, eventually, that he reached for the cookie jar the moment he opened his laptop after dinner, hungry or not, every night, and the laptop was the cue. So now he makes tea before he opens it and makes a pot. By the time the tea’s brewed the urge has mostly wandered off. On Fridays it doesn’t wander anywhere and he has a cookie, and that seems fair.

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring and easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and they work precisely because they’re easy enough to manage on a bad day. Tie the new step to something you already do (“After I make my coffee, I’ll…”), and only grow it once it’s stuck.

    Two roommates had been promising each other a big Sunday clean of the kitchen since March. It had happened once, so they agreed something smaller. After dinner, each of them wipes one surface, which takes two minutes. By the second week the stove had stopped being a source of tension. By the fourth, someone had descaled the kettle without being asked, which caused a small sensation.

    You could say “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I …, I will …”

    Dig deeper: why small steps work

  4. Change the setup, not just your willpower.

    Willpower runs low when you’re tired or hungry. Your surroundings stay the same all day. So make the new habit easier, by putting it in sight or getting things ready the night before, and make the old one a little harder, by moving it out of reach. I’d back a small change to the setup over a big push of effort almost every time. At home that can be as simple as one owner for each job.

    Willpower isn’t a fuel tank, probably A popular idea says self-control runs out like fuel. When 23 labs tried to repeat the classic experiment together, they found an effect close to zero. The argument isn’t over, but it’s one more reason to change the setup rather than lean harder on yourself.

    Hagger and colleagues (2016) · Strong: a large pre-registered replication; the debate continues

    Callan is seventeen and wants to sleep better, and for weeks he tried to stop scrolling in bed by sheer force of will. It didn’t work once. Then his mom suggested charging his phone in the kitchen overnight, next to the bread box. He said this was ridiculous, and did it anyway, mainly to prove her wrong, and within a week he was asleep before midnight most nights. Every few weeks he tells her it’s ridiculous, without much conviction.

  5. Being in two minds is normal.

    Part of you wants to change and part of you doesn’t, and the reluctant part usually has its reasons. The old habit may give comfort, or protect you from something. Pushing harder against it rarely works, and listening to it often does.

    Asif wanted to drink less, and he also loved Friday nights at the bar with his team. When he finally asked himself what Friday actually gave him, the answer wasn’t really the beer. It was the people, the laughing, the end of the week. So he kept the Friday and switched to alcohol-free after the first beer. If anyone at the table noticed, they didn’t say, which is its own sort of kindness.

    Try two lists, one of what you like about how things are, one of what you’d like about changing. You’re allowed both, and I suspect the first one will be the more honest of the two.

    Dig deeper: readiness and mixed feelings

  6. Slips are part of change.

    Nearly everyone who changes something slips back sometimes, and one slip doesn’t undo the progress. What does the damage is usually the thought that follows. “I’ve blown it, so why bother?” Being kind to yourself after a slip makes it a good deal more likely you’ll start again.

    There’s a name for “why bother?” Relapse-prevention research calls it the abstinence violation effect: after one slip, guilt and a sense of total failure make a full slide back more likely. It comes from addiction treatment, but the shape is the same for cookies and evening runs.

    Marlatt and Gordon (1985) · Moderate: a foundational clinical model, widely used in addiction treatment

    Three calm weeks, and then Lewis shouted at his son over spelling homework. Really shouted, the old way. That night he sat on the end of the bed in the dark. “I shouted, and I’m sorry. I’m working on it.” A small voice from under the comforter said “Okay.” The next evening they did the spelling homework at the kitchen table with the radio on. Nobody raised their voice. The word was “necessary,” which neither of them could spell.

    Things to tell yourself “A slip is one moment out of many.” “What made today harder, and what can I plan for next time?”

    Decide now, while things are going well, what you’ll do after a slip. Have one kind sentence to say to yourself and one small step to start again. A game can help some people practice, and the Re-check Drive turns small kind steps into calm football.

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

    Change made only to please someone, or out of shame, is hard to keep up. Change that connects to what you care about has deeper roots, and each small action becomes a vote for the person you’re turning into.

    Rania kept meaning to be better at keeping in touch, and kept not being. What shifted it was a different sentence in her head. She stopped telling herself she must message people and started thinking of herself as someone who shows up for her friends. A voice note on Sunday evenings became part of that. Some weeks it’s thirty seconds of nonsense and some weeks it turns into an hour on the phone.

    Dig deeper: values and who you’re becoming

  8. You can support change, but not force it.

    It’s natural to want the people we love to change, to drink less, or look after themselves better. Their change belongs to them, though, and pressure tends to make people dig in. It helps more to say clearly what you need, ask what would help, and notice their small steps out loud.

    Grant kept missing deadlines, and his teammate had the lecture about being organized all ready, she’d been polishing it for two weeks, and at the last minute she didn’t give it. She asked what would make the handover easier, and he looked surprised. Between them they set up a shared checklist, and he fills it in every Friday before lunch, mostly. She has kept the lecture, though, in a note on her phone.

    Ways to say it “I can’t change this for you, but I’d love to help if you want me to.” “I noticed you did that this week. That really helped.”

Two weeks later, 7:05am

The sneakers are still by the door. The phone sleeps in the kitchen now, and the plan has shrunk to shoes on, out to the end of the road. Some mornings that’s all. Most mornings it turns into a loop past the community gardens and back. On the Thursday you miss, the voice starts up again, and you tell it you’ll get to the end of the road tomorrow. It says you won’t. You do.

Two ways to read this

Change, two ways

Some people love a fresh start and throw themselves at a new plan. Others need to change one small thing and see it hold first. If you’re a fresh-starter, the slow one isn’t lacking willpower. They’re protecting what already works. If you’re slow and steady, the fresh-starter isn’t being reckless. Novelty is part of what keeps them going.

Where the fields meet. Behavioral science says habits beat good intentions, and business adds the simple check: one owner for a change and one small look each week at whether it held. The root is feedback loops, small things repeated with a check each time.

In everyday life

Take a couple who want to spend less of the evening on their phones. Earlier attempts failed because each of them watched the other. This time they put a basket by the door, both phones go in at nine, and there’s one exception for whoever’s on call for an elderly parent. The first week is awkward. On the Wednesday one of them sneaks a look at the football. By the third week the basket is part of the furniture and the evenings have a shape again, a movie or a crossword or an early night.

Big life changes, a move or a new baby, break old routines, which is disruptive and also a good moment to start new habits. If you have ADHD, make the cue impossible to miss, do the step the moment it appears, and make the reward come right away. Expect the novelty to wear off, and swap the method when it does instead of blaming yourself. More in the expanded version.

On your own: a kind plan for change

If you’re working on something alone, the whole chapter fits into one small plan. Choose one change, the one that would make the most difference or the one that feels most doable. Give it a cue, written as “After I ___, I will ___.” Make one tweak to your surroundings so it’s easier to start. Decide on the smallest version you could still manage on a tired day. And write down one kind sentence to use after a slip. Your self-discovery path and Know yourself, next walk through it step by step.

Common mix-ups

“If I really wanted it, I’d just do it” is the cruelest of these, and it’s wrong. Wanting is only the start. Habits, setup, tiredness and stress all get a vote. Then there’s the famous 21 days. There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range. Being hard on yourself doesn’t keep you on track, either. Harshness makes people give up, and kindness makes it easier to try again. And routines don’t have to mean being rigid. For a lot of people, autistic people especially, routines are what make life manageable, and change can respect that.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you advise (“Would ideas help, or would you rather just talk it through?”), and help them find their own reasons by asking what would be different for them if this changed. Practical help with the setup, like a ride to the gym, often does more than encouragement. Notice small steps out loud and be kind about slips. Look after yourself too. You can care a great deal and still have limits on what you’ll put up with.

Words to try “I’m on your side with this. What would help?” “You’ve done this three days in a row. That’s not nothing.”
