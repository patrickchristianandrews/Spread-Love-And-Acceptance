Habits and change: why change is hard and how small steps work

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why it’s so hard to change, even when we really want to, and what actually helps: small steps, a kinder setup, patience with slips, and remembering that other people’s change belongs to them.

In short

  • Change is hard for good reasons. Habits save effort, so the old way is always the easy way.
  • A small step and a kind setup outlast willpower. Make the first step tiny and change what is around you.
  • Slips are part of change. A missed day tells you something about the plan and nothing about your character.
  • Other people’s change is theirs. You can support it, though you can’t force it.

Monday, 7:05am

The plan was a walk before work. The trainers are by the door. Somehow the phone is in your hand instead, and at 7:40 you’re still scrolling, past a video of a man restoring a rusty axe, next to a cup of coffee that went cold twenty minutes ago. By evening a quiet voice is telling you that you never stick to anything. What you actually ran into this morning was a habit with years of practice behind it, and it won.

This chapter is the reason several tools in these books are so small. One small step a week (Part One, Next) starts smaller than feels useful and decides ahead of time. The setup score and One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) change the setup rather than asking anyone for more willpower. And What keeps coming back? (Part Two, Chapter V) treats a repeat as information about the plan, because slips are a normal part of changing anything.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    Much of what we do each day runs on autopilot. That saves energy, and it’s the whole reason habits exist. The downside is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one, so change asks your brain to do something effortful again and again, for weeks, until the new way becomes the easy one.

    Jodie wants to stop snapping at her children in the morning rush, and she means it every night. Every morning the same rush brings the same snap, somewhere between the lost reading book and the cold toast, because the morning has worn a groove deeper than her good intentions.

    Things to tell yourself “This is hard because it’s a habit.” “Old grooves take time to fill in.”

    Pick one thing you’d like to change and write down when it usually happens, where, and what’s going on just before. That’s the start of the next idea.

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger: a time, a place, a feeling, a person, or the thing you’ve just done. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward (relief, comfort, a little buzz) keeps it going. Once you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    A man notices that he reaches for the biscuit tin the moment he opens his laptop after dinner, hungry or not. The laptop is the cue. So he starts making a pot of tea before he opens it, and the biscuits, mostly, stay in the tin. For three days, each time your habit happens, jot down the time, the place, the feeling and what you were doing just before.

    Things to tell yourself “What happened right before I did that?” “What was I getting from it?”

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring but easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and they work because they are easy enough to do on a bad day. It helps to tie the new step to something you already do, as in “After I put the kettle on, I’ll…”, and to grow it only once it has stuck.

    Two housemates want a tidier kitchen. They’ve been promising each other a big Sunday clean since March, and it has happened once. So they agree instead that after dinner each of them wipes one surface. It takes two minutes. By the second week the hob has stopped being a source of tension, and by the fourth somebody has descaled the kettle without being asked.

    You could say “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I ..., I will ...”

    Dig deeper: why small steps work

  4. Change the setup, not just your willpower.

    Willpower runs low when we are tired, hungry or stressed, while your surroundings stay the same all day. So make the new habit easier, by putting it in sight or getting things ready the night before, and make the old one a little harder, by moving it out of reach or adding a step. I’d back a small change to the setup over a big push of effort almost every time.

    A teenager wants to sleep better and has tried for weeks to stop scrolling in bed. Then he starts charging his phone in the kitchen overnight, next to the bread bin, and within a week he’s asleep before midnight most nights. He grumbles about it. He also keeps doing it.

    Words you could use (to yourself or a partner) “What could we change about the setup so this gets easier?”

  5. Being in two minds is normal.

    Part of you wants to change and part of you doesn’t, and the reluctant part usually has its reasons. The old habit may give comfort, save effort or protect you from something. Pushing harder against that part rarely works, while listening to it often does.

    Asif wants to drink less, but he also loves Friday evenings in the pub with his team. When he asks himself what Friday gives him, the answer is friends, laughter and a way to unwind, so he keeps the Friday and switches to alcohol-free after the first pint. Nobody at the table notices, or if they do, nobody says.

    Thoughts you could try “What does the old habit give me?” “What would make the change worth it to me?”

    Try two lists, one of what you like about how things are and one of what you’d like about changing. Both lists are allowed, and the first one is usually more honest.

    Dig deeper: readiness and mixed feelings

  6. Slips are part of change.

    Nearly everyone who changes something slips back sometimes, and one slip doesn’t undo your progress. What often does the damage is the thought that follows, the “I’ve blown it, so why bother?” Treating yourself kindly after a slip makes it more likely that you will start again.

    After three calm weeks, a dad shouts at his son over homework. That night he sits on the end of the bed and says, “I shouted, and I’m sorry. I’m working on it.” The next evening they do the homework at the kitchen table with the radio on, and nobody raises their voice.

    Things to tell yourself “A slip is one moment out of many.” “What made today harder, and what can I plan for next time?”

    Decide now, while things are going well, what you’ll do after a slip: one kind sentence to yourself, and one small step to start again.

    Dig deeper: slips and starting again

    Tidbit

    A slip is a stumble, not a fall. Try the tiny step again tomorrow. A game for small kind acts can make the practising easier.

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

    Change made only to please others, or out of shame, is hard to keep. Change that connects to what you care about, to the kind of person you want to be, has deeper roots, and each small action becomes a vote for that person.

    A woman who keeps meaning to be better at keeping in touch starts thinking of herself as someone who shows up for her friends. Sending one voice note on a Sunday evening becomes part of who she is, and some weeks it turns into an hour on the phone.

    Things to tell yourself “Who am I becoming with this step?” “This matters to me because...”

    Dig deeper: values and who you’re becoming

  8. You can support change, but not force it.

    It is natural to want the people we love to change, perhaps to drink less, tidy more or look after themselves better. Their change belongs to them, though, and pressure tends to make people dig in. It helps more to say clearly what you need, ask what would help, and notice their small steps out loud.

    A colleague keeps missing deadlines. His teammate has the lecture about being organised all ready to go, and skips it. She asks what would make the handover easier, and the two of them set up a shared checklist that he now updates every Friday before lunch.

    Ways to say it “I can’t change this for you, but I’d love to help if you want me to.” “I noticed you did that this week. That really helped.”

Two weeks later, 7:05am

The trainers are still by the door, but the phone charges in the kitchen overnight now, and the plan has shrunk to shoes on and out to the end of the road. Some mornings that is all you manage. Most mornings it turns into a walk past the allotments and back. On the Thursday you miss, the voice starts up again, and you tell it that tomorrow you will get as far as the end of the road.

In everyday life

Take one couple who want to spend less time on their phones in the evening. Earlier attempts failed because each of them watched the other, so this time they agree on a basket by the door where both phones go at nine, with one exception for whoever is on call for an elderly parent. The first week is awkward, and on the Wednesday one of them sneaks a look at the football scores. By the third week the basket has become part of the furniture, and the evenings have a shape again, with a film, a crossword or an early night.

Big life changes, such as a move, a new baby or a new job, break old routines, which is disruptive and also a good moment to start new habits. If you have ADHD, make the cue impossible to miss, do the step the moment it appears and make the reward come straight away. Expect the novelty to wear off, and swap the method when it does instead of blaming yourself. More in the full chapter.

On your own: a kind plan for change

If you are working on something alone, the ideas fit into one small plan. Choose one change, the one that would make the most difference or the one that feels most doable. Give it one cue, written as “After I ___, I will ___.” Make one tweak to your setup so it is easier to start, decide on the smallest version you could still do on a tired day, and write down one kind sentence to use after a slip. Common mix-ups

“If I really wanted it, I’d just do it” is the cruellest of these, and it’s wrong. Wanting is only the start; habits, setup, tiredness and stress all have a vote. Then there’s the famous 21 days. There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range. Being hard on yourself doesn’t keep you on track either. Harshness tends to make people give up, and kindness makes it easier to try again. And routines don’t have to mean being rigid. For a lot of people, autistic people especially, routines are what make life manageable, and change can respect that.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you advise, perhaps with “Would ideas help, or would you rather just talk it through?”, and help them find their own reasons by asking what would be different for them if this changed. Practical help with the setup, such as a lift to the gym, a shared calendar or a quieter evening, often does more than encouragement alone. Notice small steps out loud, be kind about slips, and look after yourself as well, because you can care deeply and still set limits on what you will put up with.

Words to try “I’m on your side with this. What would help?” “You’ve done this three days running. That’s not nothing.”

When to get more help

  • If a habit is harming your health or life, such as drinking, drugs, gambling, eating or self-harm, please talk to a doctor or a specialist service. Needing help with this is common and nothing to be ashamed of.
  • If you can’t stop even though you’ve tried many times, professional support can make a real difference. It isn’t a sign of weakness.
  • If change feels impossible because you’re exhausted or low most days, that may be burnout or depression. A doctor or counsellor can help.
  • If someone is demanding you change in ways that frighten you, or punishing you when you don’t, that isn’t support. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
