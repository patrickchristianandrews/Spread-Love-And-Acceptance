Part Three · Chapter 10

Values, meaning and a good life: what philosophy offers everyday life

Paperback textRead the expanded version

What matters to you, how to find it, what to do when two good things pull against each other, and what a few very old ideas can offer an ordinary, busy week.

At a glance

  • Values are directions you keep turning toward. You never finish being kind, and that is why values still work on days when goals fall apart.
  • Strong feelings point to what you value. Anger often guards fairness, and hurt often guards closeness.
  • Meaning comes from ordinary places. People, care and everyday work give most of us most of our meaning.
  • Values live in small acts. One small thing this week, done because it matters, counts.

Sunday, 9pm

The week ahead has the car inspection on Tuesday, a dentist, two late meetings and your turn for the school drop-off, twice, and none of it feels like yours, somehow. You’re not sad, exactly. You just can’t remember the last time you did something because it mattered to you, and not because it was next on the list.

This chapter is about values, the things you care about and the direction you want your life to face, and about what makes a life feel worth living. It borrows from psychology, and from philosophy, which has been asking how we ought to live for more than two thousand years. You don’t need to have read any of it. I’ve tried to keep the old names to a minimum and the words plain.

In quick strokes, a full week looks like a diary problem. Underneath, it is a values problem, two good things wanting the same evening. Underneath that, what quietly gets rewarded at home. The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives. The chapter then slows down on each.

  1. Values are directions you keep walking in.

    A goal is something you can check off, like painting the hall or passing the test. A value is a direction you keep walking in, like looking after your health, or making home feel cared for, or being a fair friend. Nobody ever finishes being kind. That’s exactly why a value still works on a day when a goal falls apart.

    Bethan was going to go to the gym three times this week. She went once, on the Monday. On Thursday evening she was on the couch in her gym clothes eating a family bag of fries and feeling like a failure. So she asked herself what the gym was for. Looking after her body, was the answer, and when she asked what that was for, it was so she’d have energy for the kids. On Friday she walked twenty minutes at lunchtime, around the little park behind the office, past the man who feeds the pigeons, and as far as she was concerned it counted.

  2. Your strong feelings point to what you value.

    You don’t have to invent your values from scratch. They show up in your strongest reactions. Anger often means something you care about has been stepped on. Envy can point to something you want for yourself. Sadness tends to show what you love.

    A colleague stood up in the quarterly review and took credit for a whole team’s work, and Sacha was so angry he couldn’t eat his lunch. He spent the afternoon drafting emails he didn’t send. On the train home it dawned on him that it wasn’t really about the colleague. It was fairness. Seeing people’s effort. That was what had been stepped on. At the next team meeting he raised it as a fairness thing (“Can we agree how we credit joint work?”) instead of seething at one person for a month, and it went better than any of the emails would have.

    Dig deeper: finding your values

  3. Two good things can pull in different directions.

    Most hard choices aren’t between something good and something bad. They’re between two good things, honesty and kindness, say, or family and rest. Feeling torn just means you care about more than one thing. Even when you choose well, it’s normal to feel a bit sad about the side you didn’t pick.

    Pickles, the family Labrador, was very ill, and Megan’s eight-year-old kept asking. Megan cares about being honest, and she also cares about protecting her son. She sat on the kitchen floor next to the dog’s bed with him and tried to do both. “Pickles is very sick. The vet is helping. We’ll look after him together.” Her son thought about it for a long time, picking at the edge of the dog blanket. “Is he going to die?” “He might,” she said. “We don’t know yet.” He put his hand on Pickles’s side, very carefully, and left it there.

    If you’re stuck on a choice, write down the good thing on each side. You may be able to honor some of both, even if you can’t have all of either.

    Dig deeper: when values clash

  4. Put your effort where it can land.

    About two thousand years ago a teacher called Epictetus, who had been born into slavery, taught that some things are up to us and some aren’t. It’s one of the oldest ideas in this book and still one of the most useful. Your own choices and how you treat people are up to you. Other people’s choices, and the weather, and the past, are not. Worry tends to pile up in the second heap. Values do their work in the first.

    Ingrid wants her partner to enjoy Christmas at her mom’s, and she can’t make him. She has tried, for six years, mainly by telling him it’ll be fun. What she can do is plan a shorter visit, put a long muddy walk on the day after Christmas into it, which he’ll like, and be warm herself. That’s her part, and it turns out to be quite a lot.

    Dig deeper: what is up to us

  5. A good life is more than a good mood.

    Feeling good matters. But many thinkers, from the Greek philosopher Aristotle to psychologists now, also describe a second kind of well-being. It comes from growing, using your strengths, being a decent friend, and doing things that matter even when they’re hard. Aristotle called it flourishing, and he thought you build it the way you build any skill, by practice, a little at a time.

    Jade spent a whole month of evenings helping a friend move out of a damp, miserable apartment. She scrubbed black mold off a bathroom ceiling with a toothbrush. She carried boxes down three flights of stairs while the friend cried on the landing. It was tiring and not much fun, and at the end of the year, when she thinks back over it, she counts it as one of the best months she had.

  6. Meaning comes from ordinary places.

    Researchers who study meaning often describe three parts to it: your life makes some sense to you, you’re heading somewhere, and your life matters to someone. Most meaning comes from people, from work done with care and from small roles other people count on, far more than from big moments.

    Three meanings of meaning The three parts above come from Martela and Steger, who split meaning into coherence (life makes sense), purpose (a direction) and significance (your life matters). It’s a way of thinking that has shaped later research rather than a finding in itself.

    Martela and Steger (2016) · Theory: a conceptual paper that has shaped later measurement

    Walter has lived alone since his wife died, and for a long time his days went very quiet. Now he reads to the preschoolers at the library on Wednesday mornings. He irons a shirt for it on Tuesday nights. He does the voices, badly. When he had a cold in February and missed two weeks, a four-year-old called Albie asked the librarian every single day where the man was.

    Try listing the roles you play, big or small (neighbor, sister, the one who waters the office plants), and next to each one write who it matters to.

    Dig deeper: where meaning comes from

  7. Different values aren’t wrong values.

    Two people can share a home, a family or a team and still put the same good things in a different order, one putting safety first and another adventure. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that there are many real goods in life and they can’t all be ranked on one scale, which leaves room to disagree without either person being bad.

    People who love you will sometimes try to change your mind, and that’s ordinary influence. They tell you why, they listen to your reasons, and the choice stays yours. It becomes pressure when it keeps coming after you’ve said no, when saying no costs you something, such as days of coldness, money or being left out, or when it’s aimed at the people and things that matter most to you. A good test is whether you can say “I see it differently” and still be treated warmly the next day.

    Jasper likes a calm, tidy house. His roommate Darnell likes an open door and people dropping in. For months each of them privately thought the other was a bit much, uptight in one direction, messy in the other. It came out one night over takeout. “I just like knowing who’s going to be in the kitchen,” Jasper said. Darnell said, “I just like it when anyone is.” Fridays are open house now, and Sundays are quiet, or as quiet as Darnell can manage.

    In your next disagreement, you might ask, “What matters to you about this?” and listen for the value under the position.

    Dig deeper: living with different values

  8. Values live in small, everyday acts.

    A good life is built mostly from small things done again and again. How you say hello. Whether you keep small promises. How you talk about someone who isn’t in the room. The Chinese teacher Confucius taught that small, everyday courtesies shape who we become, and later thinkers who wrote about care said that being good starts with paying attention to the person in front of you.

    Kindness does something for the giver A meta-analysis of 27 experiments found that acts of kindness gave the person doing them a small-to-medium lift in well-being. The authors flagged worries about study quality, so take the size with a pinch of salt and the direction with rather less.

    Curry and colleagues (2018) · Moderate: the authors noted concerns about study quality and possible publication bias

    An older man at the self-checkout can’t get the card machine to take his card. The line behind him is growing and somebody near the back huffs, loudly. You’re next, and you don’t sigh. You say, “No rush.” He says, “It’s always this one, isn’t it,” and you agree that it is. The whole thing costs you about forty seconds.

    Pick one value, choose one tiny act that shows it, and do it every day this week. You’ll drift, as everyone does, and turning back is the practice.

Your part, and only your part. You can choose your direction. When you notice you’ve drifted, you can turn back without shame. You can’t choose other people’s values for them, and you don’t have to adopt theirs to love them. You can share what matters to you, listen to what matters to them, and look for the places where your directions overlap.

Sunday, 9:20pm

On the back of an envelope you write people, health and making things, and under each one, a small thing for this week. See Grandma, walk on Wednesday, finish the shelf in the spare room, the one that has been “nearly done” since Easter. The week is just as full as it was. You prop the envelope against the kettle, where it’ll be the first thing you see.

Two ways to read this

Values, two ways

Some people hold a value as a rule: honesty means saying the true thing, every time. Others hold it as a direction, adjusted to the moment. If you hold rules, the direction person isn’t being slippery. They’re weighing two good things at once. If you hold directions, the rule person isn’t being harsh. A clear rule is how they stay steady.

Where the fields meet. Politics has a lot to say about two people with different values under one roof: a fair way to decide, and a say for everyone, and a way to change the rule later. The root is ownership and structure, plus quiet incentives, because a home drifts toward whatever it rewards.

In everyday life

Partners may rank the same values differently, security and adventure, say, and it helps to say what each of you is protecting before you argue about the plan. Two generations of a family can both value family and mean very different things by it, from living around the corner to being free to go, so it’s worth asking what the word means to each of you. When a job keeps asking you to act against a value, the strain often shows up as tiredness or cynicism, and naming the clash is a first step. A club, a faith group or some volunteering can give you a place to act on a value alongside other people.

If you want to help someone else with this

“What matters most to you about this?” helps more than telling someone what they ought to care about. People often see their values for the first time in someone else’s words, so it can help to notice them out loud. “You stayed late to help your friend. Loyalty really matters to you, doesn’t it?” With children, naming the value when you see it teaches more than any lecture. Try not to use values as a weapon, because “I thought you valued family” lands as a guilt trip. You can disagree with someone’s choice and still see the good thing they were reaching for.

You could say “What would a good outcome look like to you?” “What do you want to be able to say about how you handled this, a year from now?”
