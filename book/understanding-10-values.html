Part Three · Chapter 10

Values, meaning and a good life: what philosophy offers everyday life

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What matters to you, how to find it, what to do when two good things pull against each other, and what a few very old ideas can offer an ordinary, busy week.

In short

  • Values are directions you keep turning towards. You never finish being kind, and that is why values still work on days when goals fall apart.
  • Strong feelings point to what you value. Anger often guards fairness, and hurt often guards closeness.
  • Meaning comes from ordinary places. People, care and everyday work give most of us most of our meaning.
  • Values live in small acts. One small thing this week, done because it matters, counts.

Sunday, 9pm

The week ahead is full: the MOT on Tuesday, a dentist, two late meetings, your turn for the school run. None of it feels like yours. You aren’t sad, exactly. You just can’t remember the last time you did something because it mattered to you, rather than because it was next on the list.

This chapter is about values, the things you care about and the direction you want your life to face, and about what makes a life feel worth living. It borrows from psychology and from philosophy, which has been asking how we should live for more than two thousand years. You don’t need to have read any of it. I’ve tried to keep the old names to a minimum and the words plain.

  1. Values are directions you keep walking in.

    A goal is something you can tick off, such as running a 5k, painting the hall or passing a test. A value is a direction you keep walking in, such as looking after your health, making your home feel cared for or being a fair friend. Nobody ever finishes being kind, and that’s exactly why a value still works on a day when a goal falls apart.

    Bethan planned to go to the gym three times this week and went once, and on Thursday evening, eating crisps on the sofa in her gym kit, she felt like a failure. When she asked herself what the gym was for, the answer was looking after her body so that she had energy for her children. On Friday she walked for twenty minutes at lunchtime, round the park behind the office, and it counted. If you have a goal that has stalled, try asking “what is this for?” three times in a row, and write down where you end up.

    Dig deeper: goals and values

  2. Your strong feelings point to what you value.

    You don’t have to invent your values from nothing. They show up in your strongest reactions. Anger often means something you care about has been stepped on. Envy can point to something you want for yourself. Sadness tends to show what you love. Sacha is furious when a colleague takes credit for the team’s work. Under the anger is a value, which is fairness and seeing people’s effort, and once he names it he can raise fairness at the next team meeting instead of seething at one person for a week. He might say, “I think I’m upset because fairness really matters to me, more than winning this one.”

    Think of the last time you felt strongly, in a good way or a bad one, and finish the sentence “That mattered to me because I care about…”

    Dig deeper: finding your values

  3. Two good things can pull in different directions.

    Most hard choices aren’t between a good thing and a bad one. They’re between two good things, honesty and kindness, say, or family and rest. Feeling torn usually just means you care about more than one thing, and even when you choose well it’s normal to feel a little sad about the side you didn’t pick. A mum cares about being honest and about protecting her eight-year-old. When the family dog is very ill, she finds a way to honour both and tells the truth gently, in words a child can hold. “Biscuit is very poorly. The vet is helping, and we’ll look after him together.”

    If you feel stuck on a choice, write down the good thing on each side. It often helps to ask whether you can honour some of both, even if you can’t have all of either.

    Dig deeper: when values clash

  4. Put your effort where it can land.

    About two thousand years ago a teacher called Epictetus, who had been born into slavery, taught that some things are up to us and some aren’t. It’s one of the oldest ideas in this book and still one of the most useful. (I spent my career as a government auditor, and sorting what could actually be fixed from what couldn’t was a large part of the work.) Your own choices, effort and treatment of other people are up to you, while other people’s choices, the weather and the past are not. Worry tends to collect in the second pile, and values do their work in the first. Ingrid wants her partner to enjoy Christmas with her family, and she can’t make him enjoy it. She can plan a shorter visit, put a long muddy walk on Boxing Day into it, which he’ll like, and be warm herself. That’s her part, and it turns out to be quite a lot.

    One way to try this is to write a worry in two columns, the parts that are up to you and the parts that aren’t, then pick one small thing from the first column and do it.

    Dig deeper: what is up to us

  5. A good life is more than a good mood.

    Feeling good matters. Many thinkers, from the Greek philosopher Aristotle to psychologists today, also describe a second kind of wellbeing, which comes from growing, using your strengths, being a good friend and doing things that matter even when they are hard. Aristotle called it flourishing, and he thought we build it the way we build any skill, by practice, a little at a time. Jade spent a month of evenings helping a friend move out of a damp, unhappy flat, scrubbing black mould off a bathroom ceiling and carrying boxes down three flights. It was tiring and not much fun. When she looks back on her year, she counts it among the best months of it.

    Tonight you might write down one thing that felt good today and one that felt worthwhile, and notice whether they turn out to be the same thing.

    Dig deeper: happiness and flourishing

  6. Meaning comes from ordinary places.

    Researchers who study meaning often describe three parts to it. Your life makes some sense to you, you are heading somewhere, and your life matters to someone. Most meaning comes from people, from work done with care and from small roles that others count on, far more than from big moments. Walter has lived alone since his wife died, and for a long time his days went quiet. Now he reads to children at the library one morning a week, which gives him a reason to iron a shirt on Tuesday nights and a small group of people who notice when he isn’t there.

    Try listing three roles you play, big or small, such as neighbour, sister or the one who waters the office plants, and next to each one write who it matters to.

    Dig deeper: where meaning comes from

  7. Different values aren’t wrong values.

    Two people can share a home, a family or a team and still put the same good things in a different order, with one putting safety first and another adventure. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that there are many real goods in life and that they can’t all be ranked on one scale, which leaves room to disagree without either person being bad. Jasper values a calm, tidy home, and his housemate Darnell values an open door and friends dropping by. For months each privately thought of the other as messy or uptight. Once they named what each of them cared about, they could make a plan, and Friday nights became open house while Sundays stayed quiet.

    In your next disagreement, it may help to ask, “What matters to you about this?”, and to listen for the value underneath the position.

    Dig deeper: living with different values

  8. Values live in small, everyday acts.

    A good life is built mostly from small things done again and again, such as how you greet people, whether you keep small promises and how you talk about someone who isn’t in the room. The Chinese teacher Confucius taught that small, everyday courtesies shape who we become, and later thinkers who wrote about care said that being good starts with paying attention to the person in front of you. At the supermarket an older man is struggling with the card machine, and the queue behind him is growing; someone near the back tuts. You wait without sighing and tell him there’s no rush. It costs you about forty seconds.

    Pick one value, choose one tiny act that shows it, and do it each day this week. You’ll drift. Everyone does. Turning back is the practice.

    Dig deeper: everyday ethics

Your part, and only your part. You can choose your direction, notice when you drift, and turn back without shame. You can’t choose other people’s values for them, and you don’t have to adopt theirs to love them. You can share what matters to you, listen to what matters to them, and look for the places where your directions overlap.

Sunday, 9:20pm

You write three words on the back of an envelope, people, health and making things, and under each one a small thing for this week. Ring Gran. Walk on Wednesday. Finish the shelf in the spare room, the one that has been “nearly done” since Easter. The week is still full. You prop the envelope against the kettle, where you’ll see it in the morning before you see anything else.

In everyday life

Partners may rank the same values differently, such as security and adventure, and it helps to say what each of you is protecting before you argue about the plan. Two generations of a family can both value family and mean quite different things by it, from living close by to being free to go, so it is worth asking what the word means to each of you. When a job keeps asking you to act against a value, the strain often shows up as tiredness or cynicism, and naming the clash is a first step. A club, a faith group or volunteering can give you a place to act on a value alongside other people.

If you want to help someone else with this

Asking “What matters most to you about this?” helps more than telling someone what they ought to care about. People often see their values for the first time in someone else’s words, so it can help to notice them out loud, as in “You stayed late to help your friend. Loyalty really matters to you, doesn’t it?” With children, naming the value when you see it teaches more than a lecture. Try not to use values as a weapon, because “I thought you valued family” lands as a guilt trip. You can disagree with someone’s choice and still see the good thing they were reaching for.

You could say “What would a good outcome look like to you?” “What do you want to be able to say about how you handled this, a year from now?”

When to get more help

Feeling a bit lost or flat for a while is part of being human. But some signs mean it’s worth talking to someone trained:

  • Life has felt empty or pointless for more than two weeks, and things you used to enjoy don’t reach you. That can be depression, which is common and treatable. A doctor or counsellor is a good first step.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be alive. Please talk to someone today. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • Someone in your life is pushing you to give up what matters to you, cutting you off from friends, or making you afraid to disagree. That’s about control, not values. The page When it’s more than a rough patch can help you think it through.

Some therapies, such as acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), work directly with values. A counsellor can help you find yours when everything feels foggy.
