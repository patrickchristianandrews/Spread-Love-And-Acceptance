The book at a glance: where to start

This page is the whole book in a few short paragraphs: one for each part and chapter, saying what it’s about and where it can help. There are two versions. On your own is for one person, working on themselves. With others is for partners, family, roommates and coworkers. The switch above changes every section at once. Nothing is stored except your choice of version, and that stays in this browser.

Where to begin

Start with Part One. It’s about you, and you can do every step of it alone. Read Then, Now and Next in order, or go straight to the self path, which turns them into six short steps: check your battery, get to know your wiring, see where your lens came from, plan what settles you, find words for what you feel, and practice kind ways to say no.

You don’t have to finish anything. You can read one card and stop, and come back another day.

Start with the Preface, then Chapter I. The Preface helps you both see the work nobody sees. Chapter I explains why two kind people can still clash, and how to get back in step. Each chapter after that adds one idea and one tool.

One week of notes is enough to begin. The examples often use a couple at home, but the ideas fit anyone who shares a load: family, roommates, co-parents and coworkers.

Part One, Then: where you came from

Why you expect what you expect. Your home, school, friends and culture taught you rules that still make small decisions for you. This part helps you write down the rulebook from the home you grew up in, and choose which rules you still want to keep.

Two people can bring two rulebooks into one home, like “whoever notices, does it.” Neither is wrong. Saying your old rules out loud, on a calm day, turns a fight about dishes into a talk about who owns what, and lets you choose shared rules on purpose.

Part One, Now: who you are today

Your wiring, your weather and your words. Your wiring is how you’re built, and it stays. Today’s conditions, like sleep and stress, pass. This part helps you tell them apart, check your state before you speak, plan what settles you, and say what helps in one short sentence.

Telling people what helps you, kindly and early, lets them plan instead of guess: “A heads-up helps me. It isn’t about you.” This part also covers matching energy: giving care and effort in proportion to what comes back, so nobody runs on empty.

Part One, Next: who you are becoming

Choosing a direction. Start with what matters to you, pick one small goal in plain words, make the first step tiny, and look back kindly once a month. A missed day is information about the plan, not about you.

Tell one person what you’re trying and what would help: “Could you ask me about it on Sunday?” It’s information, not a demand. They can say yes, not yet or no, and you don’t need their agreement to start.

Preface: the work nobody sees

For one week, notice the jobs only you remember, and write them down for yourself. Count it, don’t charge it. The point is to see your real load, so you can plan your energy.

Running a shared home takes work nobody sees: noticing, remembering, planning and smoothing over. Each of you writes down your own week, then you read the two lists together. It isn’t keeping score. It’s so you both see the same picture.

Chapter I: why we get out of tune

Learn your own pace, tone and what “soon” means to you, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. When you drift, step out, settle and come back. Retuning can start with one person.

Two people can each say something reasonable and still clash, like two radios slightly out of tune. Nobody has to be wrong. Name it, pause, slow down, then go back to the topic with one clear sentence.

Chapter II: is the split working?

Are you spending more energy than comes back in? Set what you carry beside what refills you. If it runs in the red, drop, delay, ask, or say no kindly.

A calm check of whether the way you share the work can last, using what you both wrote down: how the hours split, whether each job has an owner, and how stressed each of you is. It judges the setup, never a person.

Chapter III: full tanks and different angles

A full stress tank makes small things feel big. Check your own tank for a few mornings in a row, ask how much of a reaction is left over from earlier, and decide on a calm day what settles you.

Two people can see the same moment from different angles, like tiredness, history or time pressure, and both be right. Before a hard talk, each of you says how full your tank is and which angle you’re seeing from.

Chapter IV: two kinds of fair

Write down what fair means to you in one sentence, and list the jobs you truly own. When a comment stings, let it land before you answer, then reply with a fact, a feeling and an ask.

Fair can mean “did it all get done?” or “did each of us do what we said?” Agree which you’ll use, give each job one owner, and say it out loud when you cover. Agree on a phrase like “Give me a second” for when a comment lands hard.

Chapter V: the monthly look-back

Once a month, look back at your own month, kindly. What filled your battery and what drained it? Which limits held? Keep one thing, and change one thing.

Once a month, look for the jobs that keep slipping, and fix the setup instead of having the same argument again. Twenty minutes is usually enough. It’s never a review of either person.
