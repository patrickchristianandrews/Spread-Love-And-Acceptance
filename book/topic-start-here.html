The book at a glance: where to start

This page is the whole book in a few short paragraphs: one for each part and chapter, saying what it’s about and where it can help. There are two versions. On your own is for one person, working on themselves. With others is for partners, family, housemates and coworkers. The switch above changes every section at once. Nothing is stored except your choice of version, and that stays in this browser.

Introduction: Spread Love & Acceptance

The idea of this book is simple, like a ledger: a notebook where you write down what happened, so you can look at it calmly later. Write down what is really going on, look at it on your own or with others, and change the setup instead of blaming a person.

It has three parts, and they are meant to be read in this order. Part One (Then, Now and Next) is about you, and it is where the book begins. Part Two opens with its own Preface, The work nobody sees, and then Chapters I to V on the people you share a life, a home or a job with. Part Three, Understanding people, explains how people work, chapter by chapter, for anyone: on your own, at home, with friends, at work or in your community. Part Two’s chapters use Roman numerals (I to V) and Part Three’s use ordinary numbers (1 to 17), so “Chapter IV” and “Part Three, Chapter 4” are different chapters.

Every chapter comes in two sizes. The short version takes about 10 to 15 minutes to read (each chapter shows its own time at the top) and gives you the main ideas, each with one small thing to try. You don’t have to read it in one go: one idea card takes a minute or two. The in-depth version explains the reasoning, walks through a full example, lists common mix-ups and answers questions people often ask. Start short. Go deeper only when a chapter clearly fits your life.

Where to begin

Start with Part One. It’s about you, and you can do every step of it alone. Read Then, Now and Next in order, or go straight to the self path, which turns them into six short steps: check your battery, get to know your wiring, see where your lens came from, plan what settles you, find words for what you feel, and practise kind ways to say no.

You don’t have to finish anything. You can read one card and stop, and come back another day.

Part One works for anyone: someone living alone, someone fresh out of a breakup, a student in a shared flat, a parent who has no spare evening, or a partner who wants to do some work before asking anyone else to. If the people around you aren’t interested, nothing in Part One needs them. You only need a pen, ten quiet minutes, and the willingness to look at yourself kindly.

Words you could use (to yourself): “I’m not fixing everything. I’m reading one card.” “I can stop here and come back.” “This is information about me, not a grade.”

Try this: Before you read anything else, take the one-minute My weather today check. Then open the self path at step one, and stop after one card.

Start with the Preface, then Chapter I. The Preface helps you both see the work nobody sees. Chapter I explains why two kind people can still clash, and how to get back in step. Each chapter after that adds one idea and one tool.

One week of notes is enough to begin. The examples often use a couple at home, but the ideas fit anyone who shares a load: family, housemates, co-parents and coworkers.

You don’t need the other person to sign up first. One person can read the Preface, keep their own week of notes and share them as information. Many people start that way. If the others do want to join, agree three things before you begin: each person writes only their own side, nobody uses the notes to win an argument, and anyone can say “not tonight.”

  • A couple or a triad can read the Preface on the same evening and each keep a week of notes.
  • Housemates, or students sharing a room in halls, can start with Chapter IV (one owner per job) and a ten-minute house meeting.
  • Separated co-parents can start with Chapter I (getting back in tune) and keep messages short and practical.
  • Coworkers and business partners can start with Chapter II (is the split working?) and the team tools on At work.
  • Grown-up children and their parents, or adult siblings, can start with Part One, Then: the rulebook each of you brought from the same home is rarely the same rulebook.

Words you could use: “I found a short book about sharing the work at home. Would you read the first chapter with me this week? No pressure if not.” “I’m going to write down my own week. I’m not keeping score. I just want us to see the same picture.”

Three ways to read the book

  • Straight through: Then, Now and Next, then the Preface to Part Two and Chapters I to V, then Part Three, from Chapter 1 to the end. Every chapter ends with a “Next” link to the one after it. Together, one chapter a week and a twenty-minute talk at the weekend covers Parts One and Two in about five weeks. Part Three can come next, or be read on its own: here is a suggested order.
  • By topic: if one thing is pressing, read only what every chapter says about it: staying calm, knowing yourself, talking it through or sharing the load.

Part One, Then: where you came from

Why you expect what you expect. Your home, school, friends and culture taught you rules that still make small decisions for you. This part helps you write down the rulebook from the home you grew up in, and choose which rules you still want to keep.

An example: you feel guilty sitting down while anyone else is still busy. Looking back, you notice that in your childhood home, resting was only okay once everything was done. That rule made sense for a busy household. You can decide whether it still fits a flat you live in alone.

Two people can bring two rulebooks into one home, like “whoever notices, does it.” Neither is wrong. Saying your old rules out loud, on a calm day, turns a fight about dishes into a talk about who owns what, and lets you choose shared rules on purpose.

It matters in every kind of home. Two partners from different faiths or cultures may each hold a different idea of what a holiday is “supposed” to look like (see two faiths, one family). Adult siblings raised in the same house can remember very different rules. A foster or kinship child arrives with a whole rulebook from somewhere else (see foster and kinship families).

Words you could use: “In my house growing up, whoever noticed did it. What was it like in yours?”

Part One, Now: who you are today

Your wiring, your weather and your words. Your wiring is how you’re built, and it stays. Today’s conditions, like sleep and stress, pass. This part helps you tell them apart, check your state before you speak, plan what settles you, and say what helps in one short sentence.

An example: a night-shift nurse snaps at a friend’s text at 3pm. Is that who they are (wiring), or is it that they slept four hours (today’s conditions)? It’s the sleep. The fix is a nap and a later reply, not a change of personality. See different hours for more on shift work.

Telling people what helps you, kindly and early, lets them plan instead of guess: “A heads-up helps me. It isn’t about you.” This part also covers matching energy: giving care and effort in proportion to what comes back, so nobody runs on empty.

A friend who always does the reaching out, a partner living with an illness, a teen who needs time alone after school: each of these is about energy, not love. Saying what you have to give, and asking what the other person has, keeps it fair without anyone being blamed. See friends and when one of you is ill.

Words you could use: “I’m low on battery this week. I can do the school run, but not the forms. Could we look at those on Sunday?”

Part One, Next: who you are becoming

Choosing a direction. Start with what matters to you, pick one small goal in plain words, make the first step tiny, and look back kindly once a month. A missed day is information about the plan, not about you.

An example: after a divorce, someone wants to “get my life back.” That’s a wish. “Call one friend each Sunday” is a goal. “If I’m too tired to call, I send one text” is the backup. See on my own for the weeks after a breakup.

Tell one person what you’re trying and what would help: “Could you ask me about it on Sunday?” It’s information, not a demand. They can say yes, not yet or no, and you don’t need their agreement to start.

This works with a partner, a housemate, a grown-up child, or a coworker. When the house goes quiet after the kids leave, two people can each name one thing they want to try next, and ask for a little support (see kids grown and gone).

Words you could use: “I’m trying to walk after dinner. You don’t have to come. Would you ask me how it went on Sunday?”

Preface to Part Two: the work nobody sees

For one week, notice the jobs only you remember, and write them down for yourself. Count it, don’t charge it. The point is to see your real load, so you can plan your energy.

An example: someone living alone writes down a week and finds that nearly half their evenings went on looking after other people, like a mother’s appointments, a friend in crisis and a group chat to organise. None of it was a chore in the usual sense, and all of it took energy.

Running a shared home takes work nobody sees: noticing, remembering, planning and smoothing over. Each of you writes down your own week, then you read the two lists together. It isn’t keeping score. It’s so you both see the same picture.

The unseen work shows up everywhere: the housemate who always buys the toilet roll, the co-parent who tracks both homes’ school forms, the sibling who organises every visit to an ageing parent, the business partner who quietly chases every invoice.

Words you could use: “I’m not asking for a medal. I’d just like us both to see this list. What’s on yours?”

Chapter I: why we get out of tune

Learn your own pace, tone and what “soon” means to you, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. When you drift, step out, settle and come back. Retuning can start with one person.

An example: you notice you reply to your brother’s messages within seconds, and he replies the next day. You read his pace as not caring. It may just be his pace. Slowing your own reply a little takes the pressure off both of you.

Two people can each say something reasonable and still clash, like two radios slightly out of tune. Nobody has to be wrong. Name it, pause, slow down, then go back to the topic with one clear sentence.

It happens between a grandparent and a busy parent (“when you get a chance” means today to one, next month to the other), between students sharing a room, and between a manager and a new hire.

Words you could use: “I think we’re out of tune. Can we take ten minutes and try again?”

Chapter II: is the split working?

Are you spending more energy than comes back in? Set what you carry beside what refills you. If it runs in the red, drop, delay, ask, or say no kindly.

An example: a carer for a parent with dementia adds up the week and sees almost nothing refilling them. That isn’t a failure. It’s a sign to ask a sibling for one fixed afternoon, or to look for respite. See caring for someone you love.

A calm check of whether the way you share the work can last, using what you both wrote down: how the hours split, whether each job has an owner, and how stressed each of you is. It judges the setup, never a person.

A shift-working couple, three housemates, or two co-founders can all use the same check. When one person works from home and seems “always available,” the check often shows that the split was never agreed, only assumed (see different hours).

Words you could use: “Can we look at the setup, not at each other? I think it might need a rethink.”

Chapter III: full tanks and different angles

A full stress tank makes small things feel big. Check your own tank for a few mornings in a row, ask how much of a reaction is left over from earlier, and decide on a calm day what settles you.

An example: you snap at a coworker about a small change to a document. Looking back, you’d had a hard call with your teenager that morning. The document wasn’t the problem. Your tank was already full.

Two people can see the same moment from different angles, like tiredness, history or time pressure, and both be right. Before a hard talk, each of you says how full your tank is and which angle you’re seeing from.

A partner home from months away, a parent just off a night shift, or a family after a funeral will all see the same evening differently. Naming your angle first saves a lot of guessing. See coming home after time apart.

Words you could use: “My tank’s pretty full tonight, and I’m seeing this mostly from the tired angle. What about you?”

Chapter IV: two kinds of fair

Write down what fair means to you in one sentence, and list the jobs you truly own. When a comment stings, let it land before you answer, then reply with a fact, a feeling and an ask.

An example: in a shared flat, you always end up taking out the recycling. You’ve never agreed to own it. You just noticed first. Writing that down helps you decide whether to keep it, or hand it back kindly.

Fair can mean “did it all get done?” or “did each of us do what we said?” Agree which you’ll use, give each job one owner, and say it out loud when you cover. Agree on a phrase like “Give me a second” for when a comment lands hard.

Three partners sharing a home, two foster carers, or a team at work can each own named jobs. Ownership ends the “I thought you were doing it” loop. See more than two partners and sharing a room.

Words you could use: “Can we each own a few jobs, start to finish? I’ll take bins and bills. Which would you like?”

Chapter V: the monthly look-back

Once a month, look back at your own month, kindly. What filled your battery and what drained it? Which limits held? Keep one thing, and change one thing.

An example: a part-time parent notices that the weekends with the kids leave them flat on Monday every time. The change for next month is small: an easy dinner planned ahead for Sunday night.

Once a month, look for the jobs that keep slipping, and fix the setup instead of having the same argument again. Twenty minutes is usually enough. It’s never a review of either person.

Couples, housemates, co-parents and small teams can all run it. Grandparents who help with childcare can join a look-back with the parents, so problems get fixed while they’re still small (see for grandparents).

Words you could use: “Shall we do our twenty-minute look-back on Sunday? Let’s start with one thing that went well.”

Part Three: understanding people

How people work, starting with you. Chapters on feelings, stress, how we see each other, talking, trust, change, self-worth, grief, minds that work differently, values, healing, helping, anger, personality and love. Each one explains how something works first, then what you could do with it. You don’t need anyone else to read it.

An example: you feel flat every Sunday evening and can’t say why. Chapter 1 helps you name it more exactly, like “dread about Monday” rather than “bad”. Chapter 2 helps you see where you are on the stress ladder, and which kind of rest would actually help.

Thoughts you could try: “Something in me is sending a signal. What is it trying to tell me?” “This is how people work. It isn’t a flaw in me.”

The same chapters, used between people: a partner, a friend, a parent, a grown-up child, a housemate, a coworker or a stranger. They explain why kind people misread each other, how trust grows, and how to repair things after a hurt, without blaming anyone.

An example: two coworkers keep misreading each other’s short emails. Chapter 3 shows why the brain fills a gap with a guess, often the worst one. Chapter 4 shows how one checking question clears it up.

Words you could use: “Can I check what you meant? I think I might be filling in the gaps.”
