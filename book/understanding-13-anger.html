Anger: what it’s for and what to do with it

Paperback textRead the expanded version

What anger is for, what to do in the hot minutes, how to repair afterward, and how to tell when anger has turned into harm. For anyone, in any kind of relationship, or on your own.

In the paperbacks: Book 2, Heal and Steady, Chapter 2.

At a glance

  • Anger is a signal that something seems unfair, blocked or threatening. The feeling is allowed, and what you do with it is the part you choose.
  • The first minutes are about the body. Slow down, breathe out long, and step away with a time to come back.
  • Shouting doesn’t empty anger. Shouting tends to feed it, while calming the body, putting it into words and repairing any sharp moments help more.
  • Anger that hurts someone has become harm. The feeling is yours to own, and so is what you do with it.

Tuesday, 6:10pm

Your brother. Cant do thurs after all sorry!! work thing

Thursday is Mom’s hospital appointment. The one with the specialist, the one you’ve been going on about for three weeks, and it’s the third time this month he’s done this. Your jaw goes and your face goes hot. Your thumbs are already moving, TYPICAL. You never, and the message is one tap from sent.

Plenty of us were taught that anger was rude, or dangerous, or grew up around anger that was frightening. Anger itself is an ordinary human feeling, though, and it very often points at something real. It also sits under two tools elsewhere. Calm-down plan (Part One, Now) works because anger rises in the body first and slowing the body is what makes words possible. And letting a comment land before you react (Part Two, Chapter IV) is the stepping back described further on.

The canceled appointment, seen quickly. The surface is a sibling who let you down again. Underneath, anger, fast and hot. Underneath that, fear about Mom and tiredness. The roots are signal and noise, and state and setting. The chapter goes back through those layers slowly.

  1. Anger is a signal about something that matters.

    Anger usually says something is unfair, or something’s in your way, or a line has been crossed. It pushes you toward a problem so you’ll change it. It can also land on the wrong person, or come out far bigger than the thing in front of it.

    Anger moves you toward things Most unpleasant feelings make us back away. A review by Carver and Harmon-Jones argued that anger usually does the opposite: like eagerness, it pushes you toward the goal or the obstacle. That’s what makes it good at showing what matters, and risky to act on at full heat.

    Carver and Harmon-Jones (2009) · Moderate: an influential review; the approach view is still debated

    Somebody keeps eating Shabnam’s lunch from the office fridge. Week one, she assumes a mistake. Week two, she writes her name on the box in marker. Week three, the box is empty again and has been put back in the fridge, neatly, with the lid on, which is the bit that does it. Her anger has now told her very clearly that a line has been crossed, and it’s the anger, frankly, that gets her to raise it at the Monday meeting. (It was the new hire. He thought the fridge was communal. He bought her a lunch combo and looked mortified.)

    Next time you’re angry, finish this privately. “I’m angry because something I care about, which is ___, seems ___.”

    Dig deeper: what anger is for

  2. Anger often guards a softer feeling.

    Anger can arrive first because it feels stronger and safer than whatever is under it, which is often hurt, or fear, or shame, or plain exhaustion. Chapter 1 calls this layers, and the layer underneath usually tells you what you actually need.

    Frankie was an hour late home. She is sixteen. Her dad was waiting in the hall when the key went in the door and what came out of him was mostly about curfews and rules and respect, at volume. She gave as good as she got. It was only later, sitting on the stairs, that he told her about the hour itself. Standing at the front window. Phone in his hand. Checking the bus app over and over. Working out which ER. She went quiet then. “Sorry,” she said, and this time she meant it.

    You could say “I’m angry, and I think underneath I was really scared.” “I came across as angry, but I think I’m actually hurt.”

    Dig deeper: what anger can cover

  3. Anger rises in the body fast.

    A hot face, a tight jaw, a pounding heart and a voice going up are the body getting ready for a fight, and it takes seconds. When anger is high, careful thinking gets a lot harder, and listening harder still. Chapter 2 calls this being flooded.

    Halfway through an argument about the trash (whose turn, and also, it emerged, whose turn for the last four years) Meera noticed that her hands were shaking. She also realized she hadn’t heard a single thing her roommate had said for at least a minute. She’d been composing, and that was her sign. Learn your own early signs, if you can, whether it’s a tight jaw or talking faster or being very, very reasonable in a slightly high voice.

  4. In the hot minutes: slow the body first.

    When anger is high, settle your body before you try to sort anything out. Stop talking. Stop typing. Breathe out slowly, so the out-breath is longer than the in-breath. Unclench your hands. If you can, get out of the room for a bit. A large review of 154 studies by Kjærvik and Bushman (2024) found that activities which calm the body, like slow breathing, relaxation and mindfulness, reduced anger, while ones that rev you up showed little or no benefit.

    Fractions, at the kitchen table, 7:30, question four. Ben could feel his voice getting tighter with every “But why is it a half?” “I’m getting frustrated,” he said in the end, “and that’s not your fault. I’m getting a glass of water. Five minutes.” He stood at the sink, looking out at the dark backyard, and drank the whole glass. When he came back they did the rest of the sheet together, and both of them got number six wrong, confidently.

    Make the plan on a calm day. Your early sign, the words you’ll say, where you’ll go for ten minutes. Upset right now? has steps for the very worst moments.

    Dig deeper: what to do in the moment

  5. Shouting doesn’t empty it. It feeds it.

    A lot of people believe anger works like steam. Shout, slam a door, hit a pillow, and it’s out, and you’re calmer. It’s a lovely tidy idea. The research doesn’t support it. In a well-known study by Bushman (2002), angry people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them ended up angrier, and more aggressive, than people who sat quietly or thought about something else.

    Going over what happened again and again in your head has a similar effect. In studies by Bushman and colleagues (2005), people who dwelled on a provocation were later more aggressive, even toward someone who only mildly annoyed them. Anger fades faster when the body is calmed and the mind has something else to do for a while.

    After a fight with a colleague, Neville spent his lunch break telling three separate people how awful she was. Each telling got a bit better. By two o’clock he was angrier than he’d been at noon, and had a headache. The next time it happened he walked to the river and back instead and didn’t tell anyone anything, which nearly killed him. By two he could think about the actual problem. He still thinks she was out of order, and says so, but at a normal volume and to fewer people.

    Dig deeper: venting and going over it

  6. Step back and look from the outside.

    Once your body has come down a bit, try looking at the moment as if you were watching from the other side of the room. What actually happened? What might the other person have been dealing with? In experiments by Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman (2012), people who looked back on an annoying moment “like a fly on the wall,” instead of reliving it, felt less angry and acted less aggressively. Rethinking what happened, which psychologists call reappraisal (Gross, 1998), helps too.

    One way in is to write two lines about it in the third person, with your own name. “Wes got angry because…” It feels a bit silly. It works annoyingly well. Wes himself, canceled on by a friend for the third time, got as far as “Wes got angry because Matt canceled again, and Matt is…” and stopped, because Matt is looking after his dad, who has been in and out of the hospital since June. The anger didn’t go. It turned, slowly, into worry, which was at least pointing the right way.

    Dig deeper: stepping back

  7. Say it, don’t throw it.

    Anger is often a fair message sent in a hurtful way. Once you’re calmer you can still say you were angry, and why, without going for anyone, by keeping to what happened and how you felt and what you’re asking for.

    Two versions of the same fight When people wrote about a time they angered someone and a time someone angered them, the two kinds of story differed in how serious the harm seemed, how understandable it was and whether it was over. Same sort of event, told from two different places.

    Baumeister, Stillwell and Wotman (1990) · Moderate: a classic study of people’s own written memories

    “You always let me down” was what Carol wanted to say to her sister. What she actually sent, after two drafts and a cup of tea, was this. “When you canceled Mom’s appointment again I felt really angry, and on my own with it. Can we sort out who takes her for the next three months?” It’s longer, and it is also the only version that gets anyone to the hospital. Chapter 4 has more on saying hard things.

    You could say “I was angry about what happened on Monday. Can I tell you why?” “When ___ happened, I felt ___, and what I’m asking for is ___.”

  8. After anger, repair.

    Everyone gets angry, and most of us say something sharp now and then. What happens next matters a lot, because a short, real repair rebuilds trust. Name what you did, with no “but” on the end, and say what you’ll do differently.

    The blue vase from the hall table is on the floor in about nine pieces and Grandma Jean has just shouted, really shouted, and Jayden is crying. An hour later she finds him on the stairs. She sits down next to him, which takes her a while with her knees. “I shouted, and that was scary,” she says. “You matter to me far more than any vase. I’m sorry.” He leans on her shoulder for a bit, and then he asks if they can glue it, so they try. It now has a very visible crack and lives on the landing, where she says it reminds her of something. Chapter 11 has more on apologies that land.

    You could say “I was sharp with you earlier. That wasn’t fair, and I’m sorry.” “I’m still upset about what happened, and I also shouldn’t have spoken to you that way.”

    Dig deeper: repair after anger

  9. When anger becomes harm.

    Feeling angry is human. But some things done in anger are harm, whatever the feeling behind them: hitting, pushing, throwing things at or near someone, breaking their things, threatening, or shouting so someone is scared. Anger is never a reason someone deserves to be hurt.

    Being angry, even loudly, is different from intimidation. Anger becomes intimidation when these are present together: it happens again and again, it arrives when the other person disagrees or says no, it decides what happens next, and people start changing what they say and do to avoid it. A raised voice in one hard conversation that gets repaired isn’t that. Neither is being loud by nature, saying a strong feeling plainly, or crying and shouting during a meltdown. Ask yourself whether your anger passes and gets repaired, and whether the other person can still say no to you.

    For example, a man realizes his partner and children go quiet when he comes home in a bad mood. He doesn’t hit anyone, but they have started keeping out of his way, and he decides to do something about it.

    You could say “I think my anger is scaring people I love. I want help with it.”

    Dig deeper: when anger becomes harm

Your part, and only your part. You can learn your early signs, settle your body, say what made you angry in words, and repair when you get it wrong. You can’t make someone else calm down.

If you’re a teenager

Ten o’clock, and Maisie’s dad has taken her phone. Right in the middle of a group chat about Saturday. She shouts and slams her bedroom door hard enough to rattle the picture on the landing, and then lies on her bed composing speeches. The next morning, calmer and also starving, she tells him it felt like he didn’t trust her, says sorry about the door, and asks for ten minutes’ warning next time. He says fine. He also says he’d much rather hear this over breakfast than through a door, which she thinks is a bit much, but lets go.

Being angry with a parent is normal, and the fights often feel most intense in the middle teens. A rule given out of care is not control, even when it feels unfair, and a calm request the next day works more often than a slammed door.

You could say “I’m too angry to talk properly. Can we talk tomorrow?” “I don’t mind the rule so much as how it happened.” “Could we try it for two weeks and then check?”

Dig deeper: more worked examples

Two ways to read this

Anger, sent and heard

Some people say anger fast and plainly and are over it within minutes. Others go very quiet and very polite, and it can last for days. If you’re quick, the quiet person may still be furious long after you’ve moved on, so check. If you’re slow and quiet, the quick one’s outburst may be bigger in sound than in meaning. Both versions carry the same message: something mattered.

Where the fields meet. The art of debating offers a useful rule for the hot minutes: separate the claim from the person, and say the strongest version of their point first. Music explains why it so often goes wrong anyway, because tone and tempo carry more than the words, and a rising voice is heard before any sentence is.

In everyday life

Anger at yourself counts. “I’m so stupid” is anger turned inward, and it helps to talk to yourself the way you would to a friend who’d done the same thing (Chapter 7 goes further). With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day, and a time you’ll always come back. Children copy what we do with anger far more than anything we say about it. At work anger is common and rarely spoken, and “I’m frustrated about the deadline change, can we talk about what’s realistic?” keeps it useful.

If you have ADHD, anger can come fast and big and go just as fast, so plan for the speed. A three-word pause line (“Back in ten”) and a rule to wait before you send anything. If you’re managing a clash between two other people, hear each of them on their own first and then together, always about what happened and never about each other. Quite often the fight is about the setup, two people who both believe they own the same job. The expanded version has a worked example.

Common mix-ups

Anger itself isn’t a bad feeling. It’s normal and it carries information, and it’s some of the things done in anger that cause harm. Letting it out isn’t the healthy option it sounds like, because shouting and hitting things tend to keep anger going. “They made me angry, so it’s their fault I shouted” mixes up two separate things. Someone can do something unfair, and what you do next is still yours. And holding it in isn’t as safe as it looks. Anger swallowed for a long time can come out sideways, as coldness or sarcasm or a sudden explosion over a teaspoon, so saying it calmly and early is the safer road.

If you want to help someone else with this

You don’t have to stay and calm someone down. If you do stay, speak lower and slower and leave the argument until they’ve come down. Then listen for the message underneath. There’s often something fair in it, and you can take that part seriously while still saying the way it came out wasn’t okay. Something like “I want to hear this, and I can’t while we’re both shouting. Can we try again at seven?” keeps hold of both halves.
