Healing after hurt: repair, forgiveness, and when to get help

Simple versionRead the full chapter

How hurt heals, why it lingers, how small breaks get mended, what a real apology sounds like, what forgiveness is and isn’t, and when it’s time to get more help.

In short

  • Hurt is real, and healing takes time. Replaying a hurt says more about how much it mattered than about any weakness in you.
  • Repair is what counts. Every close relationship has breaks, and a real apology names what happened, owns it and offers to mend it.
  • Forgiving and going back are separate choices. You can forgive without forgetting, excusing or letting someone back in.
  • Safety comes first. You can heal even if they never say sorry, and you never owe anyone a risk to your safety.

Thursday, 2am

It was one sentence, three weeks ago, said over your shoulder in somebody’s kitchen at a party on the way to the fridge, and they have almost certainly forgotten it.

You haven’t. It plays again, word for word, tone and all, followed by every clever thing you could have said back and didn’t. You’re very tired of carrying it about, and you honestly don’t know where you’d put it down if you could.

Everyone gets hurt, and everyone hurts other people, mostly without meaning to. This chapter looks at how hurt works and how relationships mend, and at how you can heal with or without the other person, for small hurts and bigger ones.

Seen fast, a sentence from three weeks ago is three things. On top, the words, which you can still quote. Under them, what they seemed to say about you. Under that, the question of whether it will happen again. The roots are balance and signal and noise. The chapter slows down on repair.

  1. Hurt is real, and healing takes time.

    Being hurt by someone shows that something mattered, whether it was the person, or your trust in them, or your sense of being respected. The mind replays hurts because it’s trying to understand them and keep you safe from the next one. Healing rarely goes in a straight line. The hurt can come back on an anniversary, or out of nowhere, which doesn’t mean you’ve failed.

    What a hurt is about When Leary and colleagues asked people about times their feelings were hurt, the common thread was feeling less valued by someone, usually someone they knew well. A stranger’s remark stings for an afternoon. A friend’s can stay for months.

    Leary and colleagues (1998) · Moderate: based on people’s recollections; the idea is widely supported

    Months after a friend said something cruel to him at a party, Kwesi still feels a jolt whenever the song that was playing comes on the radio. It happened again on Sunday, chopping onions. He said, out loud, to nobody, “Oh, that,” turned the radio down, and carried on with the onions.

    Thoughts you could try “This hurts because it mattered.” “A feeling coming back doesn’t mean I’m back at the start.”

    Dig deeper: why hurt lingers

  2. Every close relationship has breaks. Repair is what counts.

    No relationship is smooth all the time, because people snap, forget, misread each other and let each other down. Research on parents and babies, on couples and on therapy all points the same way. Breaks are normal, and what matters most is whether they get mended. A small repair, made soon, often leaves a relationship feeling safer than before the break.

    Even therapy has ruptures Across 11 studies, repairing breaks in the relationship between therapist and client went with better results. Training therapists in repair didn’t show a clear effect on outcomes in the six studies that tested it, which is a useful bit of honesty.

    Eubanks, Muran and Safran (2018) · Strong: a meta-analysis, though the main finding is correlational

    Orange juice, a full glass, right across the breakfast table and into the toast rack. Toby’s dad shouted, not a huge shout, but enough. Twenty minutes later he sat down next to him on the sofa. “I was grumpy and I shouted. That wasn’t fair, spills happen. Want to help me make a new one?” They went and did it together. Toby poured, very slowly, with his tongue out, and slopped some, and they both pretended he hadn’t.

    Dig deeper: breaks and repair

  3. Telling someone they hurt you.

    People can’t mend what they don’t know about. Saying “that hurt” is a risk, but it gives the relationship a chance. It works best when you describe what happened and how it felt, and leave out what kind of person they are. Pick a calm moment, well away from the middle of a row.

    Amara had a stronger word ready, one she’d been working on for days. On a walk with her friend, not over the phone, she said this instead. “When you told the group about my breakup before I was ready, I felt exposed. I’d like to be the one who shares my news.” Her friend, who had honestly thought she was helping, went bright red. There was a long, awful pause next to a bench with a brass plaque on it for somebody called Maud. Then the friend said, “God. Yes. Sorry,” and they walked on. It was a bit stiff for the rest of the walk and fine by the following week.

    Before you say it, try writing it down in three short lines, one for what happened, one for how you felt and one for what you’d like.

    Dig deeper: saying “that hurt”

  4. A real apology names it, owns it, and mends it.

    “Sorry if you were upset” doesn’t heal much. A real apology says what you did, takes responsibility without excuses, shows you understand what it did to the other person, and offers to put it right or do things differently. Research, such as a study led by Roy Lewicki (2016), suggests owning it and offering repair are the parts that matter most.

    At the Tuesday meeting, Leila presented an idea she’d worked out with a colleague as if it were all hers. She saw his face. She spent the evening telling herself it didn’t matter, then the next morning went and found him at the coffee machine. “I presented our idea as mine. That wasn’t fair to you. I’ll email the team today and say it was joint work.” He said, “Okay,” and then, after a moment, “Thanks.” She sent the email at 9.40 and copied him in.

    Next time you say sorry, check it has all four parts. If it went wrong in a conversation, Rewind the talk helps you find the words, and Apology languages has more.

  5. Forgiveness isn’t forgetting, excusing or going back.

    Forgiveness is usually described as letting go of resentment and the wish to get even, and it happens inside you. Forgiving someone doesn’t mean saying what happened was okay. It doesn’t mean forgetting, or trusting them again, or keeping them in your life. It’s a choice, often a slow one, and you never owe it on anyone else’s timetable.

    Danielle forgave her father for missing most of her childhood, though it took her well into her thirties. She keeps his visits to an hour and won’t leave the children with him if he’s been drinking. He knows why. They don’t discuss it. For her the forgiving and the protecting sit side by side without much strain.

    Things to tell yourself “I can let go of wanting to get even, and still keep my limits.” “I don’t have to forgive today. I can just stop feeding the anger today.”

    Dig deeper: what forgiveness is

  6. You can heal even if they never say sorry.

    Waiting for an apology that never comes can keep you stuck for years, because the person may be gone, or unwilling, or unable to understand. Your healing doesn’t have to depend on them. You can make sense of what happened, tell your story to someone safe, and decide what it means for you now.

    Writing helps, a bit Pooling 146 randomised studies, Frattaroli found that writing about something painful had a small but real benefit on average. Small is the honest word, and it varies a good deal from person to person.

    Frattaroli (2006) · Moderate: a real but small effect that varies by person

    After the breakup, Martha wrote a letter she never sent. Four pages in biro, both sides, about what hurt and what she’d hoped for and what she was taking with her, which was mostly the good saucepans. She folded it into the back of a notebook and for a while she read it most nights, then less, and then one day in April she realised she hadn’t looked at it for a month.

    Writing works for a lot of people. Write about the hurt for fifteen minutes on three or four days in a row, the facts and the feelings both. If it ever makes you feel much worse, stop and talk to someone instead.

    Dig deeper: healing on your own

  7. Trust comes back in small, kept promises.

    After trust is broken, one big apology rarely rebuilds it. Trust grows back slowly, in lots of small moments where someone does what they said they’d do. If you broke trust, being patient and consistent matters more than a grand gesture. If you were let down, it’s fair to watch what someone does as well as what they say.

    Declan hid a credit card debt from his partner for most of a year. When it came out it was very bad for a while. After that, on the first of every month, he left his bank statement on the kitchen table without being asked, with no speech, just the statement left by the fruit bowl. About a year in, one ordinary evening, his partner said she could breathe again. He went into the garden so she wouldn’t see his face.

    Dig deeper: how trust is rebuilt

  8. Going back is a choice. Safety comes first.

    After a hurt, you can mend the relationship or change its shape. You can also step away. All three can be healthy. If someone keeps hurting you after you’ve told them, if you feel afraid of them, or if they control, threaten or put you down, the work is to protect yourself, not to repair. Counselling together isn’t advised when someone is afraid of their partner.

    Ellie’s housemate borrowed money twice and didn’t pay it back. Ellie let the first time go, because it was only a tenner and she’d been skint herself. The second time it was forty pounds for a train ticket to Leeds, and she said, “I won’t lend money again. I still want us to get on in the house.” The housemate sulked for two days and left a slightly pointed note about the recycling. They still do Sunday roast together most weeks, and nobody mentions Leeds.

    Three questions can guide what comes next: is it safe, has the hurt stopped, and do you want this relationship in your life?

    Dig deeper: limits, distance and safety

  9. Look after the hurt part of you.

    Hurt lives in the body as well as the mind, as poor sleep or a jumpy feeling that won’t settle. Healing goes better with basic care, which means rest and food and moving about, people who are kind to you, and talking to yourself the way you’d talk to a hurting friend. Some people find they grow after something painful and some don’t, and neither is a test you can fail.

    A man on the top deck of the 38 shouted at Idris and called him names, the whole way from Angel to Dalston, and nobody else said a word. For days afterwards Idris felt shaky. He told a friend. He went for long walks after work. He kept reminding himself it was about the man, and that he was safe now. Over a couple of weeks the shakiness faded, though he sat downstairs on the 38 until well into the spring.

    Pick one small act of care for today, an early night or a call to someone kind. Calm tools are in Upset right now?

    Dig deeper: healing in body and mind

Your part, and only your part. You can say what hurt, apologise for what you did, and look after your own healing. You can’t make someone apologise or change, and you don’t have to wait for them to start feeling better. Forgiveness, trust and going back are all yours to choose, in your own time.

Saturday, 11am

You wrote it all down last night, every word of it, two pages. This morning you wrote a much shorter version. When you said that, I felt small. I just wanted you to know.

You haven’t sent it. It’s sitting in your drafts between a half-finished email to the council about the bins and a shopping list that just says “eggs??”. But the two o’clock replays have gone a bit quieter since it went onto paper.

Two ways to read this

A hurt, two ways

Some people want to talk about a hurt straight away and can’t rest until it’s mended. Others need to go quiet first, sometimes for a day. If you want it now, the quiet one isn’t refusing to repair; they’re getting ready to. If you need time, the one who wants it now isn’t pushing; an open hurt is hard for them to leave alone.

Where the fields meet. Finance gives repair a plain picture: a real apology tops up a reserve of goodwill that small hurts draw down. Psychology adds the reason repairs are needed even between kind people, that intent isn’t impact.

In everyday life

Between partners, small breaks happen most days, and a quick “That came out wrong, can I try again?” mends most of them (see How to apologise so it counts). Old family hurts often need a different kind of talk from new ones, and sometimes more time apart. A friend may not know they hurt you, and “Can I tell you something that stung? I’d rather say it than drift” keeps the door open. You can even heal a hurt with someone who has died, by writing to them or talking it through with someone you trust.

  • After an affair. The shock is real for both people. Couples can recover, and some decide to part; both can be done with care. Trust returns through kept promises over months, and a couples therapist can help. More in the full chapter.
  • If you decide to separate. Part as kindly as you can. Tell children together, without blame, and never ask them to choose. Write decisions down, and consider family mediation where it’s safe. More in the full chapter.

If you want to help someone else with this

Listen before you advise. “That sounds really painful” helps far more than “You should just let it go.” Don’t rush them towards forgiveness, which can feel like being told their hurt doesn’t matter. Don’t push them to cut someone off, either; it’s their relationship and their choice, unless they’re in danger. If you were the one who caused the hurt, give a real apology and then give them time, and don’t ask them to make you feel better about it.

Words you could use “Do you want to talk it through, or would it help more to take your mind off it?” “Whatever you decide about them, I’m on your side.”

When to get more help

Some hurts are too heavy to carry alone, and getting help takes some strength. Think about talking to a doctor, counsellor or therapist if:

  • More than a month after something frightening, you still have flashbacks, nightmares, feel constantly on edge, or avoid places and people that remind you of it. These can be signs of trauma, and there are well-tested treatments for it, such as trauma-focused talking therapies.
  • The hurt is taking over: you can’t sleep, work or enjoy anything, or you’re drinking or using more to cope.
  • The hurt came from abuse or violence, now or in the past.
  • You’re having thoughts of hurting yourself or someone else. Please reach out today.

If you and someone else both want to mend things, a couples or family counsellor can help, as long as nobody is afraid of the other.
