Healing after hurt: repair, forgiveness and old hurts

Paperback textRead the expanded version

How hurt heals, why it lingers, how small breaks get mended, what a real apology sounds like, and what forgiveness is and isn’t.

In the paperbacks: Book 2, Heal and Steady, Chapter 5.

At a glance

  • Hurt is real, and healing takes time. Replaying a hurt says more about how much it mattered than about any weakness in you.
  • Repair is what counts. Every close relationship has breaks, and a real apology names what happened, owns it and offers to mend it.
  • Forgiving and going back are separate choices. You can forgive without forgetting, excusing or letting someone back in.
  • Going back is a choice. You can heal even if they never say sorry.

Thursday, 2am

It was one sentence, three weeks ago, said over your shoulder in somebody’s kitchen at a party on the way to the fridge, and they have almost certainly forgotten it.

You haven’t. It plays again, word for word, tone and all, followed by every clever thing you could have said back and didn’t. You’re very tired of carrying it about, and you honestly don’t know where you’d put it down if you could.

Everyone gets hurt, and everyone hurts other people, mostly without meaning to. This chapter looks at how hurt works and how relationships mend, and at how you can heal with or without the other person, for small hurts and bigger ones.

Seen fast, a sentence from three weeks ago is three things. On top, the words, which you can still quote. Under them, what they seemed to say about you. Under that, the question of whether it will happen again. The roots are balance and signal and noise. The chapter slows down on repair.

  1. Hurt is real, and healing takes time.

    Being hurt by someone shows that something mattered, whether it was the person, or your trust in them, or your sense of being respected. The mind replays hurts because it’s trying to understand them and keep you safe from the next one. Healing rarely goes in a straight line. The hurt can come back on an anniversary, or out of nowhere, which doesn’t mean you’ve failed.

    What a hurt is about When Leary and colleagues asked people about times their feelings were hurt, the common thread was feeling less valued by someone, usually someone they knew well. A stranger’s remark stings for an afternoon. A friend’s can stay for months.

    Leary and colleagues (1998) · Moderate: based on people’s recollections; the idea is widely supported

    Months after a friend said something cruel to him at a party, Kwesi still feels a jolt whenever the song that was playing comes on the radio. It happened again on Sunday, chopping onions. He said, out loud, to nobody, “Oh, that,” turned the radio down, and carried on with the onions.

    Thoughts you could try “This hurts because it mattered.” “A feeling coming back doesn’t mean I’m back at the start.”

    Dig deeper: why hurt lingers

  2. Every close relationship has breaks. Repair is what counts.

    No relationship is smooth all the time, because people snap, forget, misread each other and let each other down. Research on parents and babies, on couples and on therapy all points the same way. Breaks are normal, and what matters most is whether they get mended. A small repair, made soon, often keeps a break from growing, and many people say it left them trusting each other a little more.

    Even therapy has ruptures Across 11 studies, repairing breaks in the relationship between therapist and client went with better results. Training therapists in repair didn’t show a clear effect on outcomes in the studies that tested it, which is a useful bit of honesty.

    Eubanks, Muran and Safran (2018) · Strong: a meta-analysis, though the main finding is correlational

    Orange juice, a full glass, right across the breakfast table and into the butter dish. Toby’s dad shouted, not a huge shout, but enough. Twenty minutes later he sat down next to him on the couch. “I was grumpy and I shouted. That wasn’t fair, spills happen. Want to help me make a new one?” They went and did it together. Toby poured, very slowly, with his tongue out, and slopped some, and they both pretended he hadn’t.

    Dig deeper: breaks and repair

  3. Telling someone they hurt you.

    People can’t mend what they don’t know about. Saying “that hurt” is a risk, but it gives the relationship a chance. It works best when you describe what happened and how it felt, and leave out what kind of person they are. Pick a calm moment, well away from the middle of a fight.

    Amara had a stronger word ready, one she’d been working on for days. On a walk with her friend, not over the phone, she said this instead. “When you told the group about my breakup before I was ready, I felt exposed. I’d like to be the one who shares my news.” Her friend, who had honestly thought she was helping, went bright red. There was a long, awful pause next to a bench with a brass plaque on it for somebody called Maud. Then the friend said, “God. Yes. Sorry,” and they walked on. It was a bit stiff for the rest of the walk and fine by the following week.

    Before you say it, try writing it down in three short lines, one for what happened, one for how you felt and one for what you’d like.

    Dig deeper: saying “that hurt”

  4. A real apology names it, owns it, and mends it.

    “Sorry if you were upset” doesn’t heal much. A real apology says what you did, takes responsibility without excuses, shows you understand what it did to the other person, and offers to put it right or do things differently. Research, such as a study led by Roy Lewicki (2016), suggests owning it and offering repair are the parts that matter most.

    At the Tuesday meeting, Leila presented an idea she’d worked out with a colleague as if it were all hers. She saw his face. She spent the evening telling herself it didn’t matter, then the next morning went and found him at the coffee machine. “I presented our idea as mine. That wasn’t fair to you. I’ll email the team today and say it was joint work.” He said, “Okay,” and then, after a moment, “Thanks.” She sent the email at 9:40 and copied him in.

    Next time you say sorry, check it has each of the four parts. If it went wrong in a conversation, Rewind the talk helps you find the words, and Apology languages has more.

  5. Forgiveness isn’t forgetting, excusing or going back.

    Forgiveness is usually described as letting go of resentment and the wish to get even, and it happens inside you. Forgiving someone doesn’t mean saying what happened was okay. It doesn’t mean forgetting, or trusting them again, or keeping them in your life. It’s a choice, often a slow one, and you never owe it on anyone else’s schedule.

    Danielle forgave her father for missing most of her childhood, though it took her well into her thirties. She keeps his visits to an hour and won’t leave the children with him if he’s been drinking. He knows why. They don’t discuss it. For her the forgiving and the protecting sit side by side without much strain.

    Things to tell yourself “I can let go of wanting to get even, and still keep my limits.” “I don’t have to forgive today. I can just stop feeding the anger today.”

    Dig deeper: what forgiveness is

  6. You can heal even if they never say sorry.

    Waiting for an apology that never comes can keep you stuck for years, because the person may be gone, or unwilling, or unable to understand. Your healing doesn’t have to depend on them. You can make sense of what happened, tell your story to someone safe, and decide what it means for you now.

    Writing helps, a bit Pooling 146 randomized studies, Frattaroli found that writing about something painful had a small but real benefit on average. Small is the honest word, and it varies a good deal from person to person.

    Frattaroli (2006) · Moderate: a real but small effect that varies by person

    After the breakup, Martha wrote a letter she never sent. Four pages in pen, both sides, about what hurt and what she’d hoped for and what she was taking with her, which was mostly the good saucepans. She folded it into the back of a notebook and for a while she read it most nights, then less, and then one day in April she realized she hadn’t looked at it for a month.

    Writing works for a lot of people. Write about the hurt for fifteen minutes on three or four days in a row, the facts and the feelings both. If it ever makes you feel much worse, stop and talk to someone instead.

    Dig deeper: healing on your own

  7. Trust comes back in small, kept promises.

    After trust is broken, one big apology rarely rebuilds it. Trust grows back slowly, in lots of small moments where someone does what they said they’d do. If you broke trust, being patient and consistent matters more than a grand gesture. If you were let down, it’s fair to watch what someone does as well as what they say.

    Declan hid a credit card debt from his partner for most of a year. When it came out it was very bad for a while. After that, on the first of every month, he left his bank statement on the kitchen table without being asked, with no speech, just the statement left by the fruit bowl. About a year in, one ordinary evening, his partner said she could breathe again. He went into the yard so she wouldn’t see his face.

    Dig deeper: how trust is rebuilt

  8. Going back is a choice.

    After a hurt, you can mend the relationship or change its shape. You can also step away. All three can be healthy.

    Ellie’s roommate borrowed money twice and didn’t pay it back. Ellie let the first time go, because it was only ten dollars and she’d been broke herself. The second time it was fifty dollars for a train ticket to Chicago, and she said, “I won’t lend money again. I still want us to get along in the house.” The roommate went quiet for two days and left a slightly pointed note about the recycling. They still do Sunday dinner together most weeks, and nobody mentions Chicago.

    Three questions can guide what comes next. Has the hurt stopped? Has it been owned? Do you want this relationship in your life?

    It also helps to tell one hurt from a pattern of hurt. A single hurt, owned and repaired, is something most relationships survive, and many come out closer. A pattern looks different. The same hurt keeps happening after you’ve named it, apologies arrive without any change, and you notice yourself saying less to avoid the next one. One bad month isn’t a pattern, and neither is a partner who is slow to apologize but changes what they do.

    Dig deeper: limits, distance and safety

  9. Look after the hurt part of you.

    Hurt lives in the body as well as the mind, as poor sleep or a jumpy feeling that won’t settle. Healing goes better with basic care, which means rest and food and moving about, people who are kind to you, and talking to yourself the way you’d talk to a hurting friend. Some people find they grow after something painful and some don’t, and neither is a test you can fail.

    A man at the back of the 38 bus shouted at Idris and called him names, the whole way from downtown to the end of the line, and nobody else said a word. For days afterward Idris felt shaky. He told a friend. He went for long walks after work. He kept reminding himself it was about the man, and that he was safe now. Over a couple of weeks the shakiness faded, though he sat up front on the 38 until well into the spring.

    Pick one small act of care for today, an early night or time with someone kind. Calm tools are in Upset right now?

    Dig deeper: healing in body and mind

Your part, and only your part. You can say what hurt, apologize for what you did, and look after your own healing. You can’t make someone apologize or change, and you don’t have to wait for them to start feeling better. Forgiveness, trust and going back are all yours to choose, in your own time.

Saturday, 11am

You wrote it all down last night, every word of it, two pages. This morning you wrote a much shorter version. When you said that, I felt small. I just wanted you to know.

You haven’t sent it. It’s sitting in your drafts between a half-finished email to the city about trash pickup and a shopping list that just says “eggs??” But the two o’clock replays have gone a bit quieter since it went onto paper.

How old hurts shape new reactions

What happened earlier in life can make some reactions more likely now. In surveys of tens of thousands of adults in 24 countries, most had lived through at least one traumatic event (Kessler and colleagues, 2017 · Strong evidence), and many studies have linked hard childhood experiences with health and relationship problems decades later (Felitti and colleagues, 1998 · Moderate: large, retrospective, observational). That is an association across large groups, not a forecast for any one person. A mark can be left at any age.

  • In early childhood, a child who learned to watch a parent’s mood may grow into an adult who scans every face in a meeting.
  • In the school years, being shamed in front of a class can make any public correction sting.
  • In the teens, months of being left out can make a group chat that goes quiet feel like a verdict.
  • In early adulthood, a first job or first home that went badly can make the next change feel dangerous.
  • In a partnership, a betrayal can make an innocent late text feel like the start of another one.
  • As a parent, a frightening birth or a child’s illness can leave you braced for the next emergency.
  • In midlife, a layoff, a divorce or caring for a parent can wear down the reserves that used to absorb a bad day.
  • In later life, losses and illness can arrive close together, with less time to recover between them.

Most people who go through something terrible recover without lasting problems (Bonanno, 2004 · Strong evidence). For some, especially after repeated or very severe events, the body’s alarm stays switched on. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is the clinical name for one form of this. It follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, and most people who go through such events don’t develop it (Koenen and colleagues, 2017 · Strong evidence). Trauma responses can look like being on edge, startling easily, avoiding reminders, going numb, or meeting a small thing with the force of the old one. Those responses once helped someone get through. In a conversation they usually make things harder, because the person is answering the past as well as the present.

You might recognize it in yourself when a reaction feels older than the moment, arrives in your body before you can think, follows the same kind of trigger each time, or is hard to explain afterward. You might notice it in someone else when their reaction is far bigger than what just happened. These signs are for understanding. They are not a diagnosis, and you can’t diagnose yourself or anyone else from them.

Having lived through something hard is never the person’s fault. The aim is to take back control, so the old event stops running today’s reaction. That usually starts small: noticing “this feels older than today,” naming it, steadying your body, and then choosing what to do next. Trauma-focused therapies have the strongest evidence for PTSD (NICE, 2018 · Strong evidence).

Two ways to read this

Fine all day, two ways

Some people show how they feel as they go, so a hard day shows during the day. Others hold it together for hours, at work or school, and let go only once they’re somewhere safe, which is often home. If you show it as you go, the person who falls apart at home isn’t saving their worst for you. Home is where it’s safe to stop holding on. If you’re the one who holds it in, saying “I’ve used it all up today” helps the people at home read the crash for what it is.

Where the fields meet. Finance gives repair a plain picture: a real apology tops up a reserve of goodwill that small hurts draw down. Psychology adds the reason repairs are needed even between kind people, that intent isn’t impact.

In everyday life

Between partners, small breaks happen most days, and a quick “That came out wrong, can I try again?” mends most of them (see Apology languages: saying sorry so it lands). Old family hurts often need a different kind of talk from new ones, and sometimes more time apart. A friend may not know they hurt you, and “Can I tell you something that stung? I’d rather say it than drift” keeps the door open. You can even heal a hurt with someone who has died, by writing to them or talking it through with someone you trust.

  • After an affair. The shock is real for both people. Couples can recover, and some decide to part; both can be done with care. Trust returns through kept promises over months. More in the expanded version.
  • If you decide to separate. Part as kindly as you can. Tell children together, without blame, and never ask them to choose. Write decisions down, and consider family mediation. More in the expanded version.

If you want to help someone else with this

Listen before you advise. “That sounds really painful” helps far more than “You should just let it go.” Don’t rush them toward forgiveness, which can feel like being told their hurt doesn’t matter. Don’t push them to cut someone off, either; it’s their relationship and their choice. If you were the one who caused the hurt, give a real apology and then give them time, and don’t ask them to make you feel better about it.

Words you could use “Do you want to talk it through, or would it help more to take your mind off it?” “Whatever you decide about them, I’m on your side.”
