Minds that work differently

Full version 🌱 Still growing← Back to the simple version

Monday, 8:15am

“Can you just put your shoes on?” For the third time. Your child is lying on the hall floor, hands over his ears, because the new socks “feel wrong.” You’re late. He’s not being difficult. To him, the sock seam feels like sandpaper, and the alarm clock is still ringing in his head.

Most of us grow up assuming other people’s minds work roughly like our own. When someone reacts differently, we reach for explanations about character: rude, lazy, dramatic, cold. Often the real explanation is that their brain takes in and handles the world in a different way. This chapter explains what is known about autism, ADHD, sensory differences and learning differences, how philosophers have thought about difference and disability, and how all of this looks through an autistic lens. Then it turns to how different minds can live, learn and work well together.

How to read this chapter

Each section looks at one idea through three lenses, then brings them together:

  • Psychology: what researchers have found, named honestly, with a note where the evidence is thin, changing or debated. Research in this area is moving fast, and some older ideas have been overturned.
  • Philosophy: how thinkers have approached difference, normality and disability, paraphrased fairly.
  • Through an autistic lens: how the idea looks from an autistic point of view, drawing on what autistic people have written and said. It is a perspective, not a deficit, and not a rule about any one person.

Where something is the site’s own way of putting ideas into practice, it is marked This site’s idea. Nothing here can diagnose you or anyone else.

Neurodiversity and disability

Human brains vary, just as heights, voices and immune systems do. “Neurodiversity” names that variety. “Neurotypical” is often used for people whose minds work in the ways society treats as standard, and “neurodivergent” for those whose minds work differently, for example autistic people, people with ADHD, dyslexia, dyspraxia or Tourette syndrome, and others.

Psychology

Modern research describes autism, ADHD and learning differences as neurodevelopmental: differences in how the brain develops, present from early life and usually lifelong. Twin and family studies suggest that genes play a large part in autism and ADHD, alongside many other factors. There is no single “autism gene” or “ADHD gene,” and no evidence that parenting styles cause either. The once-popular claim linking autism to vaccines has been thoroughly investigated and is not supported by the evidence.

Conditions often overlap. Many autistic people also have ADHD (sometimes called AuDHD), and dyslexia, dyspraxia, anxiety and sleep differences are common alongside both. Diagnostic manuals describe these as separate categories, but real people are often a blend.

Philosophy

The word “neurodiversity” is usually credited to the Australian sociologist Judy Singer, who used it in the late 1990s, drawing on ideas already alive in autistic online communities. Writer and scholar Nick Walker later described the “neurodiversity paradigm”: the view that neurological variety is natural and valuable, and that the idea of one “normal” brain is a cultural construction, much like the idea of one normal race or gender.

The social model of disability, developed by disabled activists and named by sociologist Mike Oliver in the early 1980s, separates impairment (a difference in the body or mind) from disability (the barriers society puts in the way). A wheelchair user is disabled by stairs more than by their legs. Philosopher Elizabeth Barnes, in The Minority Body (2016), argued that being disabled is, in many cases, a way of being a minority rather than simply being worse off. Others argue that some impairments bring real suffering whatever society does. A fair reading holds both: many difficulties come from a poor fit with the world, and some needs are real and deserve support in their own right.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people describe the neurodiversity view as life-changing: it moves the story from “what is wrong with me?” to “what do I need, and what gets in my way?” Many also say autism is disabling for them in real ways, and that support, not just acceptance, matters. Both can be true. The phrase from the disability rights movement, “nothing about us without us,” sums up a widely shared request: that research, services and policies about neurodivergent people include neurodivergent people in shaping them.

Thoughts you could try: “This is hard for me. Is it me, or the setup?” “Different isn’t less, and different can still need support.”

Autism and the double empathy problem

Autism is described in diagnostic manuals by differences in social communication and interaction, along with focused interests, repetitive behaviors, a preference for routine, and sensory differences. Autistic people often describe it more simply: a different way of sensing, thinking and connecting.

Psychology

For decades, a common theory said autistic people lacked a “theory of mind,” the ability to understand others’ thoughts and feelings. That view has been widely challenged. In 2012, autistic researcher Damian Milton described the double empathy problem: when autistic and non-autistic people misunderstand each other, the gap runs both ways. Non-autistic people often find autistic people hard to read, too.

Research since then supports this. A study led by Catherine Crompton (2020) found that information passed along a chain of autistic people was shared as accurately as along a chain of non-autistic people, but accuracy dropped in mixed chains. Noah Sasson and colleagues (2017) found that non-autistic observers formed less favorable first impressions of autistic people from brief video clips, but not when they read only what was said. In other words, part of the “social difficulty” lives in how others react to autistic people’s style, not only in autistic people themselves.

Many autistic people also experience alexithymia, difficulty identifying and describing their own feelings. Research by Geoffrey Bird and Richard Cook (2013) suggested that some empathy differences once blamed on autism may be linked to alexithymia instead, which also occurs in many non-autistic people.

Philosophy

In a famous 1974 essay, philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat. His point was that we can know facts about a bat’s echolocation, yet still not know what that experience is like from the inside. Something similar happens between different human minds. A non-autistic person can learn facts about sensory overload without knowing what it is like, and the reverse is true too. That humility, recognizing the limits of your own imagination, is a good starting point for empathy that runs both ways.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people value directness and honesty, and find hints, white lies and unspoken rules confusing or exhausting. Saying exactly what you mean is, for many, a form of respect: it trusts the other person with the truth. Autistic people often connect deeply around shared interests (“parallel play” and “info-dumping” are terms some use with affection), rather than through small talk. Many describe feeling others’ distress intensely, sometimes to the point of overwhelm, while not showing it in expected ways. The picture of the cold, uncaring autistic person is a stereotype that many autistic people find painful and untrue.

Words you could use: “When I say something plainly, I’m not being rude. I’m trying to be clear.” “Could you tell me directly? I miss hints.”

ADHD, time and motivation

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is diagnosed by persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that affect daily life. The name is a little misleading. Many people with ADHD describe not a shortage of attention but difficulty steering it.

Psychology

Psychologist Russell Barkley has described ADHD mainly as a difference in executive function, the brain’s self-management system: planning, starting tasks, holding information in mind, managing time and regulating emotions. He has also emphasized “time blindness”: difficulty sensing the passage of time and acting for future rewards. Many people with ADHD also hyperfocus, becoming absorbed for hours in something that interests them. Research on brain reward systems suggests that interest, novelty, urgency and challenge play a larger role in switching attention on for people with ADHD.

ADHD often continues into adulthood, though it may look different: less running around, more inner restlessness, disorganization or emotional ups and downs. Girls and women have historically been under-diagnosed, partly because they are more often inattentive than visibly hyperactive. Emotional intensity is common in ADHD. “Rejection sensitive dysphoria” is a term used by some clinicians for intense pain at perceived rejection; it isn’t a formal diagnosis, and research on it is limited, but many people with ADHD say it describes their experience.

Medication, coaching, therapy and environmental changes can all help. Research supports medication as effective for many people, with benefits and side effects that vary person to person.

Philosophy

William James, the philosopher and psychologist, wrote in the 1890s that what we pay attention to shapes our experience and, over time, our lives. He also saw attention as partly voluntary and partly drawn by interest. ADHD can be seen as a mind where interest has much more say. The French philosopher Simone Weil described attention as a rare and pure form of generosity. That is worth remembering when someone with ADHD gives their full, absorbed attention to something they love, or to someone they love.

Through an autistic lens

Autism and ADHD often occur together, and they can pull in different directions: one part wanting routine and sameness, another craving novelty and stimulation. Many AuDHD people describe this as a constant negotiation. Both autistic and ADHD minds can be “interest-led,” and both can find switching tasks hard. Where they differ, an autistic person may need predictability to feel safe, while an ADHD person may need variety to stay engaged. A home or workplace that offers both, steady routines with room for novelty, can suit many.

Words you could use: “I’m not ignoring you. I didn’t hear you because I was locked into this. Can you tap my shoulder next time?”

Senses, overload and needs

We usually think of five senses, but our brains also process balance, body position (proprioception) and signals from inside the body like hunger, heartbeat and needing the toilet (interoception). People differ a great deal in how strongly they register each one.

Psychology

Occupational therapist Winnie Dunn developed a widely used model (1997) describing how people differ in their sensory “thresholds” and in how they respond. Some people notice everything and are easily overwhelmed (sensitive or avoiding). Others need more input to register it, and either seek it out or miss it (seeking or low registration). Most people have a mix across different senses.

Psychologists Elaine and Arthur Aron (1997) described “sensory processing sensitivity,” a temperament trait found in many people, autistic or not, in which people process sensory and emotional information deeply. Sensory differences are now part of the formal description of autism, and are common in ADHD too. Many autistic people report differences in interoception, such as noticing hunger, thirst or pain late or very strongly, though research findings here are mixed.

Philosophy

The phenomenologist Maurice Merleau-Ponty argued that we don’t experience the world as minds floating above bodies; we experience it through the body. Perception isn’t a camera; it’s an active, bodily engagement. That makes sensory differences more than a detail. If your body meets the world differently, your world is, in a real sense, different.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people describe sensory overload as one of the most disabling parts of daily life: fluorescent lights that flicker, clothes that scratch, crowds, overlapping conversations, strong smells. Overload can lead to meltdowns (an outward loss of control) or shutdowns (going quiet and withdrawn). Neither is a tantrum or a choice. Many autistic people also describe great sensory joy: the texture of a favorite fabric, the pattern of light on water, a piece of music played again and again. Stimming, repetitive movements or sounds such as rocking, flapping or humming, is often a way of regulating and expressing feeling. Unless it causes harm, there’s usually no reason to stop it.

Words you could use: “I need to step outside for five minutes. It’s the noise, not the company.” “Is the music too loud for you? We can turn it down.”

Spiky profiles and learning tools

Learning differences are specific: they affect particular skills, such as reading, writing, number sense or coordination, while leaving others untouched or even strong.

Psychology

Dyslexia mainly affects reading and spelling. Much research, including work associated with Sally Shaywitz, links it to differences in processing the sounds of language (phonological processing). It is not a problem with vision or intelligence. Structured, phonics-based teaching helps many dyslexic readers. Dyscalculia affects number sense and arithmetic. Dyspraxia, also called developmental coordination disorder, affects planning and coordinating movement, and often organization. Developmental language disorder affects understanding and using language. All are common, and all are under-recognized, especially in adults.

A “spiky profile” describes the uneven pattern many neurodivergent people have: very strong in some areas, very challenged in others. This is why someone can be gifted and struggling at once, and why intelligence tests that give one score can hide a lot.

Philosophy

Philosopher Martha Nussbaum, with economist Amartya Sen, developed the capabilities approach: the idea that justice is about what people are actually able to do and be, not just what resources they nominally have. A library full of books doesn’t give a dyslexic child the capability to read, unless it also offers audiobooks and good teaching. Fairness, on this view, isn’t treating everyone identically. It’s making sure everyone can actually reach what matters.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people have spiky profiles too: perhaps reading early and fluently, yet finding handwriting or spoken instructions very hard. Some are hyperlexic, reading well beyond their age. Many learn best from clear, written, structured information, and struggle with group work, noisy classrooms or vague instructions like “just have a go.” Autistic learners often do best when they can follow their interests deeply. Using a special interest as a doorway into other subjects can be one of the most effective teaching tools there is.

Words you could use: “Can you write that down for me? I take it in better written.” “I learn best when I can see the whole plan first.”

Masking, energy and burnout

Masking, sometimes called camouflaging, means hiding or compensating for neurodivergent traits to fit in. It can be deliberate or so practiced it becomes automatic.

Psychology

Laura Hull and colleagues (2017) interviewed autistic adults about camouflaging and described it as a mix of masking (hiding traits), compensating (using learned strategies like scripts and rules for eye contact) and assimilating (trying to blend in). People described it as exhausting and said it could leave them unsure of who they really were. Later studies have linked high camouflaging with more anxiety and depression, though cause and effect are hard to untangle.

Dora Raymaker and colleagues (2020), in research led by autistic and non-autistic researchers together, described autistic burnout: long-term exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimulation, resulting from chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities without enough support. It is different from work burnout, though they can overlap. People described recovery as needing rest, reduced demands and the chance to unmask.

Christine Miserandino’s “spoon theory” (2003) came from living with lupus, not neurodivergence, but many neurodivergent people use it: each day starts with a limited number of “spoons” of energy, and every task uses some. Masking uses a lot.

Philosophy

The sociologist Erving Goffman described everyday life as a kind of performance, with a “front stage,” where we present ourselves to others, and a “backstage,” where we can relax. Everyone does some of this. For people who mask, the front stage is bigger and more effortful, and the backstage may be very small. Philosophers of authenticity, from Jean-Jacques Rousseau to the existentialists, have asked what it means to be true to oneself. For many neurodivergent people this isn’t abstract: it’s a daily trade-off between safety and selfhood.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people, especially those diagnosed late, say they had masked for so long that they didn’t know which parts were really them. Unmasking is often gradual, and safe only in some places. It isn’t a duty, and masking isn’t lying. It’s often a survival strategy in places where being openly different has had costs. What helps is having somewhere, and someone, where the mask can come off. Recovery time after social events isn’t antisocial; it’s maintenance.

Words you could use: “I can do the party, and I’ll need a quiet Sunday after.” “With you, I don’t have to perform. That means a lot.”

Monotropism, flow and transitions

Some minds spread attention across many things at once. Others focus deeply on a few. Neither is better. They suit different tasks.

Psychology

Monotropism is a theory developed by Dinah Murray, Wenn Lawson and Mike Lesser (published in 2005), with autistic researchers among its authors. It suggests that autistic minds tend to focus attention intensely on a small number of interests at a time, while non-autistic minds tend to spread attention more widely (“polytropism”). Monotropism may explain several autistic experiences at once: deep expertise and joy in interests, difficulty switching tasks, missing things outside the focus, and distress when interrupted. It is a theory, increasingly studied but not yet fully tested, and many autistic people find it fits their experience well.

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described flow, the state of being completely absorbed in a challenging, meaningful task. Many neurodivergent people describe flow as easy to fall into on their own terms and hard to leave.

“Autistic inertia” is a term many autistic people use for difficulty starting, stopping or switching activities, even ones they want to do. Research on it is still early.

Philosophy

Aristotle thought the best kind of life involved activity done for its own sake, especially contemplation, which he saw as the most absorbing and self-sufficient of activities. Many traditions honor deep, single-minded focus: the craftsperson, the scholar, the monk. A monotropic mind can be seen in that light, not as narrow, but as capable of a depth that busy, scattered attention rarely reaches.

Through an autistic lens

Interruptions can feel like being yanked out of a warm bath into cold air. Clear warnings help: “in ten minutes,” then “in five,” then a gentle cue. So do visual timers, predictable routines and transition objects or rituals. Many autistic people also need a wind-down after deep focus, like a diver coming up slowly. Respecting focus, and planning around it, is one of the simplest ways to reduce conflict at home and at work.

Words you could use: “Can you give me a ten-minute warning before we leave?” “I’m deep in something. Can I get back to you at four?”

Adjustments that help everyone

When people with different minds share a home, a classroom or a workplace, good things come from adjusting on both sides, clearly and without blame.

Psychology

Research on workplace and classroom adjustments suggests many are low-cost and effective: written instructions, quiet spaces, flexible hours, noise-cancelling headphones, regular check-ins, clear deadlines and remote options. In many countries the law requires reasonable adjustments for disabled people, which can include neurodivergent people. The evidence for specific adjustments varies, but asking the person what helps them, and then reviewing it, is a widely recommended starting point.

The curb-cut effect, a phrase popularized by Angela Glover Blackwell (2017), describes how changes made for one group, like dropped curbs for wheelchair users, often help many others too: people with prams, suitcases or bicycles. Universal design for learning applies the same idea to teaching: build in options from the start, so fewer people need special arrangements.

Philosophy

John Rawls proposed imagining a “veil of ignorance”: designing society without knowing which person you’ll be in it. If you didn’t know whether you’d be autistic, dyslexic or neurotypical, what kind of school, workplace or family rules would you choose? Probably ones that worked for many kinds of mind. Eva Feder Kittay, a philosopher and the mother of a disabled daughter, argues that dependency and care are part of every human life, not a special case, so a fair society builds care in rather than treating it as charity.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people say the single most helpful adjustment is clear, explicit communication: say what you mean, say what you expect, say when. It costs very little, and it helps many non-autistic people too. Another is predictability: knowing plans in advance, and being told about changes as early as possible. A third is being allowed to meet needs without explaining them every time. The double empathy idea is a reminder that adjustment is two-way: autistic people also adapt constantly, and it is fair to ask the non-autistic side to meet them partway.

This site’s idea: a one-page “how I work best” card, like the site’s wiring card, that anyone can fill in and share: how I like to be told things, what drains me, what helps me settle, and what I need before a change.

Words you could use: “Here’s how I work best. What about you?” “Could we agree that plans change only with a day’s notice, unless it’s an emergency?”

Diagnosis, identity and words

Labels can open doors to understanding, support and community. They can also be misused, as insults or as limits. How they are used matters as much as whether they are used.

Psychology

Many adults, especially women and people of color, receive autism or ADHD diagnoses later in life, after years of being misunderstood. Research and personal accounts suggest that adult diagnosis often brings relief and self-understanding, along with grief for earlier struggles. Access to assessment varies widely by country and income, and waiting lists can be long. Some people identify as neurodivergent without a formal diagnosis. Many autistic communities accept self-identification, while noting that formal diagnosis can be needed for legal protection and services.

On language, a UK study by Lorcan Kenny and colleagues (2016) found that “autistic” and “on the autism spectrum” were preferred by many autistic adults, while professionals more often used “person with autism.” Preferences differ between people and countries. The safest approach is to ask, and to follow the person’s own choice.

Philosophy

Philosopher Ian Hacking described the looping effect of human categories: when people are classified, the classification changes how they see themselves and how others treat them, which in turn changes the category. Autism and ADHD are good examples: definitions have shifted many times, partly because of how people with these labels have responded to and reshaped them. That doesn’t make the differences unreal. It reminds us that labels are human tools, and their meaning is still being written, increasingly by neurodivergent people themselves.

Through an autistic lens

Many autistic people describe their diagnosis as the moment their life made sense. Others feel it was used against them. Most agree on one thing: a label should open understanding, never close it. “You’re so autistic” said in an argument, or “everyone has a bit of ADHD” said to dismiss real struggles, hurts. Asking “what does this mean for you?” respects the person behind the word.

Words you could use: “What does being autistic mean for you, day to day?” “I’d rather you didn’t use my diagnosis to win an argument. Can we talk about what actually happened?”

Putting it together

Minds differ, and much of the pain around those differences comes from misreading them as character. Psychology now describes many differences in two-way terms: the double empathy problem, sensory profiles, spiky skill sets. Philosophy offers ways to think about fairness for different minds: the social model, capabilities, the veil of ignorance. The autistic lens, and the lens of other neurodivergent people, reminds us that understanding starts with listening to people describe their own experience.

The threads that run through the chapter:

  • Behavior is information. A meltdown, a forgotten task or a blunt remark often has a reason worth finding.
  • Energy is limited. Masking, overload and switching all use it. Rest and quiet are part of the plan.
  • Clear is kind. Saying what you mean, giving times and writing things down helps nearly everyone.
  • Adjust both ways. Different minds meet in the middle when both move.

Your part, and only your part

You can’t rewire your brain or anyone else’s. You can learn how yours works, say what helps, ask what helps others, and choose environments and people that fit you. If you’re always the one adjusting, it’s fair to say so, and fair to spend more time where you can be yourself. Part One covers this in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

Worked examples

On your own. At 34, Aisha reads about ADHD in women and recognizes herself: the lost keys, the half-finished projects, the hyperfocus, the shame. She writes down what she’s noticed and books an appointment. While she waits, she starts using alarms, a launch pad by the door, and body doubling (working alongside a friend on video). She feels less broken and more curious.

Partners. Ben is autistic; his partner Chloe isn’t. Chloe says “we should go out sometime,” meaning “I miss you.” Ben hears a vague future plan. Chloe feels unheard; Ben feels confused. They agree to say the feeling and the ask plainly: “I miss you. Could we go out on Friday?” Ben puts it in the calendar right away, and Chloe learns that’s his way of saying yes, warmly.

Parent and child. The sock-seam morning from the opening becomes a routine: seamless socks, clothes laid out the night before, a five-minute warning before shoes, and the alarm clock moved out of the hall. The mornings aren’t perfect, but the shouting stops.

Coworkers. Sam, who is dyslexic, struggles with long emails. His manager starts sending bullet points and a short voice note for anything complex. Sam’s work improves, and so does everyone else’s inbox.

Friends. Rosa, who has ADHD, often cancels at the last minute when overwhelmed. Her friend Lily, who is autistic, finds last-minute changes very hard. They talk it through: Rosa will check in the morning of a plan, and if she needs to cancel, she’ll do it by noon. Lily keeps a backup plan she enjoys alone, so a cancellation doesn’t wreck her day.

Housemates. Three students share a flat. One needs quiet to sleep, one needs music to focus, one needs the kitchen tidy to feel calm. They write a short house agreement covering quiet hours, headphones, and a five-minute tidy after cooking. See Sharing a room.

Community. A local library adds a “quiet hour” with dimmed lights and no announcements. It was suggested by autistic families. Older visitors, people with migraines and tired parents all start coming too.

Exercises

1. Your sensory map

For each sense (sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement, inside-body signals), write one thing that drains you and one that calms you. Pick one small change for your home or workspace.

2. Your “how I work best” card

Write four lines: how I like to be told things; what drains me; what helps me settle; what I need before a change. Share it with one person, and ask for theirs. The wiring card can help.

3. The energy budget

For one week, mark each activity as draining, neutral or restoring. Notice where masking or overload happens. Plan one recovery block after the most draining part of your week.

4. The plain-words swap

Think of one hint you often give (“the bins are full”). Rewrite it as a clear request with a time (“could you take the bins out tonight?”). Try it this week.

5. The transition plan

For one daily switch that causes friction (leaving the house, stopping a game, starting work), design a warning, a cue and a small reward or ritual. Try it for five days.

Questions people often ask

Can I be autistic or have ADHD without knowing?

Yes. Many adults find out later in life. If you recognize yourself in this chapter, it may be worth reading more and talking to a doctor. Only a qualified professional can diagnose, but your own observations are a valid place to start.

Isn’t everyone a little bit autistic, or a little bit ADHD?

Everyone has some of the traits sometimes. A diagnosis describes a lasting pattern, present across life and affecting daily living. The phrase can make real struggles sound small, so it’s kinder to avoid it.

Should I tell my employer or school?

That’s your choice. Telling can open the way to adjustments and legal protection in many countries; some people prefer to ask for specific adjustments without naming a diagnosis. At work has more.

My partner is neurodivergent and I feel I do all the adjusting. Is that fair?

Meeting in the middle should be two-way. It’s fair to name your own needs, too. Neurodivergent relationships has ideas for both sides, including when one person feels like the “manager.”

Are meltdowns just tantrums?

No. A tantrum is usually aimed at getting something and can stop when that happens. A meltdown is an overwhelmed nervous system losing control, and it needs safety, quiet and time, not consequences.

How do I support a neurodivergent child without making them feel different?

Talk about differences openly and positively, as part of how all people vary. Build on their strengths and interests, give clear routines, and let them meet other neurodivergent people. When a child has ADHD has more.

Limits, safety and when to get more help

  • This chapter teaches ideas. It can’t diagnose you or anyone else, and it isn’t a substitute for assessment or support from qualified professionals.
  • If you, your child or someone you care for has ongoing difficulties, a doctor, school special educational needs staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment.
  • Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common in neurodivergent people. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or find a helpline at findahelpline.com.
  • A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. Neurodivergent people face higher risks of being taken advantage of. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? has free, private help, including signs to look out for.
