Stress and the nervous system: the ladder, flooding, rest and recovery

Paperback textRead the expanded version

What stress does to your body and your mind, why kind people say things they don’t mean when they’re flooded, and which kinds of rest actually put something back.

In the paperbacks: Book 2, Heal and Steady, Chapter 1.

Monday, 7:52am

The bus is late. One school shoe is missing, last seen under the sofa on Saturday. Your phone buzzes with a message from work that begins “Quick one,” which it never is. From the hall your partner calls, “Have you seen my keys?” and you hear yourself shout, “I’m not your secretary!”

A second later you’re standing there wondering where that came from. It wasn’t the keys. You were behind before anyone had said a word, before the shoe went missing, probably before you were even out of bed.

Feelings got their own chapter (Chapter 1). This one goes a layer down, to the nervous system underneath them, and what it does when the pressure’s on. Knowing how stress works won’t make Monday any quieter. It does make your own reactions, and other people’s, a lot less mysterious.

It also explains why some of the tools elsewhere work at all. The stress tank in Part Two, Chapter III asks how full you are before you judge the moment, because stress piles up from small loads. Calm-down plan in Part One, Now settles the body before any words, and it asks you to name a time to come back, so a pause doesn’t look like walking out. And the setup score in Part Two, Chapter II counts stress as well as hours, since a split can look fair on paper and still leave one person running on empty.

In quick strokes: the surface is a missing shoe. Underneath it, a body already in alarm before anyone spoke. Underneath that, a week with no recovery in it. The roots are limited capacity and state and setting. Now one at a time, starting with the body.

  1. Stress is your body getting ready.

    Something needs dealing with, and your body gears up before you’ve decided anything. Heart faster, breath quicker, muscles tight, stress hormones up. Which is exactly what you want when a glass is sliding off the table. The trouble starts when the body stays geared up for hours and never gets the message to stand down.

    Morag is a nurse. She handles a cardiac arrest on the ward calmly and well. She gets home at nine, eats half a sandwich standing up at the counter, and at 10:30 finds herself sobbing at an ad for car insurance. Car insurance has never moved her to anything before. Her body is still running the emergency it thinks she’s in.

    So after something stressful, give your body a clear sign that it’s over, whether that’s a walk around the block or a hot shower or one song played much too loud in the car on the way home. The body takes its cue from what you do far more than from what you tell it.

  2. Stress depends on what you have to meet it.

    The same deadline lands on two colleagues on a Thursday afternoon, and one of them has a quiet evening ahead and a takeout menu. The other has a seven-year-old at home with a fever and a partner away in Chicago till Saturday. On paper it’s one task. Guess which of them is awake at 3am.

    Stress grows when a demand looks bigger than what you’ve got to meet it with, whether that’s time or energy or money or help or sleep. That’s why a small thing tips you over on a bad day. It’s also why giving someone more support can shrink their stress without changing the job at all. When something feels huge, I’d write two lists side by side, one for what it asks of you and one for what you’ve got. Then ignore the first list for a minute and ask what would make the second one a line longer.

    You could say “It’s less the task than how little I’ve got left today.” “What would make this feel more doable?”

  3. The ladder: three everyday states.

    The ladder is a picture of the states your body moves between in a day. Near the bottom you’re calm and connected, breathing easily, able to hear something difficult without bristling. Higher up you’re revved up, tense, and small things start to feel urgent or personal. Then there’s running on empty, which is low and slow, with the words just out of reach.

    Most people go up and down it many times a day. Derek watches his granddaughter on Wednesdays, and at two o’clock he’s calm and connected and they’re doing a jigsaw. By four she has found the markers and the wall, and he’s revved up. By six, when her mom comes, he is sitting on the kitchen floor eating a fish stick off her plate and can’t remember the word for colander. Neither of them had changed, only the hour, and how much afternoon was left in him.

    My weather today is a one-minute check that gives you a best guess at where you are (its sky step is the ladder). If you’d rather not use a screen, set three reminders on your phone for today, and each time one goes off, notice which rung you’re on. Don’t do anything about it, just notice.

  4. When you’re flooded, thinking goes offline.

    At the top of the stress curve your body is in full alarm, and the things a hard conversation needs most (careful thinking, listening, seeing the other side) get much harder to reach. Lots of people call this being flooded. You might say something you don’t mean, or freeze, or want nothing in the world except to be out of the room.

    Two brothers start clearing their dad’s house one wet Saturday in March. Within twenty minutes they are shouting at each other about a floor lamp. Neither of them likes the lamp. Neither of them wants it. One of them says “Fine, take it then!” and the other says “I don’t want it!” and they stand there either side of it, breathing hard, while the actual subject sits in the boxes all around them and nobody says it.

    A flooded person is still a decent person whose body has to come down before the talk can go anywhere. Know your own flood signs in advance. Heat in the face is a common one. So is a racing heart, or the room going narrow, or a voice in your head that has gone very loud and very certain. Two at once is your cue to stop. Then say so in plain words, something like “I’m too flooded to listen well. Can we stop for twenty minutes and come back at four?”

    Dig deeper: flooding

  5. Stress adds up, like a tank filling.

    Picture a tank holding everything you’re already carrying today. The traffic goes in. So does the meeting that overran, and the noisy store, and the text from your mom that just says “Call me when you can.” It’s all one tank, and by the evening something tiny makes it overflow and gets the blame for the whole lot.

    Hassles beat headlines In a classic study, everyday hassles predicted how people were doing better than major life events did, even once the big events were taken into account. Which is why a missing shoe can be the thing that tips you over.

    Kanner and colleagues (1981) · Moderate: a classic study; many later studies agree

    Asked to empty the dishwasher, Kyle, who is fifteen, explodes. Really explodes, a whole speech about how he does everything in this house. It’s days later, from a friend’s mom at school pickup, that his own mom finds out his closest friendship fell apart at lunchtime that Monday, over something said in a group chat. The dishwasher was just the next thing anyone asked of him.

    Small daily hassles count as much as big events here, which surprises people. Before you react to a small thing in the evening, ask how full you were before it happened. How much are you carrying? is five quick questions, and Part Two, Chapter III uses it to tell old stress from new.

  6. Settle the body first.

    You can’t think your way out of a body in alarm. You can help the body come down, though, and the thinking usually follows. Slow breathing with long out-breaths works for a lot of people. So does moving, or humming something you know by heart, or steady pressure like a heavy blanket, or simply leaving the noisy room. What works is personal, which is why it’s worth finding your own short list on a calm day and not in the middle of a fight.

    Before she has to give some difficult feedback, Bridie feels her heart going. She walks once around the staff parking lot, breathing out slowly past the dumpsters and the smokers’ shelter, and goes in a little steadier, if not exactly calm. Put your list on your phone. The Calm-down plan helps you build it. If you only keep one line from this section, keep “body first, words second.”

  7. Rest isn’t one thing.

    An hour on the couch scrolling can leave you more tired than when you sat down. That’s because recovery comes in different kinds, and sleep is only one of them, alongside switching off from work in your head, real relaxation, doing something absorbing that you enjoy, and the plain feeling of being in charge of your own time for once. None of them stands in for the others.

    Four kinds of recovery Researchers who study unwinding after work separate four things: switching off in your head, relaxing, doing something absorbing that stretches you a little, and being in charge of your own time. An evening that gives you none of the four isn’t much of a rest, however long you sit down.

    Sonnentag and Fritz (2007) · Strong: a widely used measure; recovery research is large and consistent

    Two roommates have friends over on a Saturday for a takeout and a game show on TV. One wakes up on Sunday buzzing. The other spends the whole of Sunday in her room with a library book and the door shut, and comes out at dinnertime feeling human again. Company fills some people up and drains others, and neither of them is doing rest wrong.

    Ask yourself which kind of tired you are, whether it’s your body or your brain, or people, or feelings, then pick a rest that matches rather than the one you always go for. Brain Breakers is one kind, if you like that sort of thing.

  8. Sleep and stress feed each other.

    Stress makes sleep harder, and poor sleep makes stress land harder. After a short or broken night small things feel bigger, tempers are shorter, and people pull away from each other. This one is well studied. In a brain-scan study by Yoo and colleagues (2007), one night without sleep made the brain’s alarm center react more strongly to upsetting pictures.

    In a daily study by Gordon and Chen (2014), couples reported more conflict after nights of poor sleep. In experiments by Ben Simon and Walker (2018), people who had missed a night’s sleep kept others at a greater distance and felt lonelier.

    Saoirse comes off her third night shift at the nursing home and bursts into tears in the parking lot over a parking ticket. Her sister, on speakerphone, says, “You’ve had no sleep, leave it, we’ll deal with it Thursday.” By Thursday it is a parking ticket again, annoying and payable, and she pays it online on her lunch break between a granola bar and a yogurt.

    You can’t always fix sleep, but you can notice when it’s part of the story. Before a hard conversation, ask whether you’ve both slept, and if not, pick another time. At night, do the same few calm things before bed in the same order, so your body learns the signal.

    You could say “I slept badly, so if I’m short with you today, that’s why.” “Can we talk about this after we’ve both slept?”

  9. Stress is easier to carry when it’s shared.

    People calm each other down. A steady voice, a hand on the shoulder, or just a quiet person in the same room can help a nervous system settle. Saying your state out loud helps too, because “I’m stressed about work” stops your partner wondering if it’s about them.

    Fish sticks again, Tuesday. Kwasi says to his ten-year-old, without quite looking at him, “I’m a bit stressed about money this month. Sorting it is my grown-up job, and you’re not in trouble.” His son nods and carries on eating. Nothing else is said. Over the next few days the boy stops tiptoeing around him, and on Friday he brings his math homework to the table again and asks for help with it, which he hasn’t done since school break.

Six minutes later your partner is by the front door, still patting their pockets. “Sorry,” you say. “I was full up before you opened your mouth.” And then, after a moment, “Bathroom shelf, next to the toothpaste.” They laugh, and ask if it’s been a bad start. The bus is still late and the shoe is still missing.

Two ways to read this

A full tank, two ways

For some people, stress shows up loudly: a sharp voice, a slammed cupboard. For others it goes inward and quiet, and the first sign anyone sees is a person who has stopped answering. If you get loud, the quiet one may be just as full, only less visible. If you go quiet, the loud one is probably not angry with you. They are usually full, and it’s coming out sideways.

Where the fields meet. Neurobiology’s idea is that your state sets the volume, and stress left over from earlier still counts. Economics says the same thing in money terms: a household or a person running at full stretch has no margin left for an ordinary bad day.

In everyday life

Living on your own means nobody’s there to notice when you’re running on empty, so build rest into the week before you need it. With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day, plus the rule that whoever pauses names a time to come back (One of you wants to talk now, the other needs space has more on this). A child can’t settle until the nearest grown-up does. Your calm is something they borrow for years before they can make their own. At work, a flooded colleague can’t take in feedback, and waiting an hour usually beats pushing on.

Some people run on empty for reasons built into their lives, like shift work, a long-term illness or caring for someone. What that needs is a different setup. More effort won’t do it (see Different hours and Caregivers). If you have ADHD, just keeping track of everything can fill the tank before breakfast. Get the remembering out of your head and into the world, with labeled alarms and one hook for the keys, and plan hard talks for your better time of day. More in the expanded version.

Common mix-ups

People often think all stress is bad, and it isn’t. Short bursts help you rise to something, and the harm comes from stress that never gets a recovery. Being calm doesn’t mean not being upset, either. You can be calm and sad, or calm and extremely firm. Pausing gets mistaken for avoiding, but a pause with a time to come back is how you finish a talk well. Avoiding is the pause that never comes back. Rest gets called lazy, though bodies and minds that never recover tend to break. And short replies with a flat face get read as not caring, when far more often they mean someone is worn out.

If you want to help someone else with this

Nobody in the history of the world has calmed down because they were told to. A lower voice, slower words and a steady presence do far more. Taking one job off someone’s list beats any pep talk. Ask what kind of help they’d like (practical help, company, distraction, some space) instead of guessing. Don’t argue with someone who’s flooded. Offer a break and a time to come back, because the facts will still be there in twenty minutes. And if someone has been running on empty for weeks, say so kindly and take one job off their list.

You could say “You seem really stretched. Is there one thing I could take off your plate?” “We don’t have to sort this now. Shall we pick it up after dinner?”
