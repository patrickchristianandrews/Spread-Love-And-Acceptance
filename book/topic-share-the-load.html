Each chapter is cut down to just this topic, in two versions: On your own, for what one person can see and do alone, and With others, for partners, family, roommates and coworkers. Preface: the work nobody sees

Running a life takes a lot of work nobody sees. Much of it is thinking, not doing: noticing, remembering, planning, checking that something happened, and smoothing over tense moments. A job is more than the few minutes it takes to do it. Someone carries it in their head first.

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing it. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember, like the appointments, the restocking and the smoothing-over, and write them down for yourself.

  • Count it, don’t charge it. The point is to see your real load, so you can plan your energy. It isn’t a bill.
  • Notice which jobs are yours by choice, and which just landed on you.
  • Pick one job you could hand back, drop or do less often, and say so kindly.

We all remember our own effort best. So while you count yours, notice the work others do that you may have missed, too.

The book groups the unseen work into a few kinds: noticing (the milk is low), remembering (the dentist is Thursday), planning (who drives where), checking (did the form go in?) and smoothing (calming a tense moment, or keeping the peace at a family dinner). Doing a job takes minutes. Holding it in your head can take all week.

A single dad’s week

He thought his week was “work, kids, sleep.” His list shows thirty-one small jobs nobody asked him to do: lunch money, a birthday present for a party, his mother’s prescription, the shared calendar with his ex. He can’t hand most of them to anyone, but seeing them explains why he’s tired, and he drops two: home-baked cakes for school events, and ironing. See on my own.

Words you could use (to yourself): “That’s a job, even though it was only in my head.” “I’m counting this so I can plan, not so I can complain.”

Try this: At the end of each day this week, add the jobs you only thought about. Put a star by any you’d gladly let go of.

Every shared home has work nobody sees: noticing the milk is low, remembering the dentist, planning the week, checking things happened, and calming a tense moment. When it’s done well, nothing seems to happen. So the other person usually isn’t ignoring it. They just haven’t seen it.

  • Each of you writes only your own side. For one week, each person logs what they did. Nobody fills in anything for anyone else.
  • Read the lists together on a calm evening. Each of you says one thing that surprised you, and gives one specific thank-you.
  • Decide one small change, and when you’ll look again.

Writing it down isn’t keeping score. Scorekeeping looks back and asks who owes whom. A shared list looks forward and asks what to change next week. The goal isn’t a perfect 50/50. It’s a split you can both see and both agree to. If the other person doesn’t want to join in, you can still share your own list as information, not as a bill, and let their answer be theirs.

The unseen work looks different in every home:

  • Three or more partners: the person who keeps the shared calendar often carries the most invisible work, simply because there are more people to fit together. See more than two partners.
  • Housemates: someone always notices the bin is full or the bills are due. Name it at the first house meeting. See roommates and housemates.
  • Co-parents across two homes: school forms, clothes that went to the wrong house, and the kids’ feelings about the switch all need someone holding them. See co-parenting.
  • Adult siblings caring for a parent: the sibling who lives closest often ends up as the default. Listing the jobs can turn guilt into a plan. See caregivers.
  • Business partners: chasing payments, keeping clients happy and noticing problems early are real work, even when they don’t show on a timesheet. See at work.

Words you could use: “Could we each list our week, including the stuff in our heads? I’d like us to see the same picture.” “I didn’t realize you did all that. Thank you.”

Part One, Now: your limits and your energy

Before you share a load, it helps to know what you have to give. Your needs and limits are information about you, not demands. A limit that is fair to you can be said in three parts:

  • What is true for you: “I’m low on battery this week.”
  • What you can do: “I can do the groceries.”
  • What you can offer instead, and when: “Could we look at the school forms on Sunday?”

Your energy is a budget. Signs you’re over-giving are quiet resentment, a silent scorecard, feeling invisible, or being tired before the day starts. Those aren’t character flaws. They’re a budget running in the red. Hidden effort, like masking or holding down an old alarm, counts as part of your load too.

Your words, your effort, your rest and your limits are in your hands. Someone else’s mood and choices are not. Spend your energy inside that circle.

An example: a woman realizes she has been the one who organizes every get-together with her old university friends for ten years. She doesn’t want to stop seeing them. She decides to plan one more and then wait to see who suggests the next. Two of them do. One doesn’t, and that’s information too. See friends.

Words you could use: “I can’t take that on this month. I could help in March.” “I’d love to keep doing this, and I need some help with it.”

Saying a need early and kindly lets other people plan, instead of guessing and getting it wrong. “I can’t take that one on this week. I can do this one instead.” Facts about you are not a score against anyone else.

When the effort runs one way, the book suggests matching energy, kindly:

  1. Notice. “I’m usually the one who plans.”
  2. Name it once, without blame. “I’d love it if you started the next one.”
  3. Adjust your side. Give the effort that’s being given.
  4. See what comes back. Some people step up once there’s space. Some don’t, and that’s information too.

Matching energy means matching care and effort, never volume or meanness. If someone is going through something, you match what they can manage, and you can choose to give more for a season. With children, or anyone who depends on you, you give what they need. And if you’re afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them: Not safe at home? has people to talk to.

When one person has less to give for a real reason, like a chronic illness, a disability, a new baby or grief, share by energy, not by hours. The person with less energy can often own jobs that can be done sitting down or at any time, like online orders, bills, or the family calendar. That keeps the split fair without pretending the hours are equal. See when one of you is ill.

Words you could use: “I can’t do the heavy jobs right now, but I can own the bills and the food order. Would that help?”

Chapter II: is the split working?

This chapter asks whether a way of sharing work can last. You can ask the same about your own week: are you spending more energy than comes back in?

  • Add up what you carry: jobs, people and worries.
  • Set it beside what refills you: rest, help, and people who give back.
  • If it runs in the red, change your side: drop, delay, ask, or say no kindly. A limit is a statement about supply, not about your worth.

Judge it from what you wrote down, not from how last night felt. And look again every few months, even when things seem fine. A setup that worked once can quietly go stale when life changes.

An example: a grandmother who looks after her grandchildren three days a week adds it up. She loves the days, but she has stopped seeing her own friends and is tired every weekend. The red isn’t the children. It’s that three days has become four without anyone deciding it. She asks to go back to three. See for grandparents.

Words you could use: “I love doing this, and I need it to fit my week too. Can we go back to what we first agreed?”

Look at the month, not last night. The chapter’s check uses three things you each wrote down:

  • Balance: how evenly the logged hours are shared. If you agreed an uneven split that suits you, compare against that. With three or more people, compare each share with an even share.
  • Ownership: does each regular job have an owner, who does it start to finish, and a backstop, who notices if it slipped and gently checks in? Keep the jobs nobody owns on the list. They matter most.
  • Stress: each of you rates how full your own stress tank is. If either of you is high, treat that as the headline.

Together they give one number from 0 to 1. 0.7 or above: the setup is holding. 0.4 up to 0.7: something is drifting, often who owns what. Below 0.4: time for a kind rethink. Start with whichever part pulled the number down most.

The number judges the setup, never a person. It’s where a talk starts, not how one ends. Each person fills in only their own log and their own stress score, never the other’s.

An example from a shared flat: three housemates score the setup and get a middling result. Hours are fairly even, and nobody is very stressed, but the ownership part is low: “cleaning the bathroom” and “buying shared stuff” have no owner. Giving each one an owner, and a backstop, brings the number up the next month without anyone working more.

Two co-founders of a small business can use the same check: the hours may look balanced while one of them carries all the worry about cash flow. Put that on the list too.

Words you could use: “The number’s about the setup, not about either of us. Which part pulled it down most?”

Chapter IV: two kinds of fair, and one owner per job

Fair starts with knowing what fair means to you, and which jobs are truly yours.

  • Write down what fair means to you, in one sentence. Is it “did everything get done?” or “did each person do what they said they’d do?” Both are reasonable.
  • List the jobs you own, and the ones you picked up without ever agreeing.
  • Notice the quiet habit of covering without saying so. Covering is kind. Silent covering slowly becomes expected.

Owning a job doesn’t mean doing it alone, every time. Swapping, asking for help or saying “not this week” ahead of time all count as looking after it. Whether you cover, and whether you say so, is in your hands. If you keep covering with no thanks and no change, you can cover less, and say so kindly.

An example: a man working from home notices he takes in every parcel, waits in for every repair, and starts dinner every night, because he’s “there.” None of it was agreed. He lists it, and asks his wife to talk about which parts he actually wants to own. See different hours.

Words you could use: “I’ve been doing this without us ever agreeing it. Can we talk about whether it should be mine?”

Many chore arguments are really two kinds of fair. Fair by results asks, “Did everything get done?” It’s flexible, but it can hide who always steps in. Fair by promises asks, “Did each of us do what we agreed to?” It’s clear, but it can feel rigid in a hard week. Neither is wrong. Friction starts when you’re each using a different one without knowing it.

  • Agree on a mix, on a calm day. Owned jobs are judged by promises. Shared or one-off jobs are judged by results: whoever can, does. A shared job that keeps coming up moves onto the owned list.
  • Give each job one owner, who does it start to finish, and a backstop who notices if it slipped (never a supervisor). Older kids can own age-sized jobs too.
  • Say covering out loud. “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Log it, thank it, and watch for repeats. Covering every week means the job needs a new owner or a talk.
  • Count both currencies. If one of you counts doing and the other counts planning, put the planning on the list too, in minutes.

Fair needs re-agreeing. After a new job, a baby or an illness, last year’s fair may not fit. Put a review date on it.

How owners and backstops work in different homes:

  • Two foster carers: one owns contact visits and paperwork, the other owns school and health appointments. Each is the other’s backstop. See foster and kinship families.
  • A couple with different money styles: one owns paying the bills, the other owns the monthly money check-in. Fair by promises keeps it calm. See money together.
  • Dorm roommates: each owns one weekly job on a room agreement, and either can say “I’ll swap with you this week.” See sharing a room.
  • A team at work: one owner per task, written down, ends the “I thought you had it” email. See at work.

Words you could use: “Can we use fair by promises for the regular jobs, and whoever-can for the one-offs?” “I covered the bins three weeks running. Should we give them a new owner?”

Chapter V: the monthly look-back

Once a month, look back at your own month, kindly. Not a scorecard, more like a weather report.

  • What filled your battery this month, and what drained it?
  • Which of your limits held, and which slipped?
  • Choose one small thing to keep, and one to change next month.

If something kept slipping, ask where the gap was: in the plan, in how much you have right now, or just a one-off week. You don’t add up old misses. You only ask what to try next. A quiet month is worth a look too. When things have gone well for a while, it’s easy to stop noticing.

An example: a woman caring for her husband after a stroke does a ten-minute look-back at the end of each month. This month, what drained her most was phone calls to services, not the physical care. The change: she asks her son to own one call a week.

Words you could use (to yourself): “What went well this month? What one thing will I change?”

Weekly check-ins miss patterns. One missed job is bad luck. The same job missed week after week is a pattern, and it shows best across a month.

  • Bring four weeks of notes and your job list. Twenty minutes is usually enough. If either of you is very stressed that day, move it.
  • Mark an ✗ for each week a job slipped: noticed but not done, done only after asking more than once, or quietly covered by someone who isn’t its owner. Three or four in a month is a real pattern.
  • Sort each repeat. No clear owner: update the job list. An owner who can’t keep up: set a time for a kind, honest talk. A short-term cause, like illness or travel: note it and move on.
  • Count the gaps. Fewer jobs with no clear owner, over several months, means the setup is working.

It’s never a review of either person. You don’t bill for past hours, though you can still say thank you for them. Start with something that went well. And keep the look-back on quiet months, with one extra question: “What have we stopped noticing or mentioning?”

An example: two partners notice that “gaming nights” and “date nights” keep colliding, so the evenings slip into resentment. At the look-back, they see the pattern clearly: four weeks, four clashes. The fix is in the setup: two fixed gaming nights and one protected evening together. See gaming and time together.

Words you could use: “This one slipped three weeks out of four. Is it the owner, the timing, or just a busy month?”

When the load changes

Your load doesn’t stay the same. A new job, a move, a new baby, an illness, a parent who needs more help, or children leaving home can change it a lot in a few weeks. When that happens, recount before you push harder. The question isn’t “Why can’t I keep up?” but “What is the load now, and what can I drop, delay or ask for?”

If you’re the only adult at home, much of the load can’t be handed to anyone. You can still lower it: simpler meals, fewer commitments, and asking a friend or family member for one specific thing, like a school pickup on Wednesdays. Specific asks get more yeses than “let me know if you can help.”

Every big change is a reason to re-agree the split:

  • A new baby: the invisible jobs multiply overnight. See new baby, sharing the load.
  • Someone coming home after months away: the person who held the fort owns everything. Hand jobs back slowly, one at a time. See coming home after time apart.
  • Retirement or children leaving: two people suddenly home together need a new split. See retired together and kids grown and gone.
  • Different shifts: sleep after a night shift is work, not free time. See different hours.
  • Money changes: moving in, a wedding or a job loss shifts who pays for what. See money together.

When only one of you is on board: you can still keep your own log, own your own jobs clearly, say covering out loud, and share your list as information. You can’t make the other person join in, and you don’t need to. Small, clear changes on your side often shift the whole setup.

Part Three: helping well, and belonging

Sharing the load also means knowing how to help, and how to be helped. Chapter 12 explains the difference between support and taking over, why asking “what would help?” beats guessing, and how kind limits keep a helper from burning out. Chapter 5 explains why belonging matters so much, and how trust grows from small, reliable moments, the same moments a fair split of work is made of.

An example: you always say yes when your sister asks for help, and then feel resentful. Chapter 12 suggests a smaller, honest yes: “I can do Saturday morning, not the whole weekend.” That’s still help, and you’ll still be able to give it next month.

Thoughts you could try: “I can help with this part. The rest isn’t mine to carry.”

In a shared home or a team, help works best when it’s asked for and agreed, not assumed. Chapter 12 shows how to offer without taking over, and how to receive help without feeling small. Chapter 5 shows why small, kept promises, like a job done when you said it would be, build the kind of trust that makes the next talk easier.

An example: one housemate keeps quietly redoing the dishes, and the other feels judged. A two-minute talk, “Would it help if I took the dishes on weeknights, done my way?”, turns a silent rescue into an agreement.

Words you could use: “What would actually help this week?” “I’d like to help, and I don’t want to take over. Which part could be mine?”
