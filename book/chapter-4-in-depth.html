The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both keeping score honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

This chapter has two halves. The first is about agreeing what “fair” means in your home. The second is about the few seconds after a comment lands, before you reply. They belong together, because most arguments about chores mix up both: a question about a job, and a reaction to how it was said.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Borrowed from systems thinking, behavioral economics and nervous-system science.)

  • In you: Notice which kind of fair comes naturally to you, and notice the quiet habit of covering for others without saying so. When a comment stings, check your own state before you answer.
  • Between you and others: Give each job one clear owner instead of blaming. Watch how unowned jobs slide toward one person, and keep things steady by mentioning help out loud and saying thank you.

Deontological parity: the two kinds of fair

Many chore arguments are really arguments about what fairness means, dressed up in a tea towel. Philosophers have argued about this for centuries, and two of their big ideas are useful here:

  • Fair by results. Philosophers call this consequentialist, from “consequences.” It asks: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy, however it worked out this week?
  • Fair by promises. Philosophers call this deontological, from the Greek word for “duty.” It asks: did each person do the specific things they agreed to own, whatever kind of week it was?

Both are reasonable. The friction starts when one person is judging the week by results (“It all got done, who cares who did it?”) and the other is judging it by promises (“You said you’d handle the trash, and you didn’t, again”). Neither view is wrong. But a household needs to agree which one it is using, just as a contract needs to say which country’s laws apply.

Judging by results

What it’s good at: it’s flexible. In a week when one person is ill or away, the other simply picks things up, and nobody has to negotiate.

Where it goes wrong: it can hide a pattern. If the same person always steps in, the results look fine, and the stepping-in becomes invisible. Over time, that is exactly how unbilled debt builds up.

Judging by promises

What it’s good at: it’s clear. Everyone knows what they own, so nobody has to keep deciding who does what, and nobody gets left with jobs by default.

Where it goes wrong: it can feel rigid. Life doesn’t always cooperate. Holding someone to the letter of an agreement in a week when their parent was in hospital isn’t fairness. It’s missing the point.

The agreement: look to the owner

Deontological parity is this book’s name for a simple agreement. “Parity” means equal standing. The agreement is this: once a job has a named owner in the job list (WP-03), you judge it by whether the owner did their part. You don’t judge it by whether things happened to work out because someone else quietly covered.

This protects the person who keeps filling the gaps. Without it, their help gets used as proof that the gap doesn’t matter: “Well, it got done, didn’t it?”

It doesn’t mean the owner has to do everything personally. Swapping, asking for help or saying “not this week” in advance all count as looking after your job. WP-01 has kind scripts for saying “not right now”: you’re already full, someone else is better placed, or you’ll do it later at a named time. What parity rules out is the job silently falling to someone else.

Choosing your kind of fair

Most homes end up with a mix, and that’s fine, as long as it’s agreed. One simple way:

  • Owned jobs (on the WP-03 list with names) are judged by promises.
  • Shared or one-off jobs (a flat tire, a sick child, a surprise visitor) are judged by results. Whoever can, does.
  • If a shared job keeps coming up, it moves onto the owned list at the next weekly catch-up.

Agree this on a calm, easy day, not in the middle of a disagreement. Write it at the top of your WP-03 sheet so you can both see it.

Covering without resentment

Stepping in for someone is kind. It only turns sour when it’s silent. Three habits keep it kind:

  1. Say it simply, without a sting. “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Not a complaint, just a note.
  2. Log it. In your WP-01 week, write the job under your name, with a note that you covered it.
  3. Watch for repeats. Covering once is kindness. Covering every week is a sign the job needs a new owner or a conversation about capacity. The monthly look-back in Chapter V is built to catch this.

A worked example: the trash, three weeks running

In WP-03, Alex owns the trash. For three weeks, Alex has been working late, and Sam has put the trash out each time without saying anything.

Judged by results: the trash went out every week. No problem. Except Sam is getting quietly resentful, and Alex has no idea.

Judged with parity: the trash is Alex’s job, and Alex didn’t do them. That isn’t a judgment of Alex as a person. It’s a fact about the job. And it points to a real question: can Alex still own the trash with the new work hours?

At the weekly catch-up, Sam says, “I’ve taken out the trash the last three weeks. Are Tuesday nights still realistic for you with the late shifts?” Alex says, “Honestly, no. Could I take the recycling on Saturdays instead, and you keep the trash?” They change the WP-03 sheet and both initial it. Nobody had to be the bad guy.

Sensory gating: giving a comment time to land

The term is borrowed loosely from brain science. There, “sensory gating” describes the brain’s ability to turn down repeated or unimportant sounds and sensations, so they don’t all demand attention at once. Here it’s used as a practical habit, not a medical claim: not every comment needs your full reaction the moment it’s said.

When you’re revved up or running on empty (see the Nervous System Ladder and Chapter III), even a neutral comment can arrive at full volume and seem to demand an instant reply. Gating gives it a moment to settle first.

The three steps: notice, name, decide

  1. Notice that the comment landed harder than its words probably deserved. Often you feel it in your body first: a hot face, a tight chest, a sharp breath.
  2. Name it out loud, instead of reacting from the spike: “I heard that as bigger than it probably was. Give me a second.”
  3. Decide, once you’ve settled, whether it needs a reply now, later or not at all.

This isn’t bottling things up. The aim isn’t to shut everything out for good. It’s timing: letting the spike pass before deciding how much weight a comment really deserves. The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) helps the spike pass, and WP-09, Say it so it lands, helps with the reply once it has.

WP-09 also offers four quick questions for weighing what you heard. Was it about a specific task? Would it sound the same if you weren’t already stretched? Could a neutral meaning fit? Are you answering their words, or an old argument?

Keep the two questions apart

Put the two halves of this chapter together, and you can separate two things that usually get tangled:

The questionWhat kind of questionWhat it’s checked against
“Was this job done?”A factThe agreed owner in WP-03
“Did that land as an attack?”A reactionYour own state right now

Both questions matter. They just need different answers, and they go better one at a time. Take the fact to the job list. Take the reaction to a quiet moment and WP-09.

A worked example: the flat thank-you

Wednesday, 7pm. Sam says, “Thanks for taking out the trash,” in a flat voice. Alex hears sarcasm and feels a flash of anger.

Without gating: Alex snaps back, “I do them every week, you know.” Sam, who was only tired, feels attacked. Now there’s an argument about tone, and nobody remembers it started with a thank-you.

With gating: Alex notices the flash and says, “That landed a bit oddly for me. Give me a second.” After a breath: “Did you mean that as a thank-you?” Sam says, “Yes, sorry, I’m just shattered.” It’s over in ten seconds.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, each person keeps using their own kind of fair without saying so. The one who judges by results thinks everything’s fine. The one who judges by promises feels let down over and over. Every comment about a chore gets answered at full volume.

When it goes right, you both know which jobs are owned and which are shared. Covering gets mentioned and logged. When a comment stings, one of you says, “Give me a second,” and the other waits.

Questions people often ask

What if we can’t agree on which kind of fair to use?

Use the mix above: owned jobs by promises, shared jobs by results. Try it for a month, then look again together.

Isn’t it petty to hold someone to a small job?

Not if you both agreed to it. What feels petty is usually the build-up of unspoken covering. Saying it early and kindly is the opposite of petty.

What if “give me a second” turns into an hour?

That’s fine, as long as you say so and name a time to come back. Pausing is for timing, not for avoiding the talk.

Limits and cautions

  • Parity is a tool for jobs, not for judging people. “You didn’t do your job” is a fact about a task. It never means “you’re a bad partner.”
  • Be flexible when life is hard. Illness, grief and big changes are exactly when agreements should be looked at again, not enforced.
  • Gating is not suppression. If something that was said still hurts after you’ve settled, it deserves a calm conversation.
