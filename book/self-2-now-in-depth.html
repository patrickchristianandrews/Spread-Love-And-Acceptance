Know yourself: now, your wiring, your weather and your words

Sunday, 7:50pm

A friend asks, “Are you okay?” You say, “Fine.” The truth is you can’t tell whether you’re fine, tired or quietly bothered, and you couldn’t explain it to yourself, let alone to them.

Everything in this part is about you, and it is for you. Nothing is ever filled in about someone else, nothing is a test, and nothing you try needs to be shared. This part is about today: how you are built, what kind of day you are having and what you can say about both.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This part mostly teaches Pillar III, Read your state first and Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person. (Borrowed from neurobiology, psychology and philosophy.)

  • In you: Check your state (Pillar III) and know your wiring (Pillar IV) before you judge a reaction. Count the hidden effort, like masking or holding an old alarm down, as real load (Pillar I).
  • Between you and others: Change the setup, not the person (Pillar II). Tell people what helps, tune how you send and receive, and treat a mismatch as tuning, not a moral failing.

Today is made of two layers you can work with

A reaction has three layers: wiring, patterns life taught you and today’s conditions. Then covered the middle layer, the patterns. This part is about the other two.

LayerWhat it isThe move
Your wiringTemperament, pace, sensory makeup, how you process words and feelings. It stays.Work with it: written plans, quiet time after work, a heads-up before changes, a visible calendar
Today’s conditionsSleep, stress, hunger, the load you’re carrying, the room, the time of day. They pass.Work around it: eat first, sleep on it, say “low-battery day,” move the talk to tomorrow

Conditions amplify the other layers. A mild wiring need (“I prefer a heads-up”) becomes a hard limit when you’re exhausted, and an old alarm that stays quiet on good days can go off at full volume on bad ones. Because conditions change fastest, they are the easiest layer to adjust, and the first place to look.

A lot of frustration comes from using the wrong move. Trying to force your wiring to change tends to wear you out. Treating a bad day as your permanent self makes a passing storm feel like the climate. A kind rule of thumb: change the conditions today, work with the wiring always, and work on the patterns over time.

The full guide, with a sorting table of common reactions, is Know your own wiring. It also covers work on it, work with it, work around it in more detail.

Why the two layers need different moves

Wiring is the climate and conditions are the weather. You dress for the climate every day, and you check the weather each morning. Expecting your climate to change wears you out, and treating a stormy afternoon as your climate makes you think less of yourself than you should.

In different relationships

  • Two partners on different shifts: Priya works nights and Jess works days. Their worst arguments happen at 7am, when Priya is ending a twelve-hour shift and Jess is starting her day. Neither is an angry person. They move anything important to Saturday lunch, when both are rested. See Different hours.
  • A grandparent minding grandchildren: A grandfather who loves his grandchildren finds himself short-tempered by four o’clock on minding days. His wiring likes quiet. His conditions are a long day and no lunch break. A sandwich at noon and a half-hour of cartoons at three change the whole afternoon. See Grandparents.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If I’m tired, my feelings don’t count.” They count. Tiredness turns the volume up; it doesn’t make the feeling false. Look at it again once rested.
  • “My wiring is an excuse.” Wiring explains, and a good setup is your part. “I need a heads-up” comes with “and I’ll tell you early when plans don’t suit me.”

If you’re the only one doing this: Change your own conditions first: sleep, food, when you raise things. You don’t need anyone’s agreement to eat before a hard talk.

Research note: In lab studies, people who have missed a night’s sleep show stronger reactions to upsetting images, and studies of couples have linked a poor night’s sleep with more conflict the next day. Temperament research describes lasting differences in things like sensitivity and pace from early childhood. This site’s idea: the “work with it, work around it” moves.

Your wiring, and how you like to be spoken to

Wiring isn’t a flaw to fix. It is the lasting part of how you take in the world: your temperament, your sensory makeup, your pace, and how you handle words and feelings. You can understand it, plan around it and get support for it, through routines, tools, a better setup and, if you choose, help from a qualified professional.

Two free pages help you put it into words:

  • Wavelength asks a few short questions and gives you a four-letter Wave Code. Pace is Quick Spark or Slow Simmer, lens is Forest or Trees, sending is Direct or Nuanced, and receiving is Explicit or Attuned. It is a fun way to describe your preferences, not a test that measures you, and everyone uses both ends of each line sometimes. The wiring step is optional, it is self-identified, and it isn’t a diagnosis.
  • The Wiring Card turns how you actually take in words into one page: what helps, what silence means for you, how a request is best phrased. A card is a simple note, not a label, and not permission to criticize. It tells someone how to send a request so it gets through. It doesn’t make them responsible for managing you, and it doesn’t mean you have to be available no matter how you feel.

When you share either one, pick a calm moment and offer it as information: “I wrote this so you don’t have to guess. Would you read it?” Invite theirs, and put the two side by side. The places where you differ are not problems. They are the best places for a short talk. Update yours when life changes, because what you need changes too.

Why it helps to describe your wiring

Most mix-ups between kind people come from mismatched settings, not from bad intent. When you can say how you take in words, other people stop guessing, and you stop being misread. A description you write yourself is also kinder than a label someone else gives you.

In different relationships

  • Housemates: One housemate says what they mean in a single line. The other reads short messages as cross. After swapping Wiring Cards, the first adds a smiley face to notes, and the second stops reading tone into them. See Sharing a room.
  • A parent and a grown-up son: She likes long talks with every detail. He likes the headline first. On calls, he asks, “Headline or the whole story?” and she laughs and gives him the headline first.
  • Business partners: One is a Quick Spark who decides in the meeting. The other is a Slow Simmer who decides overnight. They agree that big decisions always get a night’s sleep.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “My Wave Code says I’m Direct, so people should cope.” A code describes a preference. It doesn’t excuse unkindness, and it goes both ways.
  • Handing over a card in the middle of a row. Share it on a calm day, as information, and invite theirs.

If you’re the only one doing this: Write your card anyway, and use it yourself: it helps you notice what you need before a hard day. You can share one line of it when the moment is right.

A note on safety: A Wiring Card is never something to fill in for someone else, or to check up on them with. If someone uses what you shared to control or mock you, that isn’t a communication mix-up. Not safe at home? has people to talk to.

Research note: Well-studied personality research uses five broad traits (often called the Big Five), measured on scales rather than types. Type-based quizzes are less reliable as measurement. The Wave Code is not a personality test. This site’s idea: the Wave Code and the Wiring Card, as conversation starters.

Know your load and your state before you speak

Every conversation passes through your own state first. Chapter III is the full chapter on this, with leftover stress and this-moment stress, the three everyday states, the stress-tank score and why a full tank means “later,” not “never.” This part does not repeat them. It adds the habit of taking a reading on your own, on ordinary days, so that it is familiar by the time you need it.

  • Today’s Weather, a one-minute reading of your own conditions in weather words.
  • How much are you carrying? (WP-02), five quick questions about what you’re already carrying.

Because a reading is quick, it is easy to do a few mornings in a row. Patterns tend to show within a week. A tank that has stayed full for a long time can start to feel normal, and “I’m fine” can come to mean “I stopped noticing,” as Chapter III describes.

Why a reading on ordinary days helps

A reading you have practised on calm days is there when you need it on a hard one. And a reading over a week shows patterns a single day can’t, like the fact that every Thursday is stormy.

In different relationships

  • A long-distance couple: Both take a one-word weather reading before their evening call. “Foggy” means a light, short call. “Sunny” means time for the big topics. See Long distance.
  • A carer and a partner with an illness: The carer notices his readings have been stormy for three weeks. That tells him he needs respite, not just a better attitude. See When one of you is ill and Caregivers.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “A reading is a mood score for others to judge.” It is for you. Share it only if it helps.
  • “If my tank is full, I can’t talk at all.” A full tank means later, not never. Name a time and keep it.

If you’re the only one doing this: A reading only needs you. You don’t have to share it, and it still changes what you decide to say and when.

Research note: Studies on “affect labeling” (Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, 2007) found that putting a feeling into words reduced activity in the brain’s alarm areas in the lab. Lisa Feldman Barrett’s work on “emotional granularity” suggests people who can describe feelings in finer detail tend to cope with them better. Both are early, lab-based findings, not proof that a word cures a feeling.

Where is this feeling coming from? A four-step self-check

When a feeling is bigger than you expected, here is a short way to sort it out. It works best once the first wave has passed a little.

  1. Name it. A word or two is enough, even a rough one: “tight and annoyed,” “flat,” “jumpy.” If words are hard to find, that’s common. Start with the body instead: where do you feel it?
  2. Is it about now, or does it feel older? Ask whether this is the size of the moment. A small thing that brings a big feeling often has something left over in it, either from earlier today or from much longer ago.
  3. Which layer is it? Wiring (“this always bothers me”), a pattern (“this reminds me of something”) or conditions (“I’d probably shrug this off after a good night’s sleep”). Usually it is a mix, so pick the biggest part.
  4. What does it need? Food, rest or space. A heads-up or a written plan. A fact check, or a slower try at a new response. Sometimes it needs a real conversation, later, when you’re both settled.

Feelings often reach the body before they reach words. A hot face and fast breathing, tight shoulders, or a heavy, foggy feeling are everyday clues about your state. Everyone’s are a little different, and they are not a medical reading of anything.

Why four small steps beat one big question

“What’s wrong with me?” has no useful answer. Four small questions each do have one. They slow you down just enough for the thinking part of you to catch up with the feeling part.

In different relationships

  • Friends: A friend cancels lunch for the second time. Name it: hurt, a bit rejected. Now or older? Older; you were often left out at school. Layer: pattern. Need: a fact check. You reply, “No worries. Is everything okay with you?” and learn her child has been ill. See Friends.
  • A foster carer: A foster child shouts, “You’re not my real mum!” Name it: stung. Now or older? Mostly now, and tired. Need: to stay steady and talk later. The carer says, “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” See Foster and kinship families.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Doing the check at the peak of a feeling. Let the first wave pass. Breathe, walk, drink water, then ask.
  • “I couldn’t name it, so I failed.” Start with the body. “Tight chest” is a perfectly good first answer.

If you’re the only one doing this: All four steps happen inside you. Nobody else has to do anything for them to work.

Research note: Some people find it hard to identify and describe their feelings; psychologists call this alexithymia, and it is common, especially in some neurodivergent people. That is a reason to start with the body or a simple choice of words, not a sign the check isn’t for you. This site’s idea: the four-step self-check.

Plan what settles you on a calm day

The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) is a plan for heated moments that you make while you are calm. On an ordinary day, write down what tends to set you off and where you feel it first. Pick two calming steps. Agree with yourself on one sentence to say when you step away: how you feel, how long, and when you’ll be back. In the moment, you just follow the plan.

The kit is for you, never for them. One person uses it about themselves, and it is never something to hand to the other person. Your line always includes a time you’ll return, so a break is never mistaken for walking out. Chapter III says how to check your tank again and go back.

Why you plan it while calm

In a heated moment, the part of you that plans is the part that goes offline first. A plan made on a calm day is like a note left by a wiser you. In the moment, you only have to follow it.

In different relationships

  • Separated co-parents: A mum knows handover days make her tense. Her kit: a short walk before the handover, a scripted “Thanks, see you Sunday,” and a call to her sister afterwards. See Co-parenting.
  • A teen and a parent: A fourteen-year-old and his dad each make their own kit. Both agree that “I need ten minutes” means ten minutes, not “I’m leaving.”
  • Coworkers: A nurse keeps one line ready for a heated shift: “Let me check that and come back to you in five minutes.”

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Using your kit as a weapon: “Go and use your calm-down plan.” The kit is only ever for yourself.
  • Stepping away without a return time. Always add when you’ll be back, and come back on time.

If you’re the only one doing this: Make your own kit. It works whether or not anyone else has one, and it makes the moment safer for both of you.

A note on safety: A break is for calming down, not for escaping consequences or punishing someone with silence. If someone blocks you from leaving a heated room, or punishes you for stepping away, that is about safety, not technique. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Research note: Research on “implementation intentions” (Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran) found that deciding ahead what you’ll do in a given situation helps people follow through. John Gottman’s work with couples describes “flooding,” when the body is too stirred up to listen, and suggests breaks long enough for the body to settle, often twenty minutes or more.

Your needs, what matters to you, and what’s fair to you

A need is information. “I do better with a heads-up,” “I need an hour of quiet after work,” “I can’t take on another job this week.” These are facts about you, not demands and not complaints. Said kindly and early, they let other people plan, instead of guessing and getting it wrong.

It helps to know them before you’re asked. Knowing what matters to you (quiet, time with family, being on time, honesty, rest) also helps you tell a real limit from a passing mood. A limit that is fair to you can be stated without a fight, in three parts:

  1. What is true for you. “I’m low on battery this week.”
  2. What you can do. “I can do the groceries.”
  3. What you can offer instead, and when. “Could we look at the school forms on Sunday?”

Two workpapers help. WP-01 (Part B) has ready-made, kind ways to say no. WP-09, Say it so it lands, turns a raw reaction into a fact, a feeling and an ask before you say or send anything.

This is also where the book’s later chapters meet this part. Chapter II asks whether the way you share the work can last, and what you have left is part of that answer. Chapter IV asks what “fair” means to each of you. Both are easier to talk about when you already know your own side, and a limit that is fair to you is not a score against anyone else.

Why needs said early go further

A need said early and plainly gives other people a chance to plan. A need held in until it bursts out tends to arrive as a complaint, and gets heard as one.

In different relationships

  • Three partners at home: Ren needs Sunday mornings alone. Said as a fact (“Sunday mornings are my reset”) it fits into the week. Before, it came out as snapping. See More than two partners at home.
  • An adult child and an elderly parent: A son who visits his mother every day says, “I can come four days a week, and I’ll call on the other three.” It is honest, and it keeps him going for years instead of months.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “A limit is a punishment.” A limit is a fact about your capacity. Say it warmly and offer what you can.
  • Saying a need as a complaint about the other person. “You never give me notice” becomes “A heads-up helps me.”

Words you could use: “This is true for me right now: ___. I can ___. Could we ___ instead?”

If you’re the only one doing this: You can state a need even if the other person doesn’t state theirs. Just be ready to hear theirs when they do.

A note on safety: If stating a simple need leads to anger, threats or punishment, the problem isn’t how you said it. Not safe at home? has people to talk to.

Research note: Marshall Rosenberg’s Nonviolent Communication teaches saying an observation, a feeling, a need and a request. It is a widely used practice framework rather than a well-tested therapy. Assertiveness, saying what you want clearly while respecting others, has long been taught in skills training. This site’s idea: the three-part limit.

Being wired differently is not being wrong

Some people take in words, noise, change and feelings differently from what most social customs were designed around. That includes people who are autistic, have ADHD, are dyslexic or are highly sensitive, and many who are not sure. Nothing about that makes someone wrong. A setup that doesn’t suit you is a setup problem, so the question is how to build one that does.

  • It often has a hidden cost. Holding an old alarm down, masking, rehearsing what to say, or getting through a loud room all use real energy. This book counts it as part of your load (Pillar I), even when nobody sees it.
  • A mix-up usually runs both ways. When two differently wired people misread each other, each is decoding with a receiver built for their own kind of signal. Wired Differently explains this, and there is a full guide to fourteen kinds of wiring.
  • Don’t label anyone. This includes you. You can describe what helps without a name for it, and a name is not needed to use anything here. If you want to understand yourself more deeply, a doctor or psychologist can help, if and when you want that.

The same goes the other way. When you meet someone else’s difference, ask “What was that like for you?” and let them answer. Never fill in a Wiring Card or a Wave Code for another person. That would turn a reflection tool into a way of watching someone.

Why “different, not wrong” matters

If you believe your wiring is a defect, you spend energy hiding it. If you see it as a difference, you can spend that energy on a setup that works. The same goes for the people around you.

In different relationships

  • A parent and a child with ADHD: A dad who has ADHD himself realises his daughter isn’t ignoring him; she needs one instruction at a time. He tries one instruction, then a thumbs up. See When a child has ADHD.
  • An autistic friend and a non-autistic friend: One friend says exactly what she means. The other hears hints in everything. They agree: “If I want something, I’ll ask it straight. You can take what I say at face value.”

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If I need adjustments, I’m being difficult.” Glasses aren’t difficult. Neither is a quiet room.
  • Deciding someone else is “probably autistic.” Ask what helps them instead. Labels are theirs to choose.

If you’re the only one doing this: You can build your own setup without anyone else changing: headphones, written lists, a quiet half hour.

Research note: The “double empathy problem,” described by Damian Milton in 2012, suggests that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways. Studies of autistic adults (for example, Laura Hull and colleagues, 2017) describe “camouflaging,” or masking, as tiring and linked with stress. Elaine Aron’s research on “sensory processing sensitivity” describes people who take in their surroundings more deeply.

Explain yourself without over-sharing

You never owe anyone your history. Share the part that helps them help you, and keep the rest. Three short sentences cover most moments:

  • “This is how I’m wired. It won’t change, but here’s what helps.”
  • “This is an old alarm. I’m working on it, and here’s what helps in the moment.”
  • “Today’s a low-battery day.”

Offer them as information, not as rules, and ask about theirs. When you want a bit more, Know your own wiring has scripts and a guide to how much to share. And when someone else shares, ask questions, not labels.

Why less can be more

A short, clear sentence about what helps is easier to remember than a long story about why. It also protects you: your history is yours to share when you choose, with people who have earned it.

In different relationships

  • Coming out at work: A woman who has just come out to her family isn’t ready to share at work yet. When a colleague asks why she seems tired, she says, “It’s been a big month at home. I’m okay, thanks for asking.” See Coming out as an adult.
  • An interfaith couple at a family dinner: A husband who doesn’t drink for religious reasons says to his in-laws, “I don’t drink, but I’d love a lemonade.” No lecture needed. See Two faiths or cultures.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “Being open means telling everything.” Being open means being honest about what you share, not sharing it all.
  • Explaining as a way to win. “It’s how I’m wired” starts a conversation. It doesn’t end one.

If you’re the only one doing this: Your sentences are yours to use whether or not anyone else explains themselves.

Research note: Research on self-disclosure, going back to Irwin Altman and Dalmas Taylor in the 1970s, describes closeness growing through gradual, two-way sharing. That fits the advice here: share a little, see how it is received, and share more with people who respond with care.

A worked example: the surprise dinner, replayed

Friday, 6:40pm. Alex says, “Surprise! I booked us dinner with my cousins tonight.” They meant it as a gift. Sam feels their chest tighten and hears themselves say “Great” in a flat voice.

Without the tools: Sam goes to the dinner, stays quiet and gets through it. Alex senses something is wrong and asks. Sam says, “It’s nothing,” and thinks, “What’s wrong with me?” Both end the evening unsure what happened.

With the tools: The next morning, Sam takes a minute. Name it: tight, a bit cornered. Now or older? Bigger than the moment. Which layer? Mostly wiring, a need to know plans ahead, plus a long week. What does it need? A heads-up next time, and a rested Sam. That evening Sam says, “Thank you for thinking of me. I do better with a heads-up, it’s how I’m wired. Could a text a few hours before work for both of us?” Alex says, “Easy. I didn’t know.” They try it, and agree to look again at the end of the month.

Two more worked examples

The housemate and the hoover. Tomás comes home after a twelve-hour shift and his housemate is vacuuming. He snaps, “Seriously, now?” Later, he runs the four steps. Name it: raw, prickly. Now or older? Mostly now. Layer: conditions (exhausted) and wiring (noise always bothers him). Need: quiet when he first gets in. He says, “Sorry I snapped. After long shifts I need half an hour of quiet. Could the hoover wait until seven?” His housemate says yes, and asks him to text when he is on his way home.

A grandmother and the phone. Rosa, 72, feels hurt that her grandson only sends short texts. She takes a reading. Layer: mostly wiring; she likes voices, he likes typing. She says, “I love hearing your voice. Could we have a ten-minute call on Sundays? Texts are lovely the rest of the week.” He agrees, and starts sending her voice notes too.

Your circle of control, and matching energy

Every moment has three rings. In the middle is what you control: your words, your effort, your tone, your rest, your limits and what you ask for. Around it is what you can influence but not decide: you can ask, explain, invite and offer, and the other person still chooses. Outside is what isn’t yours at all: their mood, their past, their pace, whether they agree. Most exhaustion comes from pouring energy into the outer ring.

In your hands

  • What you say, and how and when you say it
  • Doing your own jobs well
  • Saying yes, no or “not today”
  • Resting before you’re empty
  • Asking clearly for what would help

Theirs to decide

  • How your words land
  • Whether they change
  • How fast they calm down
  • What they think of you
  • Whether they say yes

Your energy is a budget

Your battery (How much are you carrying?, WP-02) only holds so much. Signs you’re over-giving: quiet resentment, a silent scorecard, feeling invisible, being tired before the day starts. Those aren’t character flaws. They’re a budget running in the red.

How to match energy, kindly

  1. Notice. “I’m usually the one who plans, texts first or apologizes.”
  2. Name it once, without blame. “I’ve noticed I’m often the one who starts our plans. I’d love it if you started the next one.”
  3. Adjust your side. Give the effort that’s being given: a warm reply to a warm message, a lighter touch where little comes back.
  4. See what comes back. Some people step up once there’s space. Some don’t, and that’s information too.

Matching energy means matching care and effort, never volume or meanness. If someone gets loud, you don’t have to get loud. If someone is going through something, you match their capacity, not their output, and you can choose to give more for a season, as long as it’s a choice and something refills you. With children and anyone who depends on you, you give what they need. If you’re afraid of someone, none of this is about managing them, and matching energy isn’t safe with a person who frightens you: Not safe at home? has people to talk to.

Words you could use: “I want to keep showing up for this, and I need it to go both ways.” “I can do my part. That one is yours.” “I’m low today, so I’m keeping my energy for the essentials.”

Why the circle helps

When you sort a worry into “mine” or “not mine,” you stop spending energy where it can’t do anything. That energy comes back to you for the things you can change.

In different relationships

  • A business partner: You can make sure your half of the accounts is clear and on time. You can’t make your partner do theirs. You ask once, put it in writing, and then decide what you will do if it doesn’t happen.
  • A parent of a teen: You can’t make your sixteen-year-old talk. You can be in the kitchen at the same time every evening with a snack and no questions. Many teens talk more when no one is asking.
  • Friends: You notice you always start the chat. You reply warmly but stop starting for a month. See Always the one reaching out?

Mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “Not my circle” means “not my problem.” You can still care, offer and ask. You just stop trying to decide for them.
  • Matching energy as a cold tally. It is balance over time. In a hard season, you match capacity, not output.

If you’re the only one doing this: This section is, by its nature, something you do alone. Your circle is the part only you can act on.

Research note: The split between what is and isn’t up to us comes from the Stoic teacher Epictetus. Equity theory, from Elaine Hatfield (then Walster) and colleagues in the 1970s, found that people tend to feel uneasy when a relationship feels unbalanced either way. Studies of family carers generally link having practical and emotional support with lower strain.

You, in all your relationships

Your wiring and your weather come with you into every room. Here is where they tend to show up.

  • With yourself first: Noticing “I’m low today” before you decide “I’m failing,” and planning your week to suit how you’re built.
  • With a partner, or partners: A heads-up, a quiet hour, and a better time for big talks. In a home with more than two partners, each person’s wiring adds to the mix. See More than two partners at home.
  • With friends: How you like to keep in touch, and how much you can give right now. See Friends.
  • With family: Visits that run long, noise, old roles and big gatherings. A rest planned ahead turns a retreat into a plan. See For grown-up children.
  • At work: An agenda ahead, a minute before you answer, a written follow-up.
  • With housemates: Light, noise and quiet time. See Sharing a room.
  • On different hours: Sleep at odd times is still sleep. See Different hours.
  • On your own: No one to adjust around but you, which is a chance to build a week that truly fits. See On my own.

To find help for your own situation, see help by relationship. If you guide others, see For counselors, coaches and group leaders.

How they connect

The word in brackets is the root the two fields share; the polymath page explains all seven. From the most obvious connection to the most abstract:

  • Obvious: Psychology + Holistic therapies (state and setting). Calm the body before the conversation. Words land better, and come out better, once the body has settled. This holds up Pillar III, Read your state first.
  • Hidden: Behavioral science + Neurobiology (limited capacity). A tired body makes good habits harder, so the habits in this book are tiny. They have to survive the worst day of the week, which is why you practice them on a calm one. This holds up Pillar III, Read your state first.
  • Abstract: Philosophy + Neurobiology (state and setting). If getting revved up or shutting down is a body doing its job, it isn’t a character flaw. That changes what blame means, including the blame you aim at yourself. This holds up Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person.

The body tells you what state you’re in, and the story you tell about it decides what you do next.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, “This is just how I am” becomes a way to end every conversation. Or self-knowledge turns into self-criticism: a long list of what’s wrong with you. Or someone uses a label as a weapon (“You’re obviously just anxious”) and calls it understanding.

When it goes right, you take your own reading, quietly, and say one clear sentence about what helps. You ask about theirs. Over a few weeks you both learn what each of you needs, and you start to allow for it before the hard moment, not in the middle of it.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t focusing on myself selfish?

Every conversation runs through your own state first. Knowing it makes you easier to talk with and easier to hear. The other person’s side matters just as much, and the chapters after Part One are about that.

Do I need a diagnosis to use any of this?

No. Nothing here needs one and nothing here gives one. The wiring step in Wavelength is self-identified. If you would like a formal assessment, a doctor or psychologist can do one.

What if I can’t tell what I’m feeling?

That is common, and some people are wired that way. Start with the body, or with a choice between two simple words, like “more tired or more annoyed? Space or company?” Today’s Weather uses weather words for the same reason.

Can I use this to understand someone else?

Not as a verdict. You can ask, gently, what something was like for them, and then let them answer. A guess about someone else’s wiring often turns into a judgment of them. The tools are for filling in about yourself.

What if the people around me don’t care how I’m wired?

You can still build your own setup and say one clear sentence about what helps. Some people need to hear it more than once. If someone mocks or punishes you for a simple need, that is about them, and Not safe at home? has help if you feel afraid.

Where can I talk this through?

Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you work out where a feeling came from before you decide what to say.

Limits and cautions

  • This part is general education. It isn’t a diagnosis, a test or treatment, and it can’t tell you what you or anyone else has.
  • The layers are a thinking tool, not a scientific category system. Real people are messier, and the layers overlap.
  • A Wave Code is a fun description of preferences, not a test that measures you. Everyone uses both ends of every line sometimes.
  • Fill things in about yourself only. Never sort, score or label another person, and never use what you learned about yourself to win a point.
  • Research notes on this page describe well-known findings in plain words, and say where the evidence is limited. Notes marked “This site’s idea” are our own ways of putting things into practice, not established science.
  • “That’s just how I am” explains a reaction. It doesn’t excuse it, and it doesn’t replace a kind repair.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about any of this ever feels unsafe, or you feel you can’t say no, stop and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.
