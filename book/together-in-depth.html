Sunday, 9pm

Someone notices the laundry is still in the washer, the school forms are due on Tuesday, and the birthday card never got mailed. They also notice that nobody else has noticed. The house looks fine. That’s the problem.

Or, living alone: A widower says, “Since my wife died, I notice how many jobs she quietly carried: the birthday cards, the boiler service, knowing who to ring when someone was ill. I never saw them until they stopped getting done.”

Every home keeps a set of accounts that nobody agreed to keep.

Someone notices the milk is low. Someone remembers the dentist appointment three weeks away. Someone senses the mood in the room shifting before an argument starts, and quietly steers it somewhere calmer. None of this shows up on a calendar. None of it gets a thank-you, because you can only thank someone for work you have seen. And this kind of work is hidden by nature: when it’s done well, nothing seems to happen.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This preface mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load, with help from Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. (Borrowed from finance and economics.)

  • In you: Notice everything you carry, including the thinking and feeling work you have never counted. Notice, too, the work others do that you may have missed.
  • Between you and others: Put both lists on one shared, fair page, so nobody has to argue about whose work “counts.” Jobs nobody claims tend to drift to whoever notices first, and a specific thank-you helps keep things steady.

Put together, the five pillars make one simple loop: you see the load (I), redesign the setup around it (II), check your state before you talk (III), tune the message so it lands (IV), and keep an eye on the quiet incentives that let things slide back (V).

Unbilled debt: the work that goes unseen

Over months and years, that hidden work builds up like a debt that only one person can see on their books. The other person usually isn’t refusing to pay it. Most of the time, they don’t know it exists.

This book calls that gap unbilled debt: the difference between the work someone does and the work anyone notices. In a business, an unbilled hour is work that was done and logged, but never invoiced, so the client never sees it. At home, it’s much the same. The work was real. It just never made it onto the page that the other person reads.

This debt isn’t repaid in hours, and nobody sends a bill. It’s cleared by being seen, thanked and shared differently from here on. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past.

Plato’s Cave is an old story about people who mistake shadows for the real thing. At home, the few bits of work you happen to see are the shadows. The whole job is the real thing.

The reasoning. A debt nobody can see can’t be talked about, so it can’t be shared. It doesn’t disappear, though. It turns into tiredness for the person carrying it, and confusion for the person who isn’t (“Why are you so upset about a birthday card?”). Naming it as a debt, not a failing, lets both people look at the gap without either one being on trial.

Worked examples

  • Two partners, both men: Ray handles every family birthday, the car insurance and the dog’s vet visits. Jamal works longer hours and thinks they split the home “about half and half.” After a week of notes, Jamal sees about four hours of Ray’s work he had never counted. He doesn’t pay it back. He thanks Ray for the past, and takes over the car and the vet from next month.
  • A grown-up daughter and her father: Since her mother died, Lena has quietly taken over her father’s bills, medicines and doctor visits. Her father thinks she “pops in now and then.” When she writes it down, she realizes it’s most of her Saturdays. She doesn’t send a bill. She asks him which parts he’d like to do again himself, and asks her brother to take the pharmacy run. See Caregivers.
  • Two housemates: Bea is the one who always restocks the toilet roll, washing-up liquid and bin bags. It costs her money as well as noticing. They set up a small shared kitty and a list on the fridge. The unseen work becomes a shared job. See Money together.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “Unbilled debt means they owe me hours.” It doesn’t. The fix is to be seen, thanked and shared differently from now on. If you catch yourself adding up the past, switch to planning next week.
  • “If I name it, I’m calling them lazy.” Name the work, not the person: “Here’s a job that’s been invisible,” not “Here’s what you never do.”
  • “It’s only a debt if it’s big.” Most unbilled work comes in small pieces. Small and often is exactly how it builds up.

If only one of you is on board: Write your own week anyway. Use it to see your real load, plan your energy and choose one job to hand back or let go. You can share a single item, kindly, without sharing the whole list.

Words you could use: “I’ve been carrying a few things you might not have seen. I’d like to show you, not to blame you, just so we see the same picture.”

A note on safety: If someone insists you owe them for everything they do, keeps a tally to make you feel small, or controls the money so you can’t say no, that isn’t this idea. That is control. Not safe at home? lists free, private help, and Money together explains the difference between normal limits and control.

Research notes

Sociologist Arlie Hochschild, with Anne Machung, described “the second shift” in 1989: the home work many people do after their paid work ends, much of it unseen. Later research on gratitude (for example, Amie Gordon and colleagues in 2012) found that people who felt appreciated by their partner tended to be more appreciative in return, and that appreciation was linked to staying together. This site’s idea: the name “unbilled debt” and the rule that it’s cleared by being seen and shared, not by being repaid.

The kinds of unseen work

Unseen work isn’t one thing. It helps to name the different kinds, because each one hides in a different way.

  • Noticing. Seeing that something needs doing: the trash is full, the child has grown out of their shoes, the car is due for service.
  • Remembering. Holding dates and details in your head: birthdays, trash day, which form goes back to school on which day.
  • Planning and deciding. Working out the options and choosing one: what to cook this week, which plumber to call, when to visit family.
  • Checking. Making sure the thing actually happened, and following up when it didn’t.
  • Smoothing. Keeping the peace, keeping in touch with relatives, cheering someone up, and sensing when a conversation needs to slow down.

People who study home life often call the thinking parts of this the “mental load” or “cognitive labor.” The name matters less than the idea: a job is more than the few minutes it takes to do it. Someone has to carry it in their head first.

The reasoning. When people say “we split the chores,” they usually mean the doing. But each job has a front end (noticing, remembering, planning, deciding) and a back end (checking, following up). Whoever holds the front end is the one who can never fully switch off. Naming the five kinds lets you split the whole job, not just the visible slice.

Worked examples

  • Separated co-parents: Both parents do school runs. But one parent is the one who reads every school email, knows which week is non-uniform day and remembers to tell the other. They agree that each parent owns all school emails for their own weeks. See Co-parenting.
  • Kinship carers: A grandmother raising her grandson does the visible care, and also the smoothing: keeping the boy in touch with his mum, handling birthdays that bring up big feelings, and calming things after phone calls. Once she names that work, her partner starts taking the Sunday phone calls. See Foster, adoptive and kinship families.
  • Two business partners: One partner does the client work. The other notices when contracts are due, plans the cash flow and checks the invoices were paid. Neither had counted the second list as “work.” They give it a name, “running the business,” and a regular slot.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If I do the task, I’ve shared it.” Not if someone else still has to remind you. Try taking a job start to finish: noticing, doing and checking.
  • “Smoothing is just my personality.” It may come easily, but it still takes energy. Put it on the list with a rough number of minutes.
  • “Lists are for planners.” You don’t have to like lists. A five-minute chat with the five words (notice, remember, plan, check, smooth) does the same job.

If only one of you is on board: Sort your own list into the five kinds. Most people find one kind takes far more of their time than they thought. That alone can explain why you feel tired.

Words you could use: “Could you take this one from start to finish, including remembering it? That’s the part I’d most like to put down.”

A note on safety: Smoothing can hide something harder. If you spend your days managing someone’s moods so they don’t explode, that is not ordinary home work. You don’t have to manage it alone: Not safe at home?

Research notes

Sociologist Allison Daminger (2019) split the thinking side of home work into four stages: anticipating a need, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. Anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo (1987) named “kin work”: the cards, calls and visits that keep a wider family connected. Arlie Hochschild’s term “emotional labor” (1983) was first about paid jobs, but people now often use it for the smoothing work at home. This site’s idea: the five plain words, and adding “smoothing” as its own kind.

Why the other person doesn’t see it

There are a few plain reasons this work stays hidden, and none of them need anyone to be careless.

  • Success looks like nothing. When the milk never runs out, nobody sees the person who kept it from running out.
  • It happens inside someone’s head. You can see someone doing the dishes. You can’t see them remembering the field trip money.
  • It comes in tiny pieces. Thirty seconds here, two minutes there. Each piece is too small to mention, but together they fill a day.
  • Reliable work gets used to. A job that always gets done stops being noticed, and a person who is always there stops being thanked. Minds get used to whatever stays the same. That quiet drift is called complacency, and it is part of why unseen work can stay unseen for years.
  • We remember our own effort best. In a well-known 1979 study, married couples each estimated their own share of household jobs. For most couples, the two estimates added up to more than 100 percent. The likely reason isn’t dishonesty: we each remember our own effort more easily than someone else’s.

That last point is why this book never asks, “Who is right about how much they do?” Both people are usually describing their own week as they honestly remember it. The fix is to put both weeks on paper, side by side. Not seeing it is nobody’s fault. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

The reasoning. If you think the other person is choosing not to see, you’ll feel hurt and talk like a prosecutor. If you know the work is hidden by nature, you can talk like a guide: “Let me show you something.” The same facts, a very different talk.

Worked examples

  • Partners on different hours: Nadia works from home and Tom works shifts. Because Nadia is “around,” deliveries, repairs and the dog all drift to her. Tom honestly thinks she has “a quiet day.” They swap places for one Saturday, and Tom sees how many small interruptions there are. See Different hours.
  • A teen and their parents: Sixteen-year-old Ash looks after a younger brother three afternoons a week. Their parents see a child who is “home anyway.” When Ash writes down the snacks, homework help and settling arguments, the parents are surprised, and agree to pay Ash a little and cover one afternoon. See For teens.
  • Two partners after one has come home: After months working away, Sam comes home and doesn’t know where anything is now, or who does what. The partner who stayed has built a whole system in their head. Talking through the hidden system, slowly, helps Sam find a place in it. See Coming home.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “They should just notice.” Hidden work can’t be noticed from outside. Show it once, plainly, and see what happens next.
  • “They know, they just don’t care.” Sometimes that’s true, but it is rarely the first explanation. Start with “Did you know?” before deciding.
  • “I do more, so my memory must be right.” Everyone remembers their own effort best, including you. That is why both sides go on paper.

If only one of you is on board: Make your own work a little more visible without a big talk: a shared calendar, a list on the fridge, or a quick “I’ve booked the boiler service” message. Visible work is easier to thank and easier to share later.

Words you could use: “I don’t think you’ve been ignoring it. I think it’s been out of sight. Can I show you what it looks like from my side?”

A note on safety: If someone refuses to see your work and also mocks, threatens or punishes you when you raise it, the problem isn’t visibility. Signs to look out for.

Research notes

The 1979 study above is by Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly, and it is one of the best-known findings about how we remember shared work. Psychologists also describe “habituation”: we stop noticing things that stay the same, which helps explain why reliable work fades from view. This site’s idea: linking that fading to complacency in close relationships.

Writing it down is not keeping score

This is not a book about keeping score to win an argument. A ledger that only one person gets to read isn’t really a ledger. It’s a grudge (an old hurt you keep holding on to) with neater handwriting.

The point of writing anything down is the opposite of scorekeeping. It makes the hidden column visible to both people, in the same terms, at the same time. Then the conversation can start from facts you share instead of two different memories.

Here is a simple test. Scorekeeping looks backward and asks who owes whom. A shared ledger looks forward and asks what to change next week. If a list is being used to prove a point, it has turned into scorekeeping. Put it down, and come back to it when you can both use it to plan.

The reasoning. Scorekeeping makes people defensive, and defensive people stop listening. A shared ledger works only when both people feel safe to look at it. So the rules are simple: both write, both read, the past gets thanks, and the future gets the changes.

Worked examples

  • Two women married for twenty years, now with an empty nest: Without the kids, old patterns are suddenly loud. Instead of listing two decades of who did what, they each log one week of the new, quieter life and plan from there. See Empty nest.
  • Two friends sharing a holiday: One booked the flights, the rooms and the car. Instead of a resentful tally at the airport, she says, “I did the bookings this time. Could you do the planning for the day trips?” See Friends.
  • Dorm roommates: A cleaning rota starts to feel like a scoreboard. They swap it for a short room agreement they look at once a month, and only talk about what to change next. See Sharing a room.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If I don’t keep score, I’ll be taken advantage of.” Keeping a record is fine. Using it as a weapon isn’t. Write it to plan, and share it on a calm day.
  • “If they wrote less, they did less.” Some people write short lists. Ask about what’s missing before you decide.
  • Bringing the list into a fight. Put it away. Name a calm time to look at it together.

If only one of you is on board: Keep your list for your own clarity. Share what you need in one forward-looking sentence, without the list.

Words you could use: “I’m not trying to settle the past. Can we look at next week together?”

A note on safety: If you are made to account for every minute or every penny, that is not a shared ledger. It’s monitoring. Not safe at home?

Research notes

Psychologists Margaret Clark and Judson Mills described two kinds of relationships: “exchange” ones, where people track what they give and get, and “communal” ones, where people give based on each other’s needs. Their research suggests that strict tit-for-tat tallies tend to feel cold in close relationships. Studies of couples have also often found that how fair the split feels matters a lot for satisfaction, sometimes more than the exact hours. This site’s idea: a ledger read together, looking forward, is a way to keep caring about needs without letting one person’s load stay hidden.

Your side, their side

Every tool in this book follows one rule: each person fills in only their own side. You write what you did, how you felt and how full your stress tank is. You never fill those in for the other person, and they never fill them in for you.

This rule does two jobs. It keeps the facts honest, because you are the only expert on your own week. And it keeps the tools kind, because a list you write about someone else quickly starts to feel like a report on them. After you have each written your own side, you read them together.

Each of you also holds context the other can’t see: what your week was like, what you were carrying, what you assumed, what you meant. Giving yours, and asking for theirs, is how two people come to understand each other instead of trading two verdicts. It matters most when one of you has stopped noticing the other’s work, or the other has stopped saying so. Context is the way back in (see how to bring it up).

The reasoning. Everyone is the expert on their own week. As soon as one person writes about the other, the list becomes a report, and the other person has to defend themselves instead of adding what they know.

Worked examples

  • Three partners: Kai, Dani and Sol each write their own side. Dani’s list is short, but her context line says “two night shifts and a migraine.” Nobody argues about the short list. See More than two partners at home.
  • Two adult siblings who haven’t spoken much: After their father’s funeral, Joel and Marta each write what they did over those weeks. Marta had handled the will and the house; Joel had sat with their father every night in hospital. Neither knew what the other had carried. See Family rifts.
  • An interfaith couple: Yusuf keeps track of Ramadan, Eid and his family’s visits. Grace keeps track of Christmas, Easter and her family’s. Writing their own sides shows both of them are doing a full set of “festival work.” See Two faiths, one family.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “I’ll just fill in theirs to save time.” Don’t. Even a kind guess turns the list into a report on them.
  • “Context is making excuses.” Context isn’t an excuse. It’s what makes the numbers make sense. “I did less this week because my mum was in hospital” is information.
  • “My list is right and theirs is wrong.” Two honest lists can disagree. Talk about the gap with curiosity: “What did that job look like from your side?”

If only one of you is on board: Write only your side, and add your context. If you share it, say what it is: “This is just my week, as I saw it.”

Words you could use: “Would you write your side, in your own words? I won’t fill in anything for you.”

A note on safety: Nobody should be made to write their side, or have their notes read without asking. Your notes are yours to keep private.

Research notes

Psychologists use the term “naive realism” (from Lee Ross and colleagues) for the everyday sense that we see things as they really are, and that people who disagree must be missing something. Two side-by-side lists are a simple way to check that feeling. This site’s idea: the “your side, their side” rule for every tool.

Why the book borrows these words

You’ll notice this book borrows words from accounting, engineering and the science of stress. That’s on purpose. These fields are good at one particular thing: describing how a system is working right now without blaming anyone for it.

A set of accounts that doesn’t balance isn’t evil. A body under stress isn’t broken. Both are just information: a place to start, not a judgment of either person’s worth.

How the book fits together

What follows is set out as a working ledger, not a lecture. You read a chapter, try its tool, and build a small habit. The steps go like this:

  1. See the work. Each of you logs one ordinary week with WP-01, Who did what, then you read the two logs side by side.
  2. Notice when you’re out of step. Chapter I explains why fine words can still clash, and how to retune.
  3. Check whether the split can last. Chapter II turns your notes into one simple number about the setup, never about a person.
  4. Know what you’re carrying. Chapter III helps you tell leftover stress from this-moment stress, and name the angle you’re seeing things from.
  5. Agree on what fair means. Chapter IV gives every job a clear owner, the person who does it start to finish, and gives every comment a moment to land.
  6. Look back once a month. Chapter V finds the jobs that keep slipping and fixes the setup.

Along the way, three rhythms keep things running: a 90-second daily check-in (WP-13) that asks how much you were carrying today, a short weekly catch-up where you tally the week in the Lemonade Stand (a free page where each person lists their hours) and glance at your job list, and a monthly look-back (WP-04).

The goal isn’t a perfect 50/50 split measured to the minute. It’s a home where both people can see the whole picture, and neither one is quietly carrying a debt the other doesn’t know about.

Going at your own pace. You don’t have to do every step. Some people stop after the first week, because seeing it was enough. Others pick it up again a year later, after a new baby, a move or an illness. Coming back is part of the plan.

If only one of you is on board: Start at step 1 with only your own week, then read Part One, which is written for one person.

Words you could use: “Could we try one week, just to see what we notice? We can stop after that if it’s not useful.”

Who this book is for

The examples often use two people sharing a home, because that’s where unseen work is easiest to picture. But the ideas fit any shared load: partners, grown-up siblings looking after a parent, roommates, co-parents in two homes, friends on a trip, and coworkers sharing a role. How it fits your relationships shows where to start in each one.

The tools are written for two people. If more people share the load, each person still writes their own side, and you read all the lists together. Chapter II shows how to compare three or more shares.

Many kinds of shared life

Unseen work looks a little different in each kind of relationship. A few starting points:

  • Couples of every kind: same-sex, mixed-sex, married or not. Some research suggests same-sex couples often share housework more evenly, partly because they can’t fall back on old gender habits and have to talk it through. That talking is exactly what this book offers.
  • More than two partners: each person writes their own side, and you read all the lists together. More than two partners at home.
  • Friends: the plan-making and keeping in touch. Friends.

Your part, and only your part

You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back toward your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, one person keeps a private list for months and brings it out in the middle of an argument. The other feels ambushed, argues with the list, and nothing changes except that both feel worse.

When it goes right, both people write their own lists in the same week, on purpose. They read them on a calm evening. Each says what surprised them. They thank each other for something specific. Then they decide one small thing to change and when they’ll look again.

Questions people often ask

What if the other person doesn’t want to do this?

You can still start with your own list. Share it as information, not as a bill: “I wrote down my week because I was curious. Would you look at it with me?” Invite them, and let the answer be theirs. If they say no, that’s an answer too. Don’t bring the list up in a disagreement; keep it for your own clarity.

Do we have to write everything down forever?

No. One week is enough to start. If you use the monthly look-back later, a few lines a week is all it needs. If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a cup of coffee with the same questions works too.

What if our lists show a big gap?

That’s useful to know, and it doesn’t need fixing tonight. Chapter II and WP-03, Who owns each job, are there to help you rebalance calmly.

Does this only count chores?

No. Planning, remembering, keeping in touch and calming things down all count. Put them on the list with a rough number of minutes.

What if there are more than two of us?

Each person writes only their own side, and you read all the lists together. Look especially for jobs nobody claims, because those drift to whoever notices first. More than two partners at home and Sharing a room have fill-ins for groups.

What if one of us can’t do much right now?

That’s a season, not a failing. Illness, a new baby, grief or a hard job can all shrink what someone can give. Share by energy, not hours, and agree when you’ll look again. When one of you is ill and Grief go further.

Can I use this with my teenager?

Yes, gently. Let them write their own side, including school and the work of looking after siblings or pets. Thank them for something specific before you ask for anything new. For teens is written for them.

“I said I’d do it.” “I know, I’m just asking when.” Four sentences later, nobody is talking about the trash anymore, and neither of you can point to the moment it turned.

A picture, not a measurement. Nothing in this chapter records, analyzes or scores anyone’s real voice. It borrows an image from radio to explain why calm, kind people can sound harsh to each other when they’re under stress.

This chapter looks at everyday clashes in a different way: not as one person attacking the other, but as two signals that are slightly out of tune, like two radios on nearly the same station.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Drawn from psychology and neurobiology, with a picture borrowed from radio.)

  • In you: Know your own pace, your usual tone and what “soon” means to you. Notice when stress speeds you up or slows you down.
  • Between you and others: When a clash starts, treat it as a tuning problem, not a moral failing. Retune together, then translate what you meant into words the other person can hear.

The radio whistle

Tune two radios to stations that are close but not the same, and you don’t get silence. You get a whistle. The two signals overlap just enough to interfere, and they make a third, uglier sound that neither radio was sending. Engineers call this a heterodyne, or a “beat”: the extra tone that appears when two signals are almost, but not quite, in step.

This book borrows that picture for a familiar moment. Two people each say something reasonable. Each one’s tone is shaped by their own tiredness, hurry or stress. Together they get an argument that neither of them meant to start. The words weren’t the problem. The mismatch was.

The reasoning. If you think the other person attacked you, you defend. If you think two signals crossed, you check. The picture of a whistle gives you a third option between “you started it” and “I started it”: it started, between us.

Worked examples

  • Two mums at breakfast: “Have you seen my keys?” (rushed) meets “Where you left them, I expect” (tired). Neither line is cruel, but together they sound like a row. A laugh and “Sorry, that came out wrong” clears it.
  • A coworker pair: “Can you send that over?” in a team chat, with no greeting, lands as cold. The sender was typing on a phone in a lift. See At work.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “The words were fine, so it’s their problem.” The words may be fine. The clash lives in the mix, so both of you can help clear it.
  • Arguing about the exact words. “I said ‘can you’, not ‘you should’” rarely helps. Talk about how it came across instead.

If only one of you is on board: You can still notice the whistle and lower your own pace and volume. One radio moving closer to the other already cuts the static.

Words you could use: “That came out sharper than I meant.” “I think we just crossed wires.”

Research notes

The whistle is real physics: two close frequencies combine into a “beat” at the difference between them. Using it for people is this site’s idea, a picture, not a measurement. Psychologists Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec (1998) described the “illusion of transparency”: we tend to think our inner states and intentions are more obvious to others than they really are. So the sender often assumes the kindness was clear.

Why being nearly in step is the noisy part

The pitch of that radio whistle depends on the gap between the two stations. When the gap shrinks to nothing, the whistle disappears. When two stations are very far apart, the two signals don’t mix into anything you can hear.

People can work the same way. Strangers who barely interact rarely clash. People who share a home, a family or a job are close enough to overlap all day. That closeness is a good thing, and it also means small differences in pace or tone rub against each other again and again. A clash between two close people usually doesn’t mean they are badly matched. Often it means they are nearly matched, and just slightly off. Research also suggests we check our meaning less with people we know well, because we assume they already understand us.

The reasoning. This flips a painful thought. “We clash, so we must be wrong for each other” becomes “We clash because we are close, and we skip the checking.” The fix isn’t distance. It’s a little more checking.

Worked examples

  • Best friends since school: They finish each other’s sentences, so they never check. One says “Let’s do something for your birthday,” meaning a meal; the other expects a weekend away. See Friends.
  • A grandparent and a grown-up child: A father says, “I’ll have the kids Saturday, as usual.” His daughter had planned a family day and feels her plans were taken over. They are so used to each other that nobody asked. See For grown-up children.
  • A couple together for decades: With the children gone, they are home together more, and small differences in pace rub more often. See Empty nest and Retired together.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If they really knew me, I wouldn’t have to explain.” Knowing someone well makes us explain less, which is exactly when misreadings creep in. Explaining is care, not a sign of distance.
  • “We used to be in sync, so something is broken.” Lives change: new jobs, new babies, new hours. Drift is normal. Retune to the new life.

If only one of you is on board: Check your own meaning out loud more often, especially with the people closest to you. It costs a sentence and saves an evening.

Words you could use: “I know we usually just get each other, but let me check: did you mean…?”

Research notes

Kenneth Savitsky, Boaz Keysar, Nicholas Epley and colleagues (2011) found what they called the “closeness-communication bias”: people overestimated how well they communicated with friends and spouses, in some tests not doing better than with strangers. That is the finding behind this section.

What sets the frequency

Three things tend to shift how someone comes across, often without them noticing:

  • Pace. How fast you talk, how quickly you reply, and how long a pause feels. Someone who is tense may talk faster, jump in sooner, and read an ordinary pause as the silent treatment.
  • Tone. A comment meant as a simple update (“the sink’s full again”) can be sent, or heard, as a complaint. It depends on the tone underneath it.
  • Urgency. One person’s “when you get a chance” can mean tonight. Another person’s can mean next week. Neither is wrong, but they aren’t the same.

The reasoning. Pace, tone and urgency are the parts of a message that aren’t in the words, so they’re the parts most likely to differ. Naming them gives you three quick questions to check, instead of one big, vague “Why are you being like this?”

Worked examples

  • Interfaith or cross-cultural families: In Amal’s family, people talk over each other to show they care. In Ben’s family, interrupting is rude. At Sunday lunch, Ben hears a fight and Amal hears a lively chat. Neither family is wrong; their pace is set differently. See Two faiths, one family.
  • Gaming and screen time: “Coming!” from a teenager in the middle of an online match means “when the round ends,” which might be ten minutes. To the parent at the table, it meant now. Agreeing what “coming” means ends a nightly fight. See Gaming and time together.
  • Shift workers: A text sent at 3am by a night-shift nurse (“Can we sort the bins?”) arrives to a partner just waking up, and feels urgent. It wasn’t. A rule helps: “Non-urgent texts go in one morning message.” See Different hours.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “My tone was fine.” Maybe it was, to you. Ask how it landed rather than defending how it was sent.
  • “‘When you get a chance’ is obvious.” It isn’t. Add a time.
  • “Texting is safer.” Texts are calmer to send but easier to misread. For anything tender, add a feeling word, or call.

If only one of you is on board: Fix your own sending first. Add a time to your requests, say “this is just an update” before updates, and slow your own pace. One clearer signal already cuts the noise.

Words you could use: “Is this a now thing, a today thing, or a this-week thing?” “I’m not upset, I’m just in a hurry.”

Research notes

Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues (2005) found that people sending emails were too confident their tone (such as sarcasm or seriousness) would be read correctly. Anthropologist Edward T. Hall described “high-context” and “low-context” communication styles (1976): in some families and cultures, much of the meaning is in tone and setting; in others, it is in the exact words. This site’s idea: the three dials of pace, tone and urgency.

How stress changes your frequency

How your body feels changes how you sound and how you hear. The site describes three everyday states, on what it calls the Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check. It gives you a best guess, not a reading:

  • Calm and connected. Breathing is easy. You can hear something hard without getting defensive.
  • Revved up. You feel tense and fast. Your heart is quicker and thoughts move fast. Small things feel urgent or personal. Pace speeds up, and tone gets sharper.
  • Running on empty. You feel tired and flat, and words are harder to find. Replies get slower and shorter, which the other person may read as not caring.

These labels come from polyvagal theory, whose main claims are still debated by scientists. Here they are only everyday words, not a medical model. “Running on empty” just means ordinary, everyday tiredness. The point is simple: when you are revved up or running on empty, you are more likely to send and hear signals off-key. Chapter III goes deeper into how much stress you bring into a conversation from elsewhere.

The reasoning. The same words can be easy on a calm day and impossible on a tired one. If you check your state first, you can choose to wait, to warn (“I’m low, so I’ll be brief”), or to go ahead. If you skip it, your state chooses for you.

Worked examples

  • A partner with a long-term illness: On high-pain days, Jo’s replies get short. Her partner learned to read short replies as “pain,” not “cross,” and to ask, “Is this a low day?” See When one of you is ill.
  • A foster carer and a child: A child who has been through a lot may jump straight to “revved up” at a raised voice. The carer lowers their own voice and slows down first, before any talk about the broken plate. See Foster, adoptive and kinship families.
  • A parent and a child with ADHD: At 5pm, after a long school day, both are running on empty. They move homework talk to after a snack and ten minutes outside. See When a child has ADHD.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “You’re revved up” as a put-down. The ladder is for checking yourself. Don’t use it to label someone else.
  • “I’m always running on empty, so I can never talk.” If that’s true for weeks, it’s a sign to rest and get support, not to stop talking forever. Talk briefly, at your best time of day.
  • “Calm means I’m not allowed to be upset.” Calm and connected doesn’t mean happy. You can be calm and sad, or calm and firm.

If only one of you is on board: Check your own state and say it out loud. “I’m running on empty, so I’ll keep this short” changes how your words land, even if the other person never uses the ladder.

Words you could use: “I’m revved up right now. Give me ten minutes and I’ll be easier to talk to.”

A note on safety: If you are tense because you are afraid of how someone will react, that is not just stress. Your body may be warning you. Not safe at home? has free, private help.

Research notes

Sleep researchers in Matthew Walker’s lab (2007) found that after a night without sleep, the brain’s alarm center (the amygdala) reacted much more strongly to upsetting pictures. John Gottman’s research on couples described “flooding”: feeling so overwhelmed in a conflict that listening becomes very hard. As noted above, the three labels here are everyday words, not a medical model.

Nobody has to be wrong

None of this needs either person to be at fault. Two radios that both work perfectly will still whistle if they are slightly off. No amount of arguing about who’s “really” to blame fixes the interference. Retuning does.

Zhuangzi’s Empty Boat makes the same point. A man in a boat is angry when another boat bumps into his, until he sees that it is empty. With no one to blame, his anger goes. Much of the time, the bump was no one’s plan. That doesn’t mean a bump never matters. It means starting with curiosity instead of blame.

People are also wired differently. Some notice every shift in tone. Others take words very literally. Wired Differently explains how a mix-up between two differently wired people usually runs both ways.

The reasoning. Blame narrows attention to one person. “No fault” widens it to the setup: the time of day, the tiredness, the unclear words. Once the setup is in view, there is usually something easy to change.

Worked examples

  • A neurodivergent and a neurotypical partner: One partner says exactly what they mean. The other listens for hints. “It’s fine” means “it’s fine” to one, and “it’s not fine” to the other. Both are reading carefully, just in different ways. See Wired Differently.
  • Separated co-parents: A message about pickup times turns into a row about the past. They agree that pickup messages are facts only, and feelings go in a separate monthly call. See Co-parenting.
  • Someone coming out to family: After Leah comes out, her mother asks, “Are you sure?” Leah hears doubt; her mother meant fear for her. Naming that both were scared helps them start again. See Coming out as an adult.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “No fault means nothing matters.” It means start with curiosity. If something hurtful keeps happening after it’s been named, that matters, and it is fair to say so.
  • “If I don’t blame them, I must blame me.” No. Neither. Look at the setup.

If only one of you is on board: You can drop blame on your side, even if the other person doesn’t. “I don’t think you meant that the way I heard it” often softens things quickly.

Words you could use: “I don’t think you meant that the way I heard it. Can you tell me what you meant?”

A note on safety: “Nobody has to be wrong” is for ordinary clashes. It never means excusing threats, put-downs or fear. If someone frightens you, it is not a tuning problem. Not safe at home?

Research notes

Psychologist Lee Ross named the “fundamental attribution error” (1977): we tend to blame someone’s character for what was really caused by their situation. Damian Milton (2012) described the “double empathy problem”: when autistic and non-autistic people misunderstand each other, the gap runs both ways. Zhuangzi’s Empty Boat is a story, not a study.

Retuning, step by step

Before you carry on with a conversation that has started to whistle:

  1. Name the mismatch out loud, without blame. “I think we’re on different wavelengths right now.” This turns “you against me” into “us against the static.”
  2. Check your own state on the Nervous System Ladder. It’s hard to retune while you are revved up or running on empty. If you are, the Calm-Down Kit (WP-11), a simple plan for heated moments, comes first. Either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.
  3. Slow the pace on purpose. Take shorter turns. Leave more pauses. Lower your voice. Keep going like this until the static clears.
  4. Only then, go back to the topic. The topic still matters. You are just coming back to it when you can both hear properly.

The reasoning. Each step does one job. Naming it stops the spiral. Checking your state tells you whether you can go on. Slowing down lowers the heat. Going back to the topic keeps retuning from turning into avoiding.

Worked examples

  • Housemates: A row about noise at midnight. One says, “We’re both tired. Can we talk about this at breakfast?” At breakfast, it takes five minutes. See Sharing a room.
  • Three partners: When a talk gets heated, the third partner gently says, “Static,” and all three take a pause. They agreed the word on a calm day, and anyone can use it. See More than two partners at home.
  • Long-distance partners: A video call drops mid-argument and each assumes the other hung up. Now they have a rule: if the line drops in a hard talk, text “line dropped, still here” and call back after ten minutes. See Long distance.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Pausing without a time to return. That feels like being shut out. Always name a time.
  • Coming back too soon. If you’re still revved up, take longer. A few minutes is often not enough.
  • Retuning only when you are losing. Use the pause early, when you notice the static, not as an escape hatch.

If only one of you is on board: You can still pause yourself: “I need a few minutes so I can listen properly. I’ll be back at eight.” Then come back at eight.

Words you could use: “I want to hear you properly, and I can’t right now. Can we pick this up at eight?”

A note on safety: Anyone can call a pause, and nobody should be blocked from leaving the room. If you are followed, blocked or punished for pausing, that is a safety issue, not a tuning one. Signs to look out for.

Research notes

John Gottman’s work on couples suggests taking a real break when flooded, often at least twenty minutes, and doing something soothing rather than rehearsing the argument. Matthew Lieberman and colleagues (2007) found that putting feelings into words (“affect labeling”) can lower the brain’s alarm response, which may be part of why naming the mismatch helps. A worked example: the trash, replayed

Here’s the Tuesday scene again, slowed down.

Alex is tired and has a late shift tonight. Sam is tired and worried because the trash goes out first thing tomorrow. Sam asks, “Are you taking out the trash?” The words are fine. But Sam’s urgency is set to “tonight,” and Alex’s is set to “before bed.” Sam is revved up, so the question comes out quickly. Alex hears the speed as nagging and says, “I said I’d do it.” Sam hears the snap and says, “I’m just asking when.” Now both are defending themselves, and the trash is forgotten.

Now the retuned version. After Alex’s snap, Sam pauses and says, “I think we’re out of tune. I’m a bit tense about tomorrow.” Alex takes a breath: “Me too, long day.” They slow down. Sam goes back in with one clear sentence: “The trash needs to be out by 7am tomorrow. I’ll feel calmer if I know it’s out tonight. Could you take it out before bed?” Alex says yes, and names a time. The whole thing takes under a minute.

Two more replays

Adult siblings planning a parent’s birthday: Priya messages her brother, “We need to decide about Mum’s party.” Arun, who lives far away and feels left out of decisions, hears “you’re late again.” He replies, “Do whatever you want.” Retuned: Priya calls instead. “I think the message came out wrong. I really want your ideas. Could we decide by Sunday?” Arun says he’d love to do the cake. See Family rifts.

Two dads and a toddler at bedtime: Chris (revved up, late for a call) says, “Can you just do bath tonight?” Malik (running on empty) hears “you never do bath.” Malik snaps. Retuned: Chris says, “Sorry, I’m rushing. I’ve got a call at seven. Could you do bath, and I’ll do the morning?” A fact, a reason, an offer. Malik says yes.

Going back in: say it so it lands

When you return to the topic, it helps to have one clear sentence ready. WP-09, Say it so it lands, sorts what you want to say into three parts:

  • The fact: one sentence, with no blame words. “The trash wasn’t out before pickup.”
  • The feeling: what’s underneath. “I felt a bit on my own with it.”
  • The ask: what you would like to happen next. “Could we pick a fixed night for it?”

If your sentence starts with “you always” or “you never,” pause and swap it for one fact and one ask. The Signal Translator can show you how a sentence might land before you say it.

The reasoning. After a clash, both people are still a bit raw. A clear sentence gives them something solid to hold: one fact to agree on, one feeling to understand, one ask to say yes or no to.

Worked examples

  • Business partners: “The invoice to Patel & Co went out three weeks late. I felt worried about cash flow. Could we set a reminder for the 1st of each month?”
  • A teen to a parent: “You read my messages on Tuesday. I felt really embarrassed. Could you ask me first next time?” See For teens.
  • A grown-up child to a parent: “You gave the baby sweets after I said no. I felt overruled. Could you check with me about food?” See For grown-up children.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Packing in three facts and two asks. One of each. Save the rest for another day.
  • A feeling that is really a blame (“I feel you don’t care”). Use a feeling word about you: worried, alone, rushed, hurt.
  • An ask that is really a rule (“You will…”). An ask can get a no. If it can’t, it isn’t an ask.

If only one of you is on board: A clear sentence works even if the other person doesn’t know the method. You don’t have to explain it. Just say it.

Words you could use: “Here’s what happened, how I felt, and what would help.”

Research notes

Sybil Carrère and John Gottman (1999) found that the first few minutes of a couple’s conflict conversation, gentle or harsh, predicted a lot about how things went for them later. The fact, feeling, ask pattern is close to well-known methods such as “I-statements” (from Thomas Gordon’s work in the 1970s) and Marshall Rosenberg’s Nonviolent Communication, which uses observation, feeling, need and request.

Staying in tune: small and often

Radios drift. So do people. Habit research suggests that small routines tied to a regular cue, like the same time each day, are easier to keep than big efforts made rarely. That hasn’t been proven for couples, but it is a sensible way to stay in tune: tiny corrections often, instead of one big correction after weeks of drift.

The quiet stretches drift most. When things have been fine for a long time, nobody listens for the whistle, and a small offset can grow for months without a sound. That is complacency: nothing breaks on the day the checking stops. A tiny, regular correction is the cure, and it works best when each of you can add a little context about your own signal (how the day went for you), so a flat reply is read as “tired,” not “cold.”

That’s the idea behind WP-13, the 90-second daily check-in. The idea comes from electronics, where a small circuit keeps one signal in step with another by making tiny, constant corrections. Once a day, each of you says how much you were carrying today, one thing you appreciated, and, if you like, one small thing from your day that didn’t feel great (about the day, not about each other) and one thing that would help tomorrow. Either of you can pass, any day. You just listen. Nothing gets solved there.

If you want to see whether retuning is becoming a habit, the full chore-split calculator (CALC-01) has a “retuning frequency”: the number of moments that got retuned, divided by the number of moments that landed badly. For example, if five things landed badly this week and you retuned three of them before answering, that’s 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60. It isn’t a grade. It only shows whether repair is happening more often over time. If nothing landed badly this week, leave it blank. With numbers this small, watch the trend over several weeks, not any one week.

The reasoning. Big talks are hard to schedule and easy to dread. A tiny daily one costs almost nothing, and it catches drift while it’s still small enough to fix with one sentence.

Worked examples

  • Parents on opposite shifts: They leave a 90-second voice note at handover: how full their tank is, and one thing the other should know. See Different hours.
  • Friends who live far apart: A Sunday “rose and thorn” message: one good thing, one hard thing from the week.
  • Someone returning home after time away: For the first two weeks back, a short evening check-in helps them learn the new rhythm of the house. See Coming home.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Turning the check-in into a problem-solving session. Just listen. Save fixes for the weekly catch-up.
  • Dropping it when things are good. The good stretches are when drift starts. Keep it tiny so it survives.
  • Making it a duty. Either person can pass, any day, no questions asked.

If only one of you is on board: Do a check-in with yourself: how full is my tank, what did I appreciate, what would help tomorrow. You can also simply share your own line each day and let the other person join in if they like.

Words you could use: “Quick check-in? Mine: medium tank today, and I liked that you made coffee.”

Research notes

A 2010 study led by Phillippa Lally found that new daily habits took, on average, around two months to feel automatic, with a wide range from person to person, and that missing a single day didn’t undo progress. Gottman’s research on “bids” (small moments of reaching out) and turning toward them is linked to how couples stay close. See Turning toward.

Your part, and only your part

You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace, tone and urgency, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow.

  • Learn your early signs of drifting: a tight chest, a clipped reply, wanting to win.
  • Learn how you take words in best, and say it once: “I hear things better in writing.”
  • When you drift, step out, settle and come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission to retune yourself.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, both people push on. Each tries to be understood before trying to understand. Voices rise, the topic changes three times, and later neither can remember how it started. The next time the same topic comes up, both already feel tense.

When it goes right, one person notices the static early and names it kindly. Both slow down. The topic comes back a few minutes later, or at a time you agree on, with one clear sentence. Next time, the phrase “we’re out of tune” works a little faster, because you both know what it means.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t “we’re out of sync” just a way of avoiding the subject?

It can be, if it’s used to end every hard talk. That’s why the retuning steps always finish by going back to the topic, and why a pause always comes with a time to return.

What if only one of us wants to retune?

You can still retune yourself. Slow your own pace, lower your own voice, and say your own sentence clearly. One radio moving closer to the other already cuts the static.

What if we keep getting out of tune about the same thing?

Then it’s probably not just a tuning problem. Repeated clashes about one job often mean it has no clear owner: one person who does it, start to finish. Chapter IV and One owner per job (WP-03) help with that, and Chapter V shows how to spot the pattern.

Does this work over text?

Partly. Text strips out tone, so it is easy to drift. Keep texts for facts and times, add a feeling word if something is tender, and move to a call or a face-to-face talk if two messages in a row land badly.

What about with my teenager, or a child?

Children and teens drift faster when they’re tired, hungry or worried, and they rely on you to settle first. Lower your own voice, shorten your sentences, and come back to the topic after a snack or some sleep. For teens and When a child has ADHD have more.

What if we speak different first languages?

Then pace and tone differ even more, and so do words like “soon.” Check meanings often and kindly, and assume good intent first. Two faiths, one family has ideas for mixed families.

Can I talk it through with someone first?

Yes. Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you find a calm sentence to go back in with.

Limits and cautions

  • This is a picture to think with. It doesn’t measure anyone’s voice, and it can’t prove who said what.
  • Retuning helps with ordinary clashes. It isn’t meant to hide a real disagreement. Once you’re back in tune, the disagreement still deserves a proper talk.
  • Use it on yourself first. “You’re out of tune” said as an accusation is just another off-key signal. “I think we’re out of tune” works better.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

  • The tangle often isn’t about the words.
  • Three things knock you out of tune.
  • Nobody has to be wrong.
  • Get back in tune before you carry on.

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about. When the other one says it feels even, they mean it. From inside each person’s own week, it does.

Before anything else: this chapter describes a rough self-reflection tool, not a scientific instrument. Its number, the setup score, is not a proven statistical model, and it never judges either person or the worth of the relationship. It scores one narrow thing: whether the way you currently share the household work looks like it can last, based on what you wrote down. Think of a small shop asking whether it can keep paying its bills, month after month, not whether it had a good Saturday. The answer is about the shop, not about whether the owners are good people.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Borrowed from ledger accounting, systems thinking and nervous-system science.)

  • In you: See your own share honestly, from what you wrote down rather than from memory. See the way you share work as a setup you can redesign, not a flaw in you.
  • Between you and others: Put both people’s numbers on one page, and read the result as a note about the setup, never a verdict on a person. Clear owners leave less room for blame.

Where the idea comes from

  • The check uses only what you both wrote down (your logs and your own self-ratings), not what either of you remembers in the heat of the moment. Whatever it shows is about the setup, never about either of you.
  • The idea is borrowed from accounting, where people ask whether a business can keep going on what comes in. Here, a setup that can last is one where the work gets done without one person quietly running out of energy.
  • The result is a simple weighted sum we call the setup score. It isn’t a probability, and it doesn’t predict anything.

What the check is for

Sooner or later, everyone who shares a home asks some version of “Is this actually working?” Usually the answer comes from whatever happened most recently: last night’s dishes, this morning’s tone. That answers the question at the wrong scale.

The check asks a narrower, calmer question: based on what’s actually written down this month, does the way we share the work look like it can last, or is it quietly running down its reserves? “Written down” means three things: the hours logged in the Lemonade Stand (a free page where each person lists their hours) or in Who did what (WP-01), the jobs with named owners in the One owner per job list (WP-03), and each person’s own stress score from How much are you carrying? (WP-02). You’ll meet the stress meter and the job list properly in Chapters III and IV; rough numbers are fine for now.

Why written down, not remembered. Memory is not a neutral record. You remember your own effort in detail, because you were there for all of it. You mostly hear about the other person’s effort second-hand, or not at all. So almost everyone sincerely feels they do a bit more than their share. Writing down one ordinary week is the simplest fix anyone has found.

Research note. In a well-known 1979 study, psychologists Michael Ross and Fiore Sicoly asked married partners, separately, how much of each everyday task they were responsible for. Added together, the two answers usually came to more than 100%. Nobody was lying. Each person simply remembered their own part more easily. Later studies found the same pattern in work teams and group projects.

The same question in different homes:

  • Housemates in a shared flat: “Is this working?” usually arrives as a note on the fridge after a bad weekend. A month of logs turns the note into a conversation. See Sharing a room.
  • A long-distance couple: They don’t share a kitchen, but they share the work of staying close: planning visits, booking travel, starting the calls. That work can be logged too. See Long distance.
  • A grandfather raising his granddaughter: His “household” includes her school, her social worker and her other grandparents. The check helps him see whether his load can last, and what to ask the wider family for. See Foster and kinship families.

A common mix-up: “We only need this when things are bad.” The checks you take in calm months are the ones that make a bad month easier to read, because you have something to compare it with.

Words you could use: “I don’t think either of us is keeping count on purpose. Can we each write down one normal week, so we’re looking at the same picture?”

What counts as work: the parts nobody sees

A log only helps if it catches the whole job. Many jobs have a part you can see (doing it) and a part you can’t (noticing it needs doing, planning it, deciding how, and checking it got done). The hidden part is easy to miss from the outside, and it often settles on one person by habit.

  • Thinking work: meal planning, the shopping list, school forms, remembering appointments, knowing when the car needs a service.
  • Keeping-in-touch work: birthdays, cards, calls to older relatives, arranging get-togethers, smoothing things over in the wider family.
  • Care work: medicines, appointments and comfort for a child, an older parent or a partner who is ill. See When one of you is ill and Caregivers.
  • Money admin: paying bills, tracking spending, sorting tax and benefits. See Money together.
  • Faith and culture work: getting ready for festivals, keeping traditions, teaching children two sets of customs. See Two faiths, one family.
  • Carer paperwork: for foster and kinship carers, reviews, meetings and contact arrangements.

Log these in minutes. Five minutes of remembering, ten times a week, is close to an hour.

Research note. Sociologist Arlie Hochschild’s book The Second Shift (1989) described working parents, mostly mothers, coming home to a second job of housework and childcare. Sociologist Allison Daminger (2019) later described the “cognitive” part of household work: anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. In the couples she studied, that part was often less evenly shared than the physical tasks. Anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo (1987) used the term “kin work” for the work of keeping a wider family connected. Most of this research looked at mother-and-father households, but the same jobs exist in every kind of home.

A common mix-up: “Thinking isn’t really work.” If it takes time and attention, and the house would notice if it stopped, it counts.

Words you could use: “Can we add the remembering and planning to the list? I think a lot of my week is in that part, and I’d like us both to see it.”

Workload balance

Take the week’s logged hours and work out each person’s share as a percentage. Then:

workload_balance = 1 − (difference between the two percentages ÷ 100)

A perfectly even week (50% and 50%) scores 1.00. A week where one person did everything (100% and 0%) scores 0.00. A 70/30 week scores 1 − 40 ÷ 100 = 0.60.

The score is the same whichever person is doing more. The arithmetic doesn’t care who is carrying the load, only whether it is shared. Rough hours are fine. Counting to the minute would only look more precise than it is.

Balance doesn’t look at the total. A 50/50 split of a very long week can still wear you both out, which is one reason stress is in the formula too.

If you agreed an uneven split. If you both agreed an uneven split that suits you (say, one of you works longer hours outside the home), compare against the agreed split instead of 50/50. The full calculator has a setting for this.

Three or more of you. Compare each person’s share with an even share (100 ÷ number of people), or with a split you agreed. The Lemonade Stand does the arithmetic for you.

The reasoning. An uneven load keeps building, whether anyone notices it or not. A person can carry more than their share for a season, for a new baby, an exam or an illness. A setup that needs one person to carry more for ever is, almost by definition, one that can’t last.

Three or more, worked through. Three partners in a triad log 15, 9 and 6 hours: 30 in all, so their shares are 50%, 30% and 20%. An even share is a third each. The full calculator works out how much of the week would have to change hands to make it even (about 0.17, a sixth of the week), and compares it with the most that could ever need to move (0.67, if one person did everything). Balance = 1 − 0.17 ÷ 0.67 = 0.75. With two people, this gives exactly the same answer as the simple formula above. See More than two partners at home.

In different homes:

  • Shift workers: Kemi works nights and Jo works days. Their home hours split 50/50, but Kemi does her half on four hours’ sleep. Balance looks perfect. The stress input tells the rest. They move the jobs that need a clear head to whoever is rested, not just to whoever has time. See Different hours.
  • When one partner is ill: Two women, one with a long-term illness, agree an 80/20 split by energy for now. Against an even split, balance is 1 − 60 ÷ 100 = 0.40. Against their agreed split, it’s 1.00. The agreed number is the honest one, as long as the agreement is real and they look at it again every few months. See When one of you is ill.
  • A parent and a teenager: 50/50 isn’t the goal here. They agree something like 85/15, which grows as the teen does. Logging still helps, because it shows the teen what the house takes. See Teens.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “We’re 50/50, so we’re fine.” Balance ignores the total. Two people evenly sharing a very heavy week can both be worn out. Fix: read balance next to stress, never alone.
  • “The agreed split is whatever the main earner says.” An agreed split has to be agreed by everyone it affects. Fix: write it down with the date, and a date to look at it again.
  • “Paid work cancels out home work.” The split counts home jobs. Paid work sits beside it. If one of you works longer hours outside, that’s exactly what an agreed split is for.

If only one of you is on board: You can’t work out balance alone, but you can log your own hours. Share your total as information, not as a charge: “I spent about 14 hours on the house this week. I didn’t know it was that many.”

A note on safety: An “agreed” uneven split is only agreed if each person could have said no. If one person sets the split, controls the money that could pay for help, or makes the other afraid to raise it, the number isn’t the problem, and no formula will fix it. Money together explains the difference between normal limits and control, and Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Research note. Large time-use surveys in many countries (such as the American Time Use Survey) have found for decades that, on average, women in mixed-sex couples do more unpaid housework and care than men, though the gap has narrowed. Psychologist Lawrence Kurdek’s studies of gay and lesbian couples found they were more likely to divide housework in a balanced way, often by negotiating rather than by default. Across many studies, whether the split feels fair to each person matters more for how happy they are than whether it is exactly equal. None of these averages describe your home. That’s why the check uses your own log.

Words you could use: “I want our split to be one we both chose, not one that just happened.”

Ownership clarity

In WP-03, every regular job gets two names. The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The second name is a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. They can be the same person, though a second name gives the job a gentle check: if one person is both names, nobody else will notice a slip. (The idea is borrowed from project work. The doer-and-backstop pairing also borrows the “maker and checker” habit from internal controls, where one person does a task and another looks it over.)

ownership_clarity = jobs with both names filled in ÷ all jobs on the list

So if you list 10 jobs and 8 have both names, the score is 0.80.

One warning: don’t improve this score by shortening the list. Deleting the jobs nobody owns raises the number and changes nothing at home. If a job is real and unowned, it belongs on the list as an unowned row. That’s exactly the row the score is there to show.

The reasoning. Work that anyone could do tends to be done by no one. When a job belongs to everyone, each person feels a little less responsible for it, and assumes someone else has it. Naming one owner is the cheapest fix there is. The backstop solves a second problem: when the owner is ill, away or overloaded, somebody already knows the job exists.

Research note. In the “bystander” studies by John Darley and Bibb Latané (1968), people were slower to help when they believed others were also there to help: responsibility spread thin across the group. Project managers use responsibility charts (one well-known kind is called RACI) that name exactly one person accountable for each task. The owner-and-backstop pair is a home-sized version of that habit. This site’s idea: the backstop’s job is to notice and ask kindly, never to inspect.

In different homes:

  • Dorm roommates: Four students, one bathroom, nobody’s job. They write a room agreement with one owner each week, rotating, and a backstop who is next on the list. See Sharing a room.
  • Adult siblings and a parent: After their mother’s stroke, the sister who lived nearby became the owner of everything by default. Listing the jobs let her brother, three hundred miles away, own the bills and the insurance calls. If siblings are barely speaking, the list can be shared in writing, or through a cousin both trust. See Adult siblings and family rifts.
  • Foster carers: Two carers split the agency paperwork, school meetings and the drive to contact visits. Each job gets an owner, and the backstop covers when the owner is at work. See Foster and kinship families.
  • Business partners: Two partners in a small firm both “keep an eye on” the shared inbox, so customers wait for days. One owner per weekday ends it. See At work.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “The owner just does the visible part.” Then the remembering stays with the old owner. Fix: the owner owns the whole job, including noticing, planning and remembering.
  • “The backstop checks the owner’s work.” Fix: the backstop doesn’t inspect, redo or grade. They notice a slip and ask once, kindly.
  • “I’ll just own everything, to be safe.” Ownership goes up and balance goes down. The score catches this, because balance carries more weight.
  • “We handed it over, so it’s done.” A job handed over without the know-how comes back. Fix: write a two-line note: when it’s due, and where things are.

If only one of you is on board: List the jobs you own now, including the thinking part. Then offer to hand over one whole job, with its note. A specific offer is easier to say yes to than “you need to do more.”

A note on safety: A backstop who checks constantly, criticizes, or punishes a slip is not a backstop. If “checking” feels like being watched or tested, or you feel afraid of getting a job wrong, that’s not a setup problem. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Words you could use: “I’ll own the bins from now on, including remembering bin night. Can you be the backstop, and just text me if you see they’re still out?”

Leftover stress (how full your tank is)

Each of you fills in your own How much are you carrying? (WP-02), about yourself only. It measures how full your stress tank is: a higher score means more stress in the tank. You score five things from 0 to 4: sleep, workload elsewhere, any unresolved conflict, how your body feels, and time pressure today. Add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 up to 0.60 is moderate, and 0.60 or more is high. Then take the average of your two scores.

Look at each person’s own score too. If either one is 0.60 or more, treat that as the headline, whatever the average says.

This is the only input that counts against the result. The formula uses 1 − stress, so more stress means a lower score. That’s on purpose: a way of sharing work that only holds up when both people are well rested isn’t one that can last.

Never estimate the other person’s score for them. If one column is missing, the full calculator won’t work it out, and that’s the right answer.

Why only you can score you. Stress is felt from the inside. A guess about someone else’s tank, however kind, quietly overrules their own account of their life. And the scores only mean something if both were made the same way, by the person who lives with them.

In different homes:

  • A carer and a partner who is ill: The carer scores 0.75 for three months running. The split may be as fair as it can be, and still not last. The fix is outside help, not a better split. See Caregivers.
  • A parent and a teenager in exam season: The teen scores 0.65. They agree to lighten the teen’s jobs for three weeks, with an end date. That is an agreed uneven split, chosen on purpose.
  • New parents: Both score over 0.60. That is the headline. Big changes to the setup can wait, and small ones (one owner for night feeds on weekends) can’t. See New parents.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “Your score is low, so you’ve got no excuse.” A score describes a tank. It never sets what someone owes. Fix: read it as information, not as a ruling.
  • “I’m more stressed than you.” It isn’t a contest. Both scores are true. Fix: plan around the higher one.
  • “My body score is always high.” With a long-term illness or disability, the body item may be high every day. Fix: score honestly, and compare with your own usual, not with anyone else’s.

If only one of you is on board: Score your own tank and share the band, not the details: “I’m in the high band this week.” That alone can change how a conversation goes.

A note on safety: If you feel pressured to report a lower score than you feel, or your score is used against you, stop using the tool. And if the stress in your tank is fear of someone at home, that isn’t leftover stress. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Research note. Researchers study stress with tested questionnaires, such as Sheldon Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (1983). WP-02 is not one of them. This site’s idea: it is a short reflection prompt made for this book, and it can’t tell you anything about your health. The everyday idea that stress adds up over time does have research behind it: the neuroscientist Bruce McEwen called that build-up “allostatic load.” WP-02 borrows only the everyday idea.

Words you could use: “My tank is about 0.6 this week. It’s not about you. It’s work and sleep. Can we keep tonight light?”

The formula, in plain language

Rather than a mystery score, the setup score is a simple weighted sum:

setup_score = (workload_balance_score * 0.4)
            + (ownership_clarity_score * 0.35)
            + (1 - average_leftover_stress) * 0.25

The weights add up to 1, so the result is always between 0 and 1. Read it as a snapshot, not a sentence. The calculator Is the setup working for everyone? (CALC-01) does the arithmetic for you:

  • 0.7 or above: The current setup is holding. Keep the same rhythm of check-ins (short, regular talks about how things are going).
  • 0.4 up to 0.7: Something in the three inputs is drifting, often ownership first. Start with the biggest shortfall (see below).
  • Below 0.4: The work, as it is currently shared, doesn’t look like it can last. That’s a statement about the setup, not about either person’s love, effort or worth. Setups get renegotiated all the time.

Why these weights?

The weights are a judgment, not a result from research. They say that uneven hours matter a little more than unclear ownership, and that both matter more than everyday stress. Unclear ownership comes a close second because it’s what turns a fixable imbalance into a repeating one: work with no owner falls to whoever notices first, every time. If you change the weights in the full calculator, write the change down, because your old results won’t compare with your new ones.

This site’s idea. The formula, the weights and the three bands were made for this book. They have not been tested in research studies, and they shouldn’t be quoted as findings. They are a clear, open way for two or more people to look at the same thing, and you are free to disagree with them.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “0.40 means a 40% chance we’ll make it.” No. It isn’t a probability, and it doesn’t predict anything about the relationship.
  • “Let’s change the weights until the number looks better.” Fix: choose weights before you see the result, and keep them for at least a few months.
  • “The calculator decides what we do.” It points at a shortfall. You decide what to do about it, together.

A worked example

Sam and Alex each log one ordinary week.

  • Balance: Sam logged 14 hours and Alex logged 6. That’s 70% and 30%, so balance = 1 − 40 ÷ 100 = 0.60.
  • Ownership: They list 10 regular jobs. 8 have both names. Ownership = 8 ÷ 10 = 0.80.
  • Stress: Sam’s WP-02 adds up to 11, so 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55 (moderate). Alex’s adds up to 5, so 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 (low). The average is 0.40. Neither score is 0.60 or more, so the average tells the story.
(0.60 × 0.4) + (0.80 × 0.35) + ((1 − 0.40) × 0.25)
=    0.24    +     0.28      +        0.15
=    0.67

0.67 is in the middle band: something is drifting. Now watch what small changes do:

  • If Alex takes over 3 of Sam’s hours a week, the split becomes 11 and 9 hours (55% and 45%). Balance rises to 0.90, and the result becomes 0.36 + 0.28 + 0.15 = 0.79.
  • If they also give owners to the 2 unowned jobs, ownership rises to 1.00, and the result becomes 0.36 + 0.35 + 0.15 = 0.86.

For contrast, a household where the split is 90/10 (balance 0.20), only 4 of 10 jobs are owned (0.40), and average stress is 0.70 scores 0.08 + 0.14 + 0.075 = about 0.30. That’s the “kind rethink” band. It doesn’t mean anyone failed. It means the setup is asking too much of someone, and it’s time to redesign it together.

Two more worked examples, from different homes

A triad. Three partners log 15, 9 and 6 hours, so balance is 0.75 (worked through in the balance section above). They list 12 regular jobs, and 7 have both names: ownership = 7 ÷ 12 = 0.58. Their three stress scores are 0.55, 0.40 and 0.25, so the average is 0.40, and nobody is at 0.60 or more.

(0.75 × 0.4) + (0.58 × 0.35) + ((1 − 0.40) × 0.25)
=    0.30    +     0.20      +        0.15
=    0.65

That’s the middle band. The shortfalls are 0.10 for balance, 0.15 for ownership and 0.10 for stress, so ownership comes first. In a home of three, “someone will do it” is especially easy to believe. If they give owners to the five unowned jobs, ownership becomes 1.00 and the result becomes 0.30 + 0.35 + 0.15 = 0.80.

Separated co-parents. Priya has the children four nights a week and Tom has them three, and they agreed a 60/40 split of the parenting jobs. They log 12 and 8 hours: exactly 60/40, so balance against the agreed split is 1.00. 9 of 12 jobs have both names: 0.75. Priya’s tank is 0.65 and Tom’s is 0.30, so the average is about 0.48.

0.79 says “holding.” But Priya is at 0.65, in the high band, and that is the headline. The number is right about the setup and silent about her week. So they move one whole job to Tom for a month (school shoes and the dentist, including remembering them), whatever the score says. See Co-parenting.

Reading your result: start with the shortfall

Each part can earn up to its weight: 0.40 for balance, 0.35 for ownership, and 0.25 for stress. The gap between what a part earned and what it could have earned is the shortfall. The shortfall, not the total, is the part you can act on.

Start with the biggest shortfall. In the middle band that is often ownership, which is why the calculator suggests WP-03 first. For Sam and Alex it is balance, and the two are often linked: giving a job a clear owner is often how some of the hours move. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

What to try, by shortfall:

  • Balance is biggest: move one whole job, with its thinking part, from the fuller person to the other. Moving “help” rarely moves hours. Moving a job does.
  • Ownership is biggest: sit down with the job list (WP-03) and give every unowned row an owner and a backstop, even for a trial month.
  • Stress is biggest: the setup may be fine and life is heavy. Lower the load for a while: drop, delay, buy help if you can, or ask the wider family. Pick the timing of hard talks with care (Chapter III).

In different homes: housemates often find ownership is the gap (nobody owns the shared spaces). Couples after a new baby often find balance is the gap. Carers often find stress is the gap, even when the split is as fair as it can be. Adult children who have moved back home often find the gap is in the thinking work: the parent still remembers everything. See For grown-up children.

A common mix-up: fixing all three at once. Fix: one change, one month, then look again. You’ll learn what actually moved the number.

If only one of you is on board: pick the shortfall that sits in your own circle of control. If your own tank is the gap, the change can be yours alone.

Words you could use: “The biggest gap is ownership. Can we try owners for every job, just for this month, and see how it feels?”

The wider view: the overall score

The full CALC-01 also shows a second number, the overall score. It adds a fourth input, retuning frequency: of the moments this week that landed badly, how many did you retune before answering (see Chapter I)? Its weights are 0.35 for balance, 0.30 for ownership, 0.20 for stress and 0.15 for retuning. Stress enters as (1 − stress) here too, and the same bands apply. If Sam and Alex retuned 3 of 5 rough moments (0.60), their overall score is 0.21 + 0.24 + 0.12 + 0.09 = 0.66. If nothing landed badly this week, leave retuning blank; the calculator works the overall score out from the other three. And five moments is a small number, so watch the trend, not one week.

The two numbers answer different questions. The setup score asks, “Can this workload last as it’s set up?” The overall score asks, “Is the whole system working, including how we repair after friction?” They can disagree, and that difference is useful. A home can share the work well and still be struggling to repair after clashes.

One reading is a snapshot. A month of readings is a trend.

A split that was fair once can go stale. Hours, jobs and energy change with a new baby, a new job, an illness or a move, and a setup nobody has re-checked keeps running on the old numbers. Take a fresh reading when life changes, and every few months even if nothing seems wrong. “It has always worked” is an assumption, not a result (see Complacency). When you read the number together, let each person say what sits behind their own figures. The same hours can mean a calm week for one person and a very heavy one for the other.

A single reading tells you about one week. The trend over several weeks tells you whether anything is really changing. The progress report (REPORT-01) helps you keep the numbers week by week. The monthly look-back in Chapter V feeds back into this chapter. Fixing a gap it finds keeps the ownership score true, or raises it, because the list now names who really does each job. If the gap was a blank row, filling it in raises the score. If the names were filled in but wrong, the score won’t move; only the look-back catches that kind of gap.

Life changes that are worth a fresh reading:

  • A new baby, a new child joining the family through fostering or adoption, or a grandchild coming to live with you. See New parents and Foster and kinship families.
  • Someone coming home after time away, such as deployment, work away or a long hospital stay. The setup changed while they were gone. See Coming home.
  • The children growing up and leaving. Jobs disappear, but the old split often stays. See Empty nest.
  • Retirement, a new job, new shifts, or one of you becoming ill. See Retired together, Different hours and When one of you is ill.
  • A new person moving in: a partner, a housemate, a parent. See More than two.

Research note. Many studies of the “transition to parenthood” have found that after a first baby, couples’ split of housework often becomes more traditional than either partner expected, and that satisfaction with the relationship, on average, dips for a while. Averages hide a lot of variety, but the pattern is a good reason to take a fresh reading after any big change, rather than wait for it to feel wrong.

Words you could use: “Our split was set up for a life we don’t have any more. Can we look at it as if we were starting fresh?”

Your part, and only your part

The score describes the setup you share, but your levers are your own: your jobs, your hours and what you agree to take on. You can propose a fix; you can’t make the other person accept it. If the balance stays one-sided, step your load back toward your share on purpose, and say so kindly. Matching effort with effort protects your stress tank, and your tank is part of the score too.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When only one of you is on board

Often one person is keen and the other is wary. That’s understandable: numbers can feel like a trap. You don’t need the other person’s agreement to start on your own side.

  1. Log only your own week. Never log theirs.
  2. Share one finding, as information, not as a charge: “I didn’t realize I spend four hours a week on lifts.”
  3. Make one specific offer: hand over one whole job, or ask for one. Specific requests are easier to say yes to.
  4. Adjust your side if nothing changes. Step back toward your share on purpose, and say so kindly.

This works for friends too, where one person always does the planning. See Friends. And it works across generations, where a grown-up child does all the arranging for a parent. See Grandparents.

Words you could use: “You don’t have to fill anything in. I just wanted to tell you what I noticed about my own week.”

If raising the split leads to threats, punishment, or a fear of what will happen, stop. That’s a safety question, not a setup question. Not safe at home? can help.

On your own: is your own load working?

This chapter asks whether a shared setup can last. You can ask the same about your own week: are you spending more energy than comes back in?

  • Add up what you carry: jobs, people and worries.
  • Set it beside what refills you: rest, help, and people who give back.
  • If it runs in the red, change your side: drop, delay, ask or say no kindly. A limit is a statement about supply, not about your worth.

For example: After a divorce, Marcus lives alone and has his kids on alternate weeks. On kid weeks he is the owner of everything. On the other weeks he says yes to every favor at work. Adding it up showed him he never had a week that refilled him. He kept one evening a week free on purpose. See On my own.

Words you could use: “I can’t take that on this week. Ask me again next month.”

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, one person guesses both sets of numbers, gets a low score, and says, “See? The number proves it.” The other person argues with the number instead of talking about the week. Now the tool has become a weapon.

When it goes right, each person brings their own log and their own stress score. They work out the number together, look at the biggest shortfall, and choose one small change. A month later, they check again and see whether it moved.

Going right, in a different home: Two grown-up brothers share their father’s care after years of not speaking much. They each log a month of calls, visits and paperwork, and swap logs by email rather than in person. Neither number is used as proof. They each pick one job to own, and agree to swap logs again in three months. See Adult siblings and family rifts.

Questions people often ask

Our score is 0.69. Should we worry?

No. The bands are rough guides, not cliffs. 0.69 and 0.71 describe almost the same week. Watch the trend and the shortfalls, not the exact border.

What if our hours are even but it still feels unfair?

That’s important information. Hours don’t capture everything, such as who does the planning or who covers the gaps. Try adding the planning and noticing to your logs, in minutes. Chapter IV is about the different kinds of fair.

Does our score mean we’re a good or bad match?

No. It says nothing about compatibility. It only describes how the work is shared right now.

Can I ask someone to walk me through it?

Yes. Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can explain any part of the calculation.

We’re three or more. Does this still work?

Yes. Each person logs their own hours and scores their own tank. The Lemonade Stand and the full calculator handle any number of people. See More than two partners at home.

We don’t live together. Can we still use it?

Yes, for any shared job: co-parents across two homes, siblings caring for a parent, a long-distance couple sharing the planning, coworkers sharing a role. Count the shared jobs, not the house.

My partner says logging is controlling.

Logging your own week is not control. Logging someone else’s, checking it, or demanding their numbers would be. Say that clearly, offer to log only your own, and let them choose. If the subject can’t be raised at all without fear, see Not safe at home?

Can a counselor or group leader use this with people?

Yes, as a reflection exercise, with the same rules: each person fills in only their own, and anyone can stop. See For counselors and group leaders.

We have different faiths or cultures, and different ideas of who does what.

Then the agreed split matters even more. Talk about what each of you grew up with before you set a target. See Two faiths, one family and Chapter IV.

What this number is not

It is not a relationship-compatibility score. It is not a fairness verdict about who’s the better person. It never uses anyone’s tone of voice, private thoughts or way of talking as an input. It only uses the three worksheets above, which each person fills in for themselves and both then read together.

If the number is ever used to end a conversation (“well, the number says…”) instead of starting one, it’s being misused. A low score doesn’t assign blame, and a high score doesn’t silence anyone. The number’s only job is to give two people a shared, low-drama place to start the month’s review together, which Chapter V describes at the monthly scale.

Aristotle’s Three Kinds of Friendship and Buber’s I and Thou explain why the number must never stand in for the person. Aristotle noticed that arguments about fairness come up most in friendships built on being useful to each other. Buber said we all need to treat some things as an “It,” but a life lived only that way is missing something. This number is an It. Use it, then put it down and turn back to the person.

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

They ask, perfectly pleasantly, whether you remembered to call the dentist. Your reaction is much bigger than the question deserved, and you can hear it happening while it happens.

Both tools in this chapter are self-report tools. That means each person fills them in about themselves. Neither one measures anything automatically, and neither should ever be filled in by one person about the other. That would turn a reflection tool into a way of watching someone, which is exactly what this book is trying to avoid.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar III, Read your state first, with help from Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. (Borrowed from neurobiology and psychology.)

  • In you: Know how full your stress tank is, and which angle you are seeing from, before you judge a moment.
  • Between you and others: Choose the timing, pause and come back. Your state shapes how the other person’s words land, so say it out loud, and ask which angle they are seeing from.

Leftover stress (how full your tank is)

Part of your nervous system runs things without you deciding: heart rate, breathing (though you can also slow it on purpose), and how alert or braced your body feels. Stress from the day collects there, like water in a sponge that can’t take any more.

So the first question in this chapter is simply: how full is your stress tank already, before this conversation even starts? When it’s nearly full, a small drop makes it overflow. That’s why a pleasant question about the dentist can get a reaction much bigger than the question deserved.

A short version lives on the site as the Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check. The fuller version is How much are you carrying? (WP-02), which measures how full your stress tank is. A higher score means more stress in the tank.

Leftover stress and this-moment stress

Any strong reaction is made of two parts:

  • This-moment stress: the part caused by the thing in front of you right now.
  • Leftover stress: the part you carried in from somewhere else, such as a poor night’s sleep, a hard day at work, a worry about money, or an argument that never got settled.

From the outside, the two look the same. From the inside, they need very different things. “I’m upset about the dishes” is a problem about dishes. “I’m exhausted, and the dishes are what I noticed” is a problem about exhaustion. If you treat the second one as the first, you can sort out the dishes perfectly and still feel just as bad.

The Buddha’s Second Arrow fits here. The first arrow is what happened. The second is the extra hurt we add to it: the story we tell ourselves, replayed. A full tank makes that second arrow easier to add, which is one more reason to notice what you brought in with you.

Research note. Researchers call stress that travels from one part of life into another “spillover.” Studies of couples by psychologists Lisa Neff and Benjamin Karney found that when people were under more stress outside the relationship (work, money, health), they tended to react more strongly to everyday problems inside it. The everyday lesson matches this chapter: some of what you feel at home was carried in from outside.

In different homes:

  • A night-shift worker and a day worker: Leon gets in at 7am after a night shift, just as his husband is leaving for work. Every small question at that hour lands on a full tank. They agree that 7am is for “hello” and “sleep well,” and plans wait for the evening. See Different hours.
  • A dad and a 16-year-old: She comes home and snaps at his “How was your day?” Much of it is a hard day with friends, not him. A snack and twenty minutes before any questions changes the evening. See Teens.
  • Coworkers: A manager carries a tense budget meeting into a one-to-one, and a small question about a deadline gets a sharp answer. Saying “I’ve just come out of a hard meeting” first would have changed how it landed. See At work.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “If it’s leftover, it isn’t real.” It’s real. It just needs a different fix: rest, food, a walk, or help with the outside problem.
  • “My leftover stress means you have to tiptoe around me.” No. It means you name it, and you own the repair.
  • “It’s all leftover, so the dishes don’t matter.” Once you’ve rested, check again. Often a smaller version of the issue is still there, and it deserves a calm talk too.

Words you could use: “Some of this is about today, and some of it I carried in. Give me a minute to work out which.”

The three everyday states

The Nervous System Ladder describes three states that most people will recognize:

StateWhat it can feel likeWhat it’s good for
Calm and connectedEasy breathing. You can hear something hard without getting defensive.Hard conversations, planning, making agreements
Revved upQuick heartbeat, fast thoughts. Small things feel urgent or personal.Getting things done. Not a good time to settle a disagreement.
Running on emptyFlat, foggy or far away. Words are hard to find.Rest. Big decisions can wait.

These labels come from polyvagal theory (ventral vagal, sympathetic and dorsal vagal), whose main claims are still debated by scientists. Here they are only everyday words, not a medical model. You never need the terms. “Running on empty” just means everyday tiredness. Any self-check is a best guess, not a reading. Today’s Weather, a one-minute self-check, offers the same check in weather words: “clear” (calm), “gusty” (revved up) and “fogged in” (running on empty).

The reasoning. When your body is revved up, it is getting ready to deal with a threat, not to listen. Hard conversations need listening. So the state you are in shapes how the talk will go before anyone says a word. That’s why this book asks you to read your state first.

Research note. Psychologist John Gottman, who studied couples in conflict for decades, described “flooding”: some people’s heart rate and stress rise so far during an argument that they can no longer take in what is being said. His advice was a real break of at least about twenty minutes, spent doing something calming rather than rehearsing the argument. This is a separate idea from polyvagal theory, and it fits the “revved up” row above.

In different homes:

  • A kinship carer and a 9-year-old: After a contact visit with his mum, a boy is either wound up or very flat. His grandmother saves homework and questions for later, and starts with a quiet routine. See Foster and kinship families.
  • A parent and a child with ADHD: A meltdown is the revved-up row in full. Teaching and consequences go better later, once the child is calm. See When a child has ADHD.
  • A grown-up daughter and her father: He calls at the end of her long day with advice about the baby. Running on empty, she hears criticism. She asks to call back on Saturday morning. See For grown-up children.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “I can tell which state you’re in.” You can notice signs. Only they can say. Ask, don’t tell.
  • “Calm means not upset.” You can be sad or angry and still calm enough to talk. Calm is about being able to listen.
  • “Running on empty means lazy.” It’s a low-power mode, not a choice. Rest is the fix, not pressure.

Words you could use: “I think I’m revved up. I want to hear you, and I can’t yet. Can I have twenty minutes?”

The stress-tank score, step by step

In How much are you carrying? (WP-02), you score five things from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very true), thinking about the last day or two:

  1. Sleep debt: I’m less rested than usual.
  2. Workload elsewhere: work, study or caring for someone.
  3. Unresolved conflict: from any part of life.
  4. Physical state: hungry, unwell or in pain.
  5. Time pressure: today in particular.

Add the five scores and divide by 20. That gives a number from 0 to 1:

  • Under 0.30, low load: Whatever comes up is probably about the thing itself.
  • 0.30 up to 0.60, moderate: Say it out loud before a hard conversation: “Heads up, I’m carrying more than usual today.”
  • 0.60 or more, high: Be gentle with yourself, and put off anything that doesn’t need deciding in the next hour.

Example. Sam slept badly (3), had a heavy week at work (3), has a small unsettled disagreement with a friend (1), feels fine physically (0), and has a lot on today (4). That’s 11. 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55, the moderate band. Alex slept okay (1), has an ordinary work week (2), no unsettled conflict (0), feels fine (0), and has a fairly normal day (2). That’s 5. 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25, the low band.

So before they talk about the dentist, Sam says, “Heads up, I’m carrying more than usual today.” That one sentence can change how Alex hears everything that follows.

In different homes:

  • A partner with a long-term illness: The body item may be 3 or 4 every day. A 0.55 might be a good week. Compare with your own usual, and say so: “0.55, which is good for me.” See When one of you is ill.
  • New parents: The sleep item may sit at 4 for months. Add the context, so the reading isn’t heard as a complaint about the baby or each other. See New parents.
  • Coming home after time away: After deployment or months working away, the score can be high even in happy weeks. A homecoming is a big change too. See Coming home.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “They never do it, so I’ll score them.” Never. Invite them once, and score only yourself.
  • “0.59 is fine and 0.61 is a crisis.” The bands are rough guides. Read the trend, not the border.
  • “A high score shows whose fault it is.” It shows how full a tank is. It says nothing about blame.

If only one of you is on board: Score yourself and share only the band. You don’t need the other person to join in for this to change a conversation.

Research note. This site’s idea: the five items and the three bands were chosen for this book. They are not a tested questionnaire, like the Perceived Stress Scale that researchers use. Sleep is the first item for a reason: sleep studies have found that after a short night, people tend to react more strongly to upsetting events and find it harder to read other people’s feelings.

Words you could use: “I scored 0.55 today, mostly sleep. Just so you know, it’s not about you.”

How it feeds the Chapter II check

The average of both people’s stress-tank scores is the stress input in Chapter II’s setup score. In the example above, the average is (0.55 + 0.25) ÷ 2 = 0.40. It counts against the result, as 1 − 0.40 = 0.60, which is then weighted at 25%. A way of sharing the work that only holds up when everyone is rested isn’t one that can last.

When “full” has become normal

A tank that stays full for months can start to feel like how things are. “I’m fine” may then mean “I stopped noticing.” Minds get used to whatever stays the same, including a heavy load, so a reading each week, rather than only on a bad day, can show a baseline you have adapted to. It is a quiet form of complacency with yourself. When you share a reading, add a line of context (“this has been building since March”) so the other person can tell a bad day from a long stretch.

Research note. Psychologists call getting used to a steady situation “adaptation.” It helps people cope, but it also means a heavy load can stop feeling heavy, even while it wears you down. That is why this book suggests a reading each week, not only on bad days.

In different homes:

  • A long-term carer: After three years of caring for his mother, a man says “I’m fine” and means it. His weekly readings sit around 0.60 for months. See Caregivers.
  • An empty nest: When the children leave, a couple find the tension they blamed on the busy house is still there, or that the quiet itself feels heavy. See Empty nest.
  • A family rift: For someone who no longer sees a parent or sibling, the holidays fill the tank every year, as reliably as the calendar. Knowing that helps you plan. See Adult siblings and family rifts.

Words you could use: “This isn’t a bad day. It’s been building since spring, and I’ve only just noticed.”

What a high reading asks of you

A high reading isn’t an excuse, and it isn’t a diagnosis. It’s a note to both people: whatever gets decided in the next ten minutes should probably be looked at again later, when the tank is less full.

It means “later,” never “never.” The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) helps you step away well. Say how you feel, how long you need and when you’ll be back. Use your calming steps. Then check your stress tank again. The kit’s guide is simple: under 0.50, go back at the time you said. 0.50 or more, do another round. Still 0.50 or more after two rounds? That’s okay. Pick a new time, and say when. (The kit’s 0.50 sits a little below the high band on purpose, so you go back with some room to spare.)

Why naming a time matters. The person left behind needs to know it’s a pause, not a door closing. Without a time, a pause can feel like rejection, and the one who wants to talk now tends to follow, which fills both tanks. When one of you chases and one withdraws covers that pattern.

In different homes:

  • Separated co-parents: They agree no hard talks at the handover, in front of the children. Anything tricky waits for a set call on Wednesday evening. See Co-parenting.
  • Business partners: A heated disagreement about hiring. “Let’s sleep on it and decide at ten tomorrow” saves the decision from two full tanks.
  • Friends: A text exchange turns sharp. “I want to answer this properly. Can I call you Saturday?” beats a reply typed while revved up. See Friends.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “A pause is the same as the silent treatment.” A pause has a reason, a time and a return. The silent treatment has none of them.
  • “When we come back, we pick up exactly where we left off.” Start instead with what each of you understood, and what you want now.
  • “It’s always the same one of us who needs the pause.” Either person can ask. If one always does, that is worth a calm talk on its own.

If only one of you is on board: You can still pause and come back on time. If the other person follows you, say the time again, calmly, and step outside if you need to.

A note on safety: If you are afraid of what will happen when you come back, or you aren’t allowed to pause (you are blocked from leaving, followed, or punished for it), this isn’t about timing. Leaving to stay safe never needs a time to return. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Words you could use: “I want to get this right, and I can’t right now. Can we talk at eight? I’ll come and find you.”

Seven angles on the same moment

The second idea in this chapter is the angle you might be looking at a moment from. Many arguments happen because two people are each looking at the same event from a different angle, and each assumes the other sees what they see.

Here are the seven, each with a question to ask yourself. The Perspective Shifter, a free page built on these seven angles, helps you picture how the same moment might look from the other side, with questions to ask instead of guesses. To learn your own usual angle, and someone else’s, Wavelength gives each of you a four-letter Wave Code you can compare.

  1. Physical tiredness. How rested am I, really, right now?
  2. Time pressure. Whose week is genuinely fuller? Am I seeing this through a lack of time?
  3. History. Has this exact friction come up before? Am I reacting to today, or to the last five times?
  4. Family script. What did “normal” look like in the home I grew up in? Who did this job there?
  5. Sense of fairness. Does the split feel fair to me, whatever the hours say?
  6. Communication style. Do I say things directly, or do I hint and expect the other person to pick it up? Some people say exactly what they mean. Others lean on tone and context. Neither is wrong, but they often miss each other.
  7. Outside stress. Is money, work, health or anything outside this relationship shaping how I see this?

Research note. Psychologists Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described “naive realism”: most of us feel we see the world as it really is, so people who disagree must be missing something. Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett (1971) described how we tend to explain our own actions by the situation (“I was tired”) and other people’s by their character (“they’re careless”). Naming your angle works gently against both habits.

The angles in different homes:

  • Family script, two faiths: One partner grew up with a big religious festival at home; the other didn’t. The same week is a joy to one and a pressure to the other. See Two faiths, one family.
  • Fairness and outside stress, money: One person sees a purchase from the angle of security, the other from fairness. See Money together.
  • Time pressure, gaming: An evening of gaming is rest to one person and absence to the other. See Gaming, phones and time together.
  • Family script, coming out: A parent’s first reaction often comes from the home they grew up in, not from who you are. See Coming out.

Mix-ups, and how to fix them:

  • “My angle is the right one.” An angle is a place to stand, not a verdict.
  • “You’re just seeing it from your family script.” Naming someone else’s angle for them often sounds like a put-down. Fix: name your own, and ask about theirs.

Words you could use: “I think I’m seeing this from the history angle. It’s come up before. What angle are you seeing it from?”

Two true views of the same thing

In the old story of the Blind Men and the Elephant, each man touches one part of an elephant and describes it honestly. One says it’s like a pillar, another like a rope. They argue, because each thinks his part is the whole animal. In one old telling, a king who can see watches the whole elephant. Each man is right about his part and wrong about the whole, and there is still only one elephant. Putting your two views side by side is how you get closer to the king’s view.

The pile of mail in the Monday scene is the same. One person is looking from the angle of time pressure: “This is a to-do list, and I don’t have time.” The other is looking from the angle of history and fairness: “I’m always the one who opens it.” Both are right about what they see. Naming the angles turns an argument about who is right into a talk about two real concerns.

Where “both true” stops. Both views can be true when they are two honest views of the same thing. It doesn’t apply to facts you can check (the bill was paid or it wasn’t). And it never means that harm is just a point of view. If one person is hurting or frightening another, that isn’t two angles. Not safe at home? can help.

In different homes:

  • Adult siblings after a will: One sees the money from the angle of fairness. The other sees it as recognition for years of care. Both are describing something real. See Adult siblings and family rifts.
  • A grandmother and her son, about the new baby: She sees helping. He sees his choices as a parent being second-guessed. See Grandparents and For grown-up children.
  • Two friends: “You never call” comes from hurt. “I’m drowning with a newborn” comes from exhaustion. Neither cancels the other. See Friends.

A common mix-up: “If both views are true, we can never decide.” You still decide. You just decide with both concerns on the table.

Words you could use: “You’re right about the part you can see. Here’s the part I can see. What would cover both?”

Using both together

The stress-tank check asks, how full is each person’s stress tank? The seven angles ask, what does each person think this is really about?

Used before a conversation, not during one, they replace two people guessing at each other’s inner state with two people simply saying it. A two-minute opening might sound like this:

“Before we start: my stress tank’s at about 0.55 today, so I’m carrying more than usual, and I think I’m mostly seeing this from the time-pressure angle.”

“Thanks. I’m at about 0.25. For me it’s more the history angle. This has come up a few times.”

Now both people know that one of them is stretched and the other is tired of a repeating pattern. The conversation that follows is about both of those things, not about who raised their voice.

In different homes:

  • More than two partners: A short round where each person says their tank and angle, and nobody answers until everyone has spoken. It stops two people guessing about the third. See More than two partners at home.
  • A parent and a teenager: Keep it simple: a number from 1 to 10 and one word. “Seven. School.” See Teens.
  • Coworkers: Green, yellow or red at the start of a meeting works as well as a number, and keeps private details private.
  • A long-distance couple: Start the call with tanks, because you can’t see each other’s day. See Long distance.

If only one of you is on board: Say yours anyway, and don’t ask for theirs. One person saying it out loud already replaces some of the guessing.

A worked example: the dentist, replayed

Back to Thursday at 5:15pm. Alex asks whether Sam called the dentist. Sam snaps.

Without the tools: Alex hears the snap and thinks, “All I did was ask.” Sam feels guilty and defensive. The dentist, the snap and an old argument about who remembers things all get tangled together.

With the tools: Sam notices the reaction was too big and says, “Sorry, that was more than the question deserved. I’m carrying more than usual today.” Alex says, “Okay. Is now a bad time?” Sam says, “Can we talk at eight? I’ll have eaten by then.” At eight, Sam explains the angle: time pressure. The dentist call gets a named owner and a day. It takes five minutes.

A second worked example: after a contact visit

Jen is a foster carer. Kai, who is 13, comes back from a visit with his birth family and slams his bedroom door.

Without the tools: Jen, tired after a long day, shouts up the stairs: “Not in this house, you don’t.” Kai shouts back. Both tanks overflow, and the evening is about the door.

With the tools: Jen checks her own tank first: about 0.50, a long day. She doesn’t score Kai, but she knows contact days are heavy. She knocks and says, “Rough day. I’m making toast. Come down when you’re ready.” An hour later Kai comes down and says, “I’m like a nine out of ten.” They talk about the door at the weekend, briefly, and about the visit when he’s ready. See Foster and kinship families.

Your part, and only your part

Your stress tank is the only one you can empty. You can’t calm someone else’s tank for them, and pouring yours into theirs leaves two empty tanks. Protect yours: rest, say “later,” and give energy where it’s met. When both tanks are full, the kindest thing you control is the timing.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When only one of you is on board

Everything in this chapter works from one side:

  1. Read your own tank before a hard talk.
  2. Name your own angle, and ask about theirs without guessing.
  3. Pause with a time, and come back when you said.
  4. Repair your own part when your reaction was bigger than the moment.

Often, once one person starts saying it out loud, the other picks it up without being asked.

Words you could use: “I’ve started telling you how my day was before we talk about hard things. You don’t have to do it too.”

On your own: reading your own tank

A full tank makes small things feel big. On your own, the most useful habit is a one-minute reading of your battery before you react.

  • Check your battery for a few mornings in a row and look for a pattern.
  • Before a hard moment, ask yourself how much of this is leftover from earlier.
  • Decide on a calm day what settles you, so it’s ready when you need it.

For example: Living alone after a breakup, Ana noticed she snapped at her sister on the phone every Monday. A week of morning readings showed why: Sunday nights were her worst sleep. She moved their calls to Tuesday. See On my own.

Words you could use: “Mondays are hard for me. Can we talk on Tuesday instead?”

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, one person says, “You’re obviously stressed,” as a way of dismissing what the other said. Or someone uses a high score every time a hard subject comes up, so the subject never gets talked about.

When it goes right, each person names their own tank and their own angle. High scores lead to a named time to come back, and that time is kept. Over a few weeks, you both learn which angles you tend to see from, and you start to allow for them.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t this just making excuses for bad behavior?

No. A full tank explains a reaction. It doesn’t excuse it. A kind repair, such as “That was more than the question deserved,” is still part of the job.

What if my score is high every day?

Then the load itself may need to change, which is what Chapter II and One owner per job (WP-03) help with. If you feel run down most days for weeks, or often feel numb, far away or panicky, it’s also worth talking to a doctor or counselor.

Can I guess which angle the other person is using?

You can ask, gently: “Which angle are you seeing this from?” But let them answer. A guess about someone else’s angle often turns into a judgment of them.

Where can I talk this through?

Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you work out your own tank and angle before a talk.

Does this work with children?

Yes, in simpler words. Young children can use weather words (“stormy,” “foggy,” “sunny”) or a number from 1 to 10. You score yourself out loud, which shows them how. See When a child has ADHD and Teens.

What if one of us is neurodivergent?

States and angles may show differently on the outside, and faces can be hard to read either way. That’s one more reason to say your tank out loud rather than guess. See Neurodivergent relationships.

Can a counselor use this with a group?

Yes, as long as each person scores only themselves and can pass. See For counselors and group leaders.

Limits and cautions

  • The stress-tank score is a quick gut-check, not a medical test. It can’t tell you anything about your health.
  • Fill it in about yourself only. Never score the other person, and never use a score to win a point.
  • A high reading is a reason to pause, not a reason to end a conversation for good. Always name a time to come back.
  • The seven angles are a helpful list, not a complete one. If you’re seeing something from an angle that isn’t on the list, name it anyway.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

This chapter has two halves. The first is about agreeing what “fair” means in your home. The second is about the few seconds after a comment lands, before you reply. They belong together, because many arguments about chores mix up both: a question about a job, and a reaction to how it was said.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Borrowed from business, economics and neurobiology.)

  • In you: Notice which kind of fair comes naturally to you, and notice the quiet habit of covering for others without saying so. When a comment stings, check your own state before you answer.
  • Between you and others: Give each job one clear owner instead of blaming. Watch how unowned jobs slide toward one person, and keep things steady by mentioning help out loud and saying thank you.

The two kinds of fair

Many arguments about what fairness means come dressed up as an argument about chores. Philosophers have argued about this for centuries, and two of their big ideas are useful here:

  • Fair by results. Philosophers call this consequentialist, from “consequences”: you judge by outcomes, by how things turned out. It asks: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy? Done carefully, it counts the cost to whoever covered, too.
  • Fair by promises. Philosophers call this deontological, from the Greek word for “duty.” It asks: did each person do the specific things they agreed to own, whatever kind of week it was?

Both are reasonable. The friction starts when one person is judging the week by results (“It all got done, so we’re fine”) and the other is judging it by promises (“You said you’d handle the trash, and you didn’t, again”). Neither view is wrong. But a household needs to agree which one it is using, just as a contract needs to say which state’s laws apply.

Why the two kinds clash

Each kind of fair answers a different worry. Fair by results answers “Will the home keep running?” Fair by promises answers “Can I rely on you?” Both worries are real. When one person’s worry is met and the other’s isn’t, the first feels things are fine and the second feels let down, in the same week, about the same jobs. Naming which worry you each carry turns a fight about the trash into a conversation about trust and flexibility.

Three homes, one clash

  • Two mums, two kids. Maya judges the week by results: the kids were fed, school forms went in, so it was a good week. Lin judges by promises: Maya said she’d book the dentist and didn’t. When they name it (“You’re counting the week, I’m counting the dentist”), the argument shrinks to one booking and one owner.
  • Three adult siblings and their mum. One says, “Mum’s fine, isn’t she?” (results). Another keeps a rota and feels it’s ignored (promises). The third lives far away and pays for a cleaner, in a different currency again. A short call that names all three kinds of help stops each one feeling unseen. See Family rifts when old hurts are part of it.
  • Four housemates. The group chat fills with “who left the bins?” Two of them want a rota. Two want “whoever’s around.” They agree: a rota for bins and bathroom, results for everything else. See Sharing a room for a written agreement.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “Results people are lazy, promise people are controlling.” Neither is true. Fix: talk about the kind of fair, never the kind of person. “You lean results, I lean promises” is a description, not a charge.
  • “We have to pick one kind forever.” Most homes use both. Fix: decide by type of job, as in Choosing your kind of fair below.
  • “Fair means fifty-fifty.” Equal hours are one way to be fair, not the only one. Fix: ask whether the split feels fair to both of you, given work hours, health and season.

If only one of you is on board

You can still name your own ruler: “I’ve realised I count promises. That’s why a missed job gets to me, even when it all works out.” This often lowers the heat on its own, because the other person now knows what the upset is about. You can’t make them choose a kind of fair. You can choose to say yours once, kindly, and see what comes back.

Honest research notes

  • Research: Researchers who study fairness describe several rules people use: equality (the same for each), equity (in proportion to what each puts in) and need (according to what each requires). Disagreements often come from using different rules without saying so. More in the Library.
  • Research: Studies of couples and housework keep finding that whether both people feel the split is fair matters more for satisfaction than an exact fifty-fifty. More in the Library.
  • Research: When partners each estimate their share of household work, their estimates often add up to more than 100 percent. We remember our own effort more easily than other people’s (Ross and Sicoly, 1979). More in the Library.
  • This site’s idea: Calling the two kinds “results” and “promises,” after two old schools of philosophy, is a teaching picture. Researchers don’t sort households this way.

Judging by results

What it’s good at: it’s flexible. In a week when one person is sick or away, the other simply picks things up, and nobody has to negotiate.

Where it goes wrong: it can hide a pattern. If the same person always steps in, the results look fine, and the stepping-in becomes invisible. Over time, that is how unbilled debt builds up.

Fair by results, in three homes

  • Separated co-parents. “Whoever is nearer picks her up when she’s sick” works well, until one parent works from home and is always nearer. Results-fair has quietly made them the default. See Co-parenting.
  • A home with three partners. “Whoever’s free cooks” sounds relaxed. After two months, the partner on the shortest hours has cooked almost every night. See More than two.
  • Coming home after time away. While one partner was deployed, the other did everything by results, because there was no one else. When the first comes home, “whoever can” means the one who already knows how. See Coming home.

The fix in each case is the same: keep the flexibility, and add a quick monthly look at who stepped in. If it’s nearly always one person, that job moves onto the owned list.

Words you could use: “I like how flexible we are. I just noticed it’s nearly always me who’s free. Can we look at that?”

If only one of you is on board: keep your own simple tally of unplanned jobs for two weeks. Facts on paper are easier to share than a feeling, and gentler than a list of complaints.

Judging by promises

What it’s good at: it’s clear. Everyone knows what they own, so nobody has to keep deciding who does what, and nobody gets left with jobs by default.

Where it goes wrong: it can feel rigid. Life doesn’t always cooperate. Holding someone to the letter of an agreement in a week when their parent was in the hospital isn’t fairness. It’s missing the point. Even thinkers who put promises first allow that some promises give way.

Fair by promises, in three homes

  • Business partners. One owns invoices, one owns suppliers. When invoices run late, nobody argues about who works harder. They ask the invoice owner what got in the way.
  • A teen and a parent. A 15-year-old owns his laundry. One week he has exams. Fair by promises doesn’t mean “tough luck.” It means he asks ahead: “Can you do my wash this week? I’ll do the dishes all weekend.”
  • A couple when one is ill. A partner with a long-term illness owns the online shopping and bills, which can be done from bed. On a bad day, the agreement already says who covers. See When one of you is ill.

Common mix-up: “A promise is a promise, whatever happens.” Fix: build the exceptions in. Illness, grief, exams and work crunches are reasons to swap or pause a job, said ahead of time where you can.

Words you could use: “I can’t keep this one this week. Can we swap, and I’ll take it back on Monday?”

A note on safety: Fair by promises is about promises both people chose, and either can ask to change. A list of rules one person sets for the other, with checking, punishment, or money and freedom held back, is not a promise. It is control. If that sounds like your home, it isn’t your fault, and the tools in this book are not the right place to start. See Not safe at home? and normal limits and control.

The agreement: look to the owner

This book calls the agreement being fair by the rules, and it’s simple: once a job has a named owner in the job list (One owner per job, WP-03), you judge it by whether the owner did their part. You don’t judge it by whether things happened to work out because someone else quietly covered.

In this book, the owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The job list has room for a second name: a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. They can be the same person, though a second name gives the job a gentle check. Older kids can own age-sized jobs too, like feeding the cat or packing their own lunch, with a parent as the second name.

This protects the person who keeps filling the gaps. Without it, their help gets used as proof that the gap doesn’t matter: “Well, it got done, didn’t it?”

It doesn’t mean the owner has to do everything personally. Swapping, asking for help or saying “not this week” in advance all count as looking after your job. The Who-did-what log (WP-01) also has kind scripts for saying “not right now”: you’re already carrying a lot, someone else is better placed, or you’ll do it later at a named time. What this rules out is the job silently falling to someone else.

Why one name works better than “we”

A job that belongs to everyone tends to belong to no one. Each person assumes someone else will notice, and the one who notices first ends up doing it, every time. A single name ends the guessing. It also changes the conversation when a job slips: instead of “Why does nobody ever…?”, you can ask one person one question, kindly.

One owner, in three homes

  • Foster carers. Two carers and a social worker can all assume someone else phoned the school. The carers agree one of them owns school contact and the other is the second name. Some decisions belong to the agency or the care plan, and that goes on the list too. See Foster, adoptive and kinship families.
  • A parent and a child with ADHD. Owning “clean your room” is too big. Owning “put the dirty clothes in the basket before bed,” with a picture on the door, is something a child can really own. See When a child has ADHD.
  • Coworkers with a shared inbox. Customer emails sat for days because four people could answer them. One owner per weekday fixed it in a week.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “The owner has to do it alone.” Owning a job means making sure it happens. Asking for help or swapping counts.
  • “The second name is the supervisor.” The second name notices and asks once. They don’t inspect, redo or comment on how.
  • “My way is the right way.” If the old owner redoes the new owner’s work, the new owner soon stops trying. Researchers call this gatekeeping. Fix: agree what “done” means, then let the owner do it their way. More on gatekeeping.

If only one of you is on board

Own your own jobs clearly and out loud: “I own the bins and the car. Which ones would you like?” Offering a choice is easier to say yes to than being handed a list. If nothing comes back, keep owning yours, and stop silently picking up the rest. That is where your part ends.

Honest research notes

  • Research: When responsibility is shared among several people, each feels less responsible. John Darley and Bibb Latané called this diffusion of responsibility. More in the Library.
  • Research: Studies of workplaces link unclear roles (role ambiguity) with stress and conflict. Whether it works the same at home is less studied, but it seems likely. More in the Library.
  • This site’s idea: The owner and second name are a home-sized version of the charts project managers use to show who does a task and who is kept in the loop. More in the Library.

Choosing your kind of fair

Most homes end up with a mix, and that’s fine, as long as it’s agreed. One simple way:

  • Owned jobs (on the WP-03 list with names) are judged by promises.
  • Shared or one-off jobs (a flat tire, a sick child, a surprise visitor) are judged by results. Whoever can, does.
  • If a shared job keeps coming up, it moves onto the owned list at the next weekly catch-up.

Agree on this on a calm, easy day, not in the middle of a disagreement. Write it at the top of your WP-03 sheet so you can both see it.

A third disagreement: what counts. Sometimes the argument isn’t results against promises at all. One of you counts doing, and the other counts noticing and planning. Go back to the Preface: put the planning and noticing on the list too, in minutes, so both currencies are counted.

Fair also needs re-agreeing. What counted as fair last year (before a new job, a baby or an illness) may not be fair now, and an old agreement nobody has revisited slowly stops being an agreement and becomes a habit. Put a review date on it. At the review, each person says what has changed for them, then you decide together. See Complacency.

Agreeing, in three homes

  • Two faiths, one family. Two festival seasons, two sets of relatives. They agree each person owns the preparations for their own tradition, and the other is the second name and a willing helper. See Two faiths.
  • Different hours. One partner works nights. They agree that daytime sleep counts as work, so day jobs aren’t judged by who is “home.” See Different hours.
  • Long distance. A couple in two cities share planning, not chores. They agree that the person who suggests a visit owns booking it. See Long distance.

Common mix-up: “Once we’ve agreed, it’s settled.” Fix: call it a trial. A month is long enough to learn something and short enough that nobody feels trapped.

Words you could use: “Can we try this for a month and then look again? Nothing has to be forever.”

If only one of you is on board: suggest one small trial, about one job, not a whole system. A small yes is still a yes.

  • Research: People accept decisions more readily when the process felt fair: they had a say, the same rules applied to everyone, and they could see how the decision was made. This is often called procedural justice. More in the Library.
  • Research: Trying a small change, looking at what happened and adjusting comes from quality improvement (the plan–do–study–act cycle linked with W. Edwards Deming). More in the Library.

Covering without resentment

Stepping in for someone is kind. It only turns sour when it’s silent. Three habits keep it kind:

  1. Say it simply, without a sting. “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Not a complaint, just a note.
  2. Log it. In your WP-01 week, write the job under your name, with a note that you covered it.
  3. Let the cover be thanked, and asked about. Covering that nobody mentions gradually becomes expected, which is how complacency starts. Thank the person who covered, and let them say what it has been like for them. Their context is part of the picture.
  4. Watch for repeats. Covering once is kindness. Covering every week is a sign the job needs a new owner or a conversation about capacity. The monthly look-back in Chapter V is built to catch this.

Why silent covering goes sour

Silent covering sends two wrong messages at once. To the person covered for, it says “there was no gap.” To the person covering, it says “nobody noticed.” The first person carries on as before, and the second starts to keep a quiet list. Saying it out loud fixes both messages in one sentence.

Covering, in three relationships

  • Friends. One friend always hosts and plans. Nobody asked, so nobody thinks to thank. One honest line (“I love hosting, and I’d love someone else to pick the place next time”) changes the pattern. See Friends.
  • A grown-up child and parents. One adult son quietly handles his parents’ paperwork because his sister lives far away. Saying it to her, without blame, lets her offer what she can from a distance. See Grown-up children.
  • Coworkers. One person fixes everyone’s slides before meetings. Once they mention it at a team check-in, the team adds a slide checklist, and the fixing stops being invisible.

Common mix-up: “If I mention it, it sounds like I want a medal.” Fix: say it as information, the same day, in one sentence. A same-day note sounds like news. A list a month later sounds like a bill.

If only one of you is on board: if you mention covering and nothing changes, cover less, and say so kindly: “I’m going to leave the recycling this week, since it’s yours.” That isn’t punishment. It’s letting the job go back to its owner.

A note on safety: If you cover jobs because you’re afraid of what will happen if they aren’t done, that isn’t covering by choice. See Not safe at home? for free, private support.

  • Research: Equity theory, from J. Stacy Adams in the 1960s, suggests that feeling under-benefited tends to bring resentment, and feeling over-benefited can bring guilt. More in the Library.
  • Research: Feeling that your contributions are seen is closely linked with feeling that things are fair, and specific thanks tends to land better than general praise. More in the Library.

A worked example: the trash, three weeks running

In WP-03, Alex owns the trash. For three weeks, Alex has been working late on Tuesdays, and Sam has put the trash out each time without saying anything.

Judged by results: the trash went out every week. No problem. Except Sam is getting quietly resentful, and Alex has no idea.

Judged by the rules: the trash is Alex’s job, and Alex didn’t do it. That isn’t a judgment of Alex as a person. It’s a fact about the job. And it points to a real question: can Alex still own the trash with the new work hours?

At the weekly catch-up, Sam says, “I’ve taken out the trash the last three weeks. Is Tuesday night still working for you?” Alex says, “Honestly, no. Tuesday nights aren’t realistic with the late shifts. Could we try a swap for the rest of the month? You take the trash, and I’ll take the recycling and the weekly grocery order.” They write the trial into the WP-03 sheet and agree to settle it at the monthly look-back in Chapter V. Nobody had to be the bad guy.

Letting a comment land before you react

The idea is borrowed loosely from brain science, which describes how the brain turns down repeated or unimportant sounds and sensations, so they don’t all demand attention at once. In the brain that happens automatically, in a fraction of a second. This book’s pause is different: a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. It’s a practical habit, not a medical claim: not every comment needs your full reaction the moment it’s said.

When you’re tense or running on empty (see the Nervous System Ladder, a quick check of how calm or stirred up you feel, and Chapter III), even a neutral comment can arrive at full volume and seem to demand an instant reply. The pause gives it a moment to settle first.

A comment landing hard, in three relationships

  • Long distance. A one-word text, “fine,” arrives at midnight. It reads as cold. A pause, then “Fine good or fine tired?” saves a whole evening. See Long distance.
  • A grandparent and a grown-up child. “Is she still not sleeping through?” sounds like criticism of the parenting. A breath, and the parent hears a worried grandmother instead. See Grandparents.
  • A carer and a partner in pain. A sharp word on a bad pain day is often about the pain. The pause lets the carer answer the day, not the words. See Caregivers.

Common mix-up: “If I pause, I’m letting them get away with it.” Fix: the pause is about timing. You can still raise it later, calmly, if it still matters.

A note on safety: The pause is for ordinary stings between people who mean each other well. It is not a way to absorb put-downs, threats or insults that keep happening. If what you keep pausing over is someone frightening you, see Not safe at home?

  • Research: Under stress, people are more likely to read an unclear remark as hostile. Psychologists call this hostile attribution. More in the Library.
  • Research: Studies of email found that writers tend to overestimate how clearly their tone, such as sarcasm or humour, comes across (Kruger and colleagues, 2005). Text messages have the same problem.
  • Research: Sensory gating, where the brain filters repeated sounds, is a real and studied process. Using it here is only a picture. The pause in this chapter is a habit, not a brain treatment.

The three steps: notice, name, decide

  1. Notice that the comment landed harder than its words probably deserved. Often you feel it in your body first: a hot face, a tight chest, a sharp breath.
  2. Name it out loud, instead of reacting from the spike: “I heard that as bigger than it probably was. Give me a second.”
  3. Decide, once you’ve settled, whether it needs a reply now, later or not at all. Asking yourself whether a kinder meaning could fit is what psychologists call reappraisal, and it has some good research behind it.

This isn’t hiding your feelings. The aim isn’t to shut everything out for good. It’s timing: letting the spike pass before deciding how much weight a comment really deserves. The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) helps the spike pass, and Say it so it lands (WP-09) helps with the reply once it has.

WP-09 also offers four quick questions for weighing what you heard. Was it about a specific task? Would it sound the same if you weren’t already stretched? Could a neutral meaning fit? Are you answering their words, or an old argument?

If only one of you is on board

You can use all three steps alone. Noticing and naming lower your own temperature, even if the other person never learns the words. Saying “Give me a second” still works when they don’t know the plan, as long as you come back.

Common mix-up: “Naming it means saying what they meant.” Fix: name your reaction, not their intent. “That landed hard for me” invites a reply. “You’re being nasty” invites a fight.

Words you could use: “That landed harder than you probably meant. Give me a second.” “I needed a minute. I’m back. Can I ask what you meant?”

  • Research: Putting a feeling into words, such as “I’m feeling stung,” can reduce its intensity a little. Researchers call this affect labelling (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007). More in the Library.
  • Research: Changing how you read a situation (reappraisal) is generally linked with better outcomes than hiding a feeling (suppression), in work by James Gross and others. More in the Library.
  • Research: John Gottman’s research on couples describes “flooding,” when the body is too stirred up to listen. He suggests breaks of at least twenty minutes. More in the Library.

Keep the two questions apart

Put the two halves of this chapter together, and you can separate two things that usually get tangled:

The questionWhat kind of questionWhat it’s checked against
“Was this job done?”A factThe agreed owner in WP-03
“Did that land as an attack?”A reactionYour own state right now

Both questions matter. They just need different answers, and they go better one at a time. Take the fact to the job list. Take the reaction to a quiet moment and WP-09.

Two questions, in three relationships

  • Business partners. “The invoice went out late” is a fact. “You always drop the ball” is a feeling dressed as a fact. They fix the invoice date first, then talk about the frustration on Friday.
  • Adult siblings after a loss. “Who is clearing Dad’s flat on Saturday?” is a fact. “You were never there for him” is years of feeling. Mixing them guarantees a row. See Family rifts.
  • A couple coming out to family. “Who tells your parents, and when?” is a planning question. “I’m scared they’ll reject us” deserves its own gentle talk first. See Coming out as an adult.

If only one of you is on board: split them on your side. Answer the fact, then add, “And I’d like to come back to how that felt, maybe tonight.”

  • Research: The authors of Difficult Conversations (Stone, Patton and Heen, 1999) describe how a hard talk often holds a “what happened” conversation and a feelings conversation at once. What someone meant and how it landed can both be true. More in the Library.

A worked example: the flat thank-you

Wednesday, 7pm. Sam says, “Thanks for doing the dishes,” in a flat voice. Alex hears sarcasm and feels a flash of anger.

Without the pause: Alex snaps back, “You don’t have to say it like that.” Sam, who was only tired, feels attacked. Now there’s an argument about tone, and nobody remembers it started with a thank-you.

With the pause: Alex notices the flash and says, “That landed a bit oddly for me. Give me a second.” After a breath: “Did you mean that as a thank-you?” Sam says, “Yes, sorry, I’m just exhausted.” It can be over in seconds.

Your part, and only your part

You control whether, when and how you bring something up, and whether you cover a job out loud. How the other person responds is theirs. If you cover again and again with no thanks and no change, match the energy: cover less, and say so kindly. Fair by promises only works when both people keep theirs.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, each person keeps using their own kind of fair without saying so. The one who judges by results thinks everything’s fine. The one who judges by promises feels let down over and over. Every comment about a chore gets answered at full volume.

When it goes right, you both know which jobs are owned and which are shared. Covering gets mentioned and logged. When a comment stings, one of you says, “Give me a second,” the other waits, and the one who paused comes back to it.

Questions people often ask

What if we can’t agree on which kind of fair to use?

Use the mix above: owned jobs by promises, shared jobs by results. Try it for a month, then look again together.

Isn’t it petty to hold someone to a small job?

Not if you both agreed to it. What feels petty is usually the build-up of unspoken covering. Saying it early and kindly is the opposite of petty.

What if “give me a second” turns into an hour?

That’s fine, as long as you say so and name a time to come back. If a second isn’t enough, take a proper break; twenty minutes or more often works better than a quick one. Pausing is for timing, not for avoiding the talk.

Limits and cautions

  • Parity is a tool for jobs, not for judging people. “You didn’t do your job” is a fact about a task. It never means “you’re a bad partner, parent or roommate.”
  • Be flexible when life is hard. Illness, grief and big changes are exactly when agreements should be looked at again, not enforced.
  • Gating is not suppression. If something that was said still hurts after you’ve settled, it deserves a calm conversation.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. The third Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth about Tuesday. Or you could notice that Tuesday has now happened three times, and ask what it is about Tuesdays.

Like the tools in earlier chapters, the look-back in this chapter checks a shared setup over time. It never judges either person’s character, and it never settles “who owes what” for hours already passed.

The idea is borrowed from accounting, where a shortfall that keeps coming back gets a careful look at month-end. Here, the shortfall is a job that keeps falling short, and the look-back is a careful, friendly look at your month’s notes, more like a self-check than an inspection.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load. (Borrowed from systems thinking, behavioral economics and ledger accounting.)

  • In you: Look at your own routines as a setup you can adjust. Spot the easy defaults, like “I’ll just do it,” that let a gap quietly reopen.
  • Between you and others: Look at the month together, give repeating gaps a clear owner, and keep count so jobs don’t drift back to whoever notices first.

Why a monthly look, on top of the weekly ones

By this point in the book, two rhythms are already running. There’s the 90-second daily check-in (WP-13). And there’s the weekly catch-up, a short end-of-week talk where you tally the week in the Lemonade Stand (a free page where each person lists their hours) and take a quick look at your job list (WP-03, One owner per job).

Both are useful because they are small and frequent. But small, frequent checks have a blind spot. A job that slips for one week looks like bad luck. The same job slipping week after week is a pattern, and patterns are easy to miss week by week. They show up best when you step back and look at a whole month.

The monthly look-back is that step back. Instead of asking “How did this week go?”, it asks: which gaps kept reopening, however many times we closed them?

Why a month, and why notes beat memory

A month is long enough for a repeat to show up three or four times, and short enough that you still remember what was going on. It also matches how many households already run: rent, bills and school letters come monthly.

Notes matter because memory is a poor counter. The last argument feels like it happens “all the time.” The quiet weeks are forgotten. Four short weekly notes give you something steadier than either person’s memory, and they take the “I’m sure it was more than that” out of the conversation.

A look-back in three settings

  • Housemates. Five people in a shared house hold a twenty-minute house meeting on the first Sunday of the month. Each brings one thing that kept slipping. See Sharing a room.
  • A small team at work. Many teams already do this and call it a retrospective: what went well, what kept going wrong, what to try next. It’s the same habit with different words.
  • A family with school-age kids. A short family meeting with snacks. Each person names one good thing and one thing that kept getting forgotten. The kids often spot the gaps first.

If only one of you is on board

You can do a look-back on your own notes: which of your jobs slipped, and which jobs you kept picking up that aren’t yours. Then invite the other person once, with the result, not the method: “I noticed the recycling has needed doing every week and I’ve done it each time. Could we give it an owner?” A finding is easier to say yes to than a new meeting.

Honest research notes

  • Research: People judge how often something happens by how easily examples come to mind, so recent and vivid moments feel more common than they are. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman called this the availability heuristic. More in the Library.
  • Research: Looking back together at a stretch of work, asking what went well and what to try next, is a common team practice called a retrospective. More in the Library.
  • This site’s idea: Using a monthly look-back at home, and the ✗ count in WP-04, is the site’s own adaptation. It hasn’t been tested as a household method.

Getting ready

  • Your four weekly notes from this month, in WP-01 (the “who did what” log). A few lines a week is all it needs.
  • Your current WP-03 job list.
  • The WP-04 sheet (“What keeps coming back?”), on paper or on screen.
  • Twenty minutes, at a time you’ve both agreed on, when neither of you is rushed, hungry or worn out.

If either of your stress tanks is high that day (0.60 or more on How much are you carrying? (WP-02), which measures how full your stress tank is), move the look-back. It can wait a day or two.

Getting ready in different setups

  • Long distance or different hours. Do it by video, or in a shared note that each person fills in, then talk for ten minutes. Pick a time that isn’t straight after a night shift. See Long distance and Different hours.
  • Separated co-parents. Keep it about the children’s jobs only, short and in writing if talking is hard. Your parenting plan or court order comes first. See Co-parenting.
  • Foster and kinship carers. Your own household notes are separate from the records your agency or social worker asks for. Some gaps, like contact visits, belong in the care plan, not your look-back. See Foster and kinship families.
  • Children and teens. Keep it short, give them a job in the meeting (reading out the list, keeping time), and start with one good thing each.

Common mix-up: “Our notes have to be detailed.” They don’t. Three lines a week is plenty. Detail makes the notes harder to keep, and the look-back only needs to know which jobs slipped.

A note on safety: Each person’s notes are their own. Nobody should demand to see the other’s notes, check their phone, or ask for an hour-by-hour account. That is monitoring, not a look-back. If it’s happening to you, see Not safe at home?

Step one: gather the month’s notes

Go through the four weekly logs. Write down every job that slipped in any week. A job slipped if it was noticed but not done, if it only got done after someone raised it more than once, or if its owner couldn’t do it and someone else quietly covered. Mark an ✗ for each week it happened.

In accounting terms, you are reconciling the plan (WP-03, who should do each job) against the log (WP-01, who did). Every difference is an item to explain.

One ✗ is normal life. Two are worth a look. Three or four in one month is a real pattern, not just bad luck.

Common mix-ups: An ✗ is not a grade, and it goes next to a job, never next to a person. “Trash: ✗ ✗ ✗” is a fact about a job. “Alex: ✗ ✗ ✗” is a scorecard, and it will start an argument. If a job slipped because someone covered it, it still gets an ✗: the point is to find the jobs where the plan and real life don’t match.

For example: Three adult siblings share their mum’s care. Their month shows “Mum’s prescriptions” with three ✗. Nobody failed: each thought the pharmacy delivery covered it, and it only did some months.

Words you could use: “Let’s mark the jobs first and talk about why afterwards.”

What a gap looks like at this scale

A single missed job is an event. A real gap is a job that keeps producing the same event, month after month, without ever landing on a steady owner. It’s the difference between “the trash didn’t go out on Tuesday” and “the trash has quietly become nobody’s job for three months.” The first is ordinary noise. The second is a gap in the job list that the weekly check-ins keep patching instead of fixing.

This matters because gaps like these are where the unbilled debt from the Preface grows fastest. It doesn’t come from any single missed job. It comes from the same job being argued over from scratch every time it comes back, because it never got a name that stuck.

Why a repeat points to the setup

If the same job keeps slipping whoever is meant to do it, the cause is usually in the arrangement: no clear owner, an owner with no time at that moment of the week, or a job that only one person ever notices. Asking “Who keeps failing?” finds someone to blame. Asking “What about this setup keeps failing?” finds something you can change.

  • A grandparent raising grandchildren. School forms kept getting missed. The forms went into one child’s bag and the grandad never saw the bag. The fix was a tray by the door, not trying harder. See kinship care.
  • A couple when one comes home. After months away, the returning partner wants to help with “everything,” so nothing gets a new owner and old jobs keep slipping. See the handover week.
  • A parent and a child with ADHD. “Remember your PE kit” slipped every week. A checklist on the door, owned by the child and backed up by the parent, closed the gap. See When a child has ADHD.
  • Research: When a job is shared among several people, each tends to feel less responsible for it (diffusion of responsibility, from work by John Darley and Bibb Latané). More in the Library.
  • Research: Quality-improvement thinkers such as W. Edwards Deming argued that most repeated problems come from the system people work in, not from the people. More in the Library.

Step two: sort each repeat

For every job with two or more ✗, decide which of three kinds of gap it is. (As in Chapter II, the owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The second name on the list is only a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in.)

Kind of gapWhat it meansWhat to do
Structural gapThere’s no clear owner, or the named owner isn’t the person actually doing it.Update the WP-03 job list in this session, and agree on the change together.
Capacity issueThere’s a named owner, but they’ve been unable to keep up with it.Set a time for an honest, kind conversation about handing it over or changing it. Don’t just quietly promise to try harder.
One-offIt slipped more than once, but for a clear short-term reason, such as illness, travel or a busy month at work.Note it and move on. Don’t make a lasting change because of a short-term cause.

For a capacity conversation, two workpapers help: WP-01, which besides the log has kind scripts for saying “not right now,” and the fact-feeling-ask shape in WP-09, Say it so it lands.

The three kinds, in different homes

  • Structural gap, housemates. The hallway light bulb has been out for weeks. Nobody owns “things that break.” They give it an owner.
  • Capacity issue, a carer. A woman caring for her husband after a stroke owns the bills and the shopping, and both keep slipping. She isn’t careless. She’s out of hours. The talk is about who else can help, not about her trying harder. See When one of you is ill and Caregivers.
  • One-off, a family in grief. In the month after a funeral, half the list slipped. That is a one-off, and the kindest look-back that month is very short.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Calling a capacity issue a structural gap, to avoid a hard talk. Moving the job to someone else without asking how the first person is doing can miss the real problem. Fix: ask, “Is it the job, or is it everything?”
  • Calling the same gap a one-off every month. “It was just a busy month,” three months running, isn’t a one-off anymore. Fix: if a one-off comes back the next month, sort it again.
  • Deciding the kind for someone else. Only the owner can say whether they’re out of capacity. Fix: ask, don’t diagnose.

If only one of you is on board

Sort your own jobs honestly. If one of yours is a capacity issue, say so first: “The school forms keep slipping, and I think I’m out of room this term. Could we talk about it Thursday?” Going first makes it easier for the other person to say the same about theirs, later.

A note on safety: A capacity talk needs both people to be able to say “I can’t” without fear. If saying no leads to anger, threats or punishment, that is not a capacity problem you can fix with a list. See Not safe at home?

A worked example month

Here’s what Sam and Alex’s WP-04 sheet looks like at the end of a month:

JobWk 1Wk 2Wk 3Wk 4Total ✗Kind
Trash out (Tuesday night)✗✗✗3Structural gap
School forms signed✗✗✗3Capacity issue
Dishes before bed✗✗2One-off
Plants watered✗1(normal life)

✗ = it slipped that week. A blank means the job went fine.

  • The trash is Alex’s on paper. But Alex works late on Tuesdays, so for three weeks Sam quietly took it out. At the weekly catch-up in Chapter IV, they agreed to try a swap for the rest of the month, and in week 4 Sam took it out with no trouble. The named owner wasn’t the person doing it, so it’s a structural gap. Now they make the trial swap official: Sam owns the trash, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, so the hours stay even. They update WP-03 on the spot.
  • The school forms have a clear owner, Sam, who is swamped this term. That’s a capacity issue. They set a time for a proper talk on Thursday evening instead of solving it now.
  • The dishes slipped during the two weeks Alex was traveling. That’s a one-off. They note it and change nothing.

Result: one structural gap, one capacity issue, one one-off. They write those three numbers down. The whole thing takes eighteen minutes.

A second month: three siblings and their dad

Three grown-up siblings share the care of their dad, who lives alone. One lives nearby, one an hour away, one abroad. They do their look-back on a video call, using a shared list.

  • Calling Dad’s landlord about repairs: 3 ✗. Nobody had ever agreed who would. A structural gap. The sibling abroad takes it, because it can be done by email from anywhere.
  • Weekly visit: 2 ✗. The sibling nearby owns it and has been working double shifts. A capacity issue. They book a separate call for Sunday.
  • Dad’s food order: 1 ✗, the week the website was down. Normal life.

The sibling abroad says, “I know I can’t visit. I want to own something real.” That sentence did more than the list. When old hurts make even this kind of call hard, see Family rifts.

Step three: close the month and keep count

  1. Update WP-03 for every structural gap.
  2. Set a time for the conversation for every capacity issue. The look-back finds the issue. It doesn’t replace the conversation.
  3. Write down how many structural gaps, capacity issues and one-offs you found.

A falling number of structural gaps is a good sign, over several months; one month can be a normal swing. A number that keeps rising usually means something bigger has changed, such as a new job, a new baby or a move, and that change may need its own conversation.

The look-back also feeds back into Chapter II. If a gap was a blank row, filling it in raises next month’s ownership score. If the names were filled in but wrong, like the trash, the score holds steady, but now it’s true: the list names who really does each job. Only the look-back catches that kind of gap. REPORT-01 (your progress, week by week) can keep the monthly counts, including how many gaps came back after being fixed the month before. That repeat count is the one to watch most closely.

Common mix-up: “Our goal is zero.” A count of zero can mean a solid setup, or that someone has stopped marking ✗ to keep the peace. Fix: the count is for noticing a direction over months, not for winning a month.

  • Research: Goodhart’s law, from economics, warns that when a measure becomes a target, people start managing the number instead of the thing it was measuring. More in the Library.
  • Research: An unusually bad month is often followed by a more ordinary one, simply by chance. Statisticians call this regression to the mean. It’s why one month is never enough to judge a change.

If only one of you is on board: keep your own count of the jobs you own and the jobs you covered. Over three months, it shows you whether things are settling or whether you’re carrying more and more. That is useful to know for yourself, whether or not you ever show it to anyone, and it’s good evidence for a calm conversation if you choose to have one.

Words you could use: “I’ve been keeping a simple count for three months. Can I show you what I noticed?”

The quiet months

The look-back is built to find gaps, so it is tempting to skip it when there aren’t any. That’s the month it matters in a different way. Where audits are done well, a control that has always worked is still tested again on a schedule, because working so far is not the same as working now. People are no different. A month with no ✗ can mean the setup is solid. It can also mean nobody has looked closely, or that one person is quietly covering so well that the page looks clean.

On a quiet month, keep the same twenty minutes and add three questions:

  1. What’s going well that we haven’t said out loud?
  2. Is anything small that we’ve stopped mentioning because it didn’t seem worth it?
  3. Does the job list still match life as it is now?

The first question gives thanks a place. The second catches what slow drift hides. The third is the review date for the whole setup. This is the same re-check that the Complacency guide sets out for every kind of relationship and for yourself, and a quiet month is a good time to practice it.

Quiet months, in three relationships

  • An empty nest. With the kids gone, the list is short and nothing slips. The look-back finds a different gap: they haven’t planned anything together in months. See Kids grown and gone.
  • Old friends. Nothing has gone wrong, but one of them has started every catch-up for a year. A quiet month is a gentle time to say so. See Friends.
  • Gaming and time together. No chores slipped, but evenings have drifted into separate screens. The question “What have we stopped noticing?” brings it up without blame. See Gaming and time together.
  • Research: People adapt to good things as well as bad ones, so what once felt special starts to feel normal. Psychologists call this hedonic adaptation. Saying thanks out loud is one way to keep noticing. More in the Library.

Common mix-up: “If nothing’s wrong, the look-back is a waste of time.” Fix: make the quiet-month version shorter, not absent. Ten minutes and three questions is enough, and it keeps the habit alive for the month you need it.

If only one of you is on board: ask the three questions of yourself, then share one answer. “Something I’ve stopped saying: I really like how you handle the car stuff.” Starting with a thank-you is the easiest way into a quiet-month talk.

How to talk about a pattern

When a gap needs a real conversation, use Check-ins for the order: one topic, a good moment, and hearing each other back before answering. Try the Signal Translator, which shows how a sentence may land, on the sentence that names the pattern.

How you say it matters. A pattern said as a verdict (“You never take out the trash”) will get argued with. A pattern said as a repeated fact (“The trash has slipped three weeks in a row. What’s getting in the way on Tuesdays?”) can get an owner. Keep the month from turning into overload, blame or criticism of the person. The ground rules are how the conversation stays a repair.

Give context on both sides before you decide anything. A gap that looks like neglect from one side may be a heavy month from the other, and a job that “always gets done” may be getting done at a cost nobody has said out loud. Each person says briefly what the month was like for them, and the other repeats back what they heard. Only then do you decide who owns what. The Complacency guide has words for raising something someone has stopped noticing.

If the talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.

A good habit: open every look-back by each naming one thing that went well this month. Turning Toward has more ideas for small, specific thank-yous.

Naming a pattern, in three relationships

  • Coworkers: “The client notes have been missing after three of the last four calls. What would make it easier to write them up?”
  • A parent and a teen: “Your bike’s been left out in the rain three times this month. What would help you remember?” (Not: “You never look after your things.”)
  • Two faiths, one family: “Both of our holiday seasons ended with me doing all the cooking. Can we plan next time differently?” See Two faiths.

Words you could use: “This has come up three times this month. What’s getting in the way?” “I’d like to hear what this month was like for you before we decide anything.”

If only one of you is on board: name the pattern as a repeated fact, once, and ask one question. Then listen. You can’t make the other person join the look-back, but a calm question often gets a calmer answer than a meeting would.

  • Research: John Gottman’s research found that how a hard conversation starts strongly predicts how it ends. A gentle start (describe, feel, ask) goes better than an accusation. More in the Library.
  • Research: Saying back what you heard before answering, often called reflective listening, helps people feel understood. More in the Library.

What the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

The monthly look-back, set out as a working sheet in WP-04, does three things:

  1. It finds which jobs are real repeating gaps, not one-off misses.
  2. It decides whether each gap needs a new owner or a conversation about capacity.
  3. It closes the month cleanly, so next month starts from a fixed setup instead of the same unresolved argument.

It is not a monthly performance review of either person. And it never goes back to add up hours that were “owed.” Past hours can’t be handed back, and billing for them doesn’t fix anything; it just turns a look-back into a grievance. That doesn’t mean pretending they didn’t happen. You can thank someone for what they carried before (“I see how much you’ve been carrying”); you just don’t bill for the past. The only question the look-back asks about the past month is where the setup failed to hold. So the setup, not anyone’s effort or affection, is what changes for next month.

It also doesn’t replace the weekly catch-up. The monthly look-back only works because the weekly notes keep feeding it real information. Skip the weekly checks, and the monthly one becomes guesswork.

No blame, still responsible

Fields where mistakes can be serious, like aviation and healthcare, learned that when mistakes are punished, people hide them, and when they lead to learning, people report them. So they review what went wrong without assigning personal blame. The same thinking keeps a look-back honest at home. “No blame” doesn’t mean “nobody is responsible.” The owner still owns the job next month. It just means the question is what to change, not who to shame.

Common mix-up: “If we can’t bill past hours, the person who carried more just loses out.” Fix: say thank you for what was carried, and change who carries next month. That is how the balance is restored, going forward.

  • Research: Blameless reviews, and the idea of a “just culture” described by Sidney Dekker and James Reason, separate honest mistakes from reckless ones. More in the Library.
  • This site’s idea: Not billing for past hours is a choice this book makes, not a research finding. It keeps the look-back pointed forward.

Your part, and only your part

In the look-back you can only fix the gaps you own, and offer help with the rest. You can’t make someone keep a rhythm. Keep the rhythms you can keep, notice which ones are kept in return, and let your effort follow: more where it’s shared, less where it isn’t, so the rhythm doesn’t run on one person’s energy.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, the look-back turns into a list of complaints. Every ✗ becomes “you, again.” Old hours get added up. Someone leaves feeling judged, and next month both people quietly find a reason to skip it.

When it goes right, it’s short, a little dull and oddly calming. A couple of jobs get new owners. One talk gets scheduled. The number of gaps is written down. Over the next few months it gets smaller, and you both notice the difference at home.

Questions people often ask

We missed a few weekly logs. Can we still do the look-back?

Yes. Use what you have, and be honest about the gaps in your notes. Just avoid making big changes based on thin information.

What if we disagree about which kind of gap something is?

Start with the simplest question: does the job have a named owner, and is that person the one actually doing it? If you still disagree, call it a structural gap and look at the job list together. Updating the list is low-risk and easy to undo.

How long should it take?

About twenty minutes. If it runs much longer, you’re probably solving capacity issues in the meeting. Schedule those as separate talks.

Can someone help us prepare?

Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you word the sentence that names a pattern.

Limits and cautions

  • The look-back finds patterns in jobs. It can’t tell you why someone feels the way they do. That still needs a conversation.
  • Don’t use it in a week when either of you is very stretched. A calm look-back a few days late beats a tense one on time.
  • Agreements made for an old life often need changing. The Ship of Theseus is a good story to keep in mind: a ship rebuilt plank by plank is still the same ship, and a home whose agreements get rebuilt is still your home, as long as you keep the plans up to date.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

Closing the books

This is the last chapter, but the program keeps going around. Each month, you look back, change one or two things, and start again. You see the work, stay in tune, check the split can last, know what you’re carrying, agree what fair means, and look back again. The aim was never a perfect ledger. It’s a shared life where nobody is quietly carrying more than the other can see.

A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating. For example: “Load today: heavy. Thank you for the dishes. One small thing: the car. My ask: could you fill it up?”
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately. For example: “How was your day?” “Fine.” Months go by and nobody has asked a real question.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong. For example: Three busy weeks, no real talk, and small things start to feel big.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back. For example: “I need twenty minutes. Can we talk again at eight?”
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover. For example: One person answers a text in a minute; the other answers at the end of the day. Neither is wrong.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking. For example: Sam owns the recycling: noticing it’s full, taking it out and buying the bags.
Pressure
Pressure is the number in Today’s Weather that shows how much is pressing on you today. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade. For example: A pressure of 40 leaves room in the day; 75 means go gently and leave big things for later.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?” For example: “I said that badly. What I meant was…”
Retune
To retune is to notice you’ve fallen out of step with someone and adjust together: slower, softer, or at a better time. For example: “Can we start that again? I came in too fast.”
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match. For example: You ask “When will you do it?” calmly. They hear pressure. Nobody meant harm, but it crackles.
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today. For example: Your window says “wait,” so you ask to talk after dinner instead of right now.
The Five Pillars
The Five Pillars are the five ideas under everything here: see the whole load, fix the setup, read your state first, tune how you send and receive, and notice the quiet incentives. For example: A chip near the top of each page shows which pillars that page uses.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees. For example: Buying the gift is one job. Remembering the birthday, picking the gift and wrapping it are three more.
Today’s Weather
Today’s Weather is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty. For example: Short night, busy day: the forecast says to keep big talks for tomorrow.
Turning toward
Turning toward means answering someone’s small bids for attention, even briefly, instead of missing or brushing them off. For example: “Oh, that’s a funny one!” instead of scrolling on.
Unbilled debt
Unbilled debt is the work of a shared life that got done but was never noticed or thanked. For example: You remembered the dentist appointment, and nobody knew there was anything to remember.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land. For example: A hint like “the bins are full” may be heard as a plain fact, not a request.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day. For example: Some people need a plan in writing. Others think best out loud.
Workpaper
A workpaper is a short worksheet. Each person fills in their own, then you read them together. For example: WP-01 is a week’s log of who did what.
