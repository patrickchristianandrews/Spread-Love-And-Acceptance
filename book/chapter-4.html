Two kinds of fair: equal vs fair at home

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

Agree on what “fair” means before you argue about it, and give a comment a moment to land before you answer it.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

  1. There are two kinds of fair.

    “Did everything get done?” and “Did each of us do what we said we’d do?” Both are reasonable. Friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realizing.

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind judges by outcomes: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy? It’s flexible and easy-going. Done carefully, it counts the cost to whoever covered, but done carelessly, it can hide who is always stepping in.

    Dig deeper: judging by results

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, but it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way.

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. (It can be the same person.) If it’s your job, the question is whether you did it, not whether someone else quietly covered for you. That protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    Dig deeper: agreeing which kind you’ll use

  5. Covering is kind. Say it out loud.

    Helping out when the owner can’t is lovely. Just mention it, so the help is seen: “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Help that stays silent turns into unseen work, and unseen help slowly becomes expected (see Complacency).

    Dig deeper: covering without resentment

  6. Let a comment land before you react.

    When a remark hits harder than it should, say “Give me a second.” The idea is borrowed loosely from brain science, where the brain turns down sounds and sensations automatically, in a fraction of a second. This book’s pause is different: a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. Let the spike pass, then decide whether it needs an answer now, later or not at all.

    Dig deeper: giving a comment time to land

  7. Notice, name, decide.

    Notice it landed hard. Name it kindly: “That hit harder than you probably meant.” Then, once you’ve settled, decide how to reply. Say it so it lands (WP-09) helps with the words.

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    “Was the job done?” is a question about a fact. “Did that comment hurt?” is a question about a feeling. Both matter. Answer them one at a time, not in the same argument.

    Dig deeper: facts and feelings, separately

Each idea in everyday life

The cards above give the short version. Here each idea gets a plain meaning, a real-life example, some words you could say out loud, and one small step to try. The examples come from many kinds of homes, because fair matters in all of them.

1. There are two kinds of fair

What it means: “Fair” can mean “the work got done” or “each of us kept our word.” Most people lean one way without ever saying so.

For example: Priya and Jo share a dorm room. Priya thinks the room is fair if it’s tidy by Friday, whoever tidies. Jo thinks it’s fair if each of them keeps their own half clean. By Wednesday both are annoyed, for different reasons. More in Sharing a room.

Words you could use: “When you say fair, do you mean it all gets done, or that we each do our bit?” “I think I lean toward promises. Which way do you lean?”

Try this: Each of you finish this sentence on your own, then compare: “To me, a fair week is one where…”

2. Fair by results

What it means: Whoever can, does. It works well in a messy week. The risk is that the same person always turns out to be the one who “can.”

For example: A grandmother is raising her two grandchildren, and her grown-up son helps when he’s home from work away. He pitches in “wherever it’s needed.” The house runs, but she is always the one who notices what is needed. More in Foster, adoptive and kinship families.

Words you could use: “I like that we’re flexible. Can we also check who ends up stepping in most?”

Try this: For one week, put a small mark next to every job you picked up that wasn’t planned. At the end of the week, look at whose marks there are.

3. Fair by promises

What it means: Each person owns certain jobs and does them. It’s clear and steady. It also needs room for hard weeks.

For example: Two business partners run a small café. One does the ordering, the other does the staff rota. When the rota is late, it isn’t “a team problem.” It’s a question for the person who owns the rota. When that partner’s child is in hospital, they agree a swap ahead of time instead of letting it slide.

Words you could use: “I’d like us each to own a few jobs, so nobody has to keep asking.” “It’s my job, and I can’t do it this week. Could you take it, and I’ll take the bins?”

Try this: Name one job you both think of as “sort of everyone’s.” Agree who owns it for the next month.

4. Look to the owner

What it means: The owner does the job from start to finish, including remembering it. The second name (a backstop) only notices if it slipped and asks once, kindly. The second name is not the boss.

For example: In a stepfamily, the 13-year-old owns feeding the dog, and his stepmom is the second name. At 7pm the bowl is empty. She says, “Dog’s dinner?” once. She doesn’t feed the dog herself, and she doesn’t give a lecture.

Words you could use: “This one’s yours, start to finish. I’ll only be the reminder if it slips.” “Is this still working for you, or should we swap?”

Try this: Pick one job that causes friction. Write two names next to it: the owner, and the second name.

5. Covering out loud

What it means: When you do someone else’s job, say so, simply and without a sting. Help that is seen stays a gift. Help that is silent slowly becomes expected.

For example: Two grown-up sisters take turns driving their dad to his appointments. When one covers the other’s turn, she texts: “I took Dad on Thursday since you had the late shift. No problem, just so you know.” Her sister replies with thanks, and nobody is quietly keeping score.

Words you could use: “I covered the school run this week, since you were away. Just so you know.” “Thank you for covering that. How was it for you?”

Try this: The next time you cover a job, mention it in one plain sentence, on the same day.

6. Let a comment land

What it means: A short pause you choose to take before you answer something that stung. It isn’t ignoring the comment. It’s waiting until you can answer it well.

For example: A nurse comes home from a night shift, and her husband says, “Oh, you’re up.” She hears “at last.” She takes a breath and says, “Give me a second, I’m very tired.” He meant “I’m glad to see you.” More in Different hours.

Words you could use: “Give me a second.” “That landed hard. Let me think before I answer.”

Try this: Agree on your “give me a second” phrase today, and agree what the other person does while they wait.

7. Notice, name, decide

What it means: Three small steps. Notice the sting, often in your body first. Name it out loud, kindly. Decide later whether it needs an answer now, later or not at all.

For example: A teenager tells her dad, “You never listen.” He feels his chest tighten (notice). He says, “That stings, and I want to hear you properly” (name). He decides it needs a real talk, tomorrow after school (decide). More in Teens.

Words you could use: “That hit harder than you probably meant.” “I want to answer that, just not this minute. Can we talk at eight?”

Try this: Write the three words, notice, name, decide, somewhere you’ll see them, like your phone’s lock screen or the fridge.

8. Keep the two questions apart

What it means: “Was the job done?” is about a fact. “Did that hurt?” is about a feeling. Each gets its own answer, one at a time.

For example: Two separated parents share the kids across two homes. The bag came back without the school shoes (a fact). The text about it felt cold (a feeling). They sort the shoes in one short message, and talk about the tone another day, or let it go. More in Co-parenting.

Words you could use: “Can we sort the shoes first, and come back to how that text felt?” “I hear two things here. Which shall we do first?”

Try this: When a talk gets tangled, ask out loud, “Fact or feeling?” and finish one before you start the other.

Your part, and only your part. You control whether, when and how you bring something up, and whether you cover a job out loud. How the other person responds is theirs. If you cover again and again with no thanks and no change, match the energy: cover less, and say so kindly. Fair by promises only works when both people keep theirs. The weekly catch-up

Sam and Alex share a home. Alex owns the trash, but Alex works late on Tuesdays, and Sam has quietly taken it out three weeks running. At the catch-up, Sam mentions it. Alex says Tuesday nights aren’t realistic with the late shifts. They agree to try a swap for the rest of the month: Sam takes the trash, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order. They’ll settle it at the monthly look-back in Chapter V.

Wednesday, 7pm

“Thanks for doing the dishes,” one of you says, a little flatly. It was meant as a thank-you. It sounded like a criticism. Before you know it, you’ve replied to the criticism, and now there really is one.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Notice which kind of fair comes naturally to you, and notice the quiet habit of covering for others without saying so. When a comment stings, check your own state before you answer.
  • Between you and others: Give each job one clear owner instead of blaming. Watch how unowned jobs slide toward one person, and keep things steady by mentioning help out loud and saying thank you.

For example: You notice you always take the bins out without saying anything (in you). At the weekly catch-up, the short end-of-week talk, you ask for the bins to get a named owner (between you).

On your own: your own kind of fair

Fair starts with knowing what fair means to you, and which jobs are truly yours.

  • Write down what fair means to you, in one sentence.
  • List the jobs you own, and the ones you picked up without ever agreeing.
  • Practice letting a comment land before you answer, then reply with a fact, a feeling and an ask.

For example: Dev lives alone after a divorce and has his kids every other weekend. He writes one line: “Fair, to me, is that I don’t do their jobs for them just because I miss them.” So on his weekends, the kids still own clearing the table. More in On my own.

Words you could use (to yourself): “Did I agree to this job, or did I just start doing it?” “That stung. I’ll answer it later, when I’m calmer.”

Try this: Pick one job you picked up without agreeing. Decide whether to keep it on purpose, or to raise it at your next calm chance.

In real life

  • Partners: One counts hours spent cleaning. The other counts all the planning that happened before anyone picked up a cloth. Both are real work, so put the planning on the list too, in minutes.
  • Parents: Older kids can own age-sized jobs too, like feeding the cat or packing their own lunch. Put their name on the list, with a parent as the second name.
  • Family: One sibling visits a parent every week. Another pays for extra help. Each thinks they’re doing their share, in a different currency.
  • Roommates: One wants a fixed schedule. The other prefers “whoever’s free does it.” Neither is lazy. They just use different kinds of fair.
  • Coworkers: One person quietly fixes everyone else’s slides before the meeting. Because it always gets done, nobody knows it was ever a problem.
  • More than two partners: In a home with three adults, the one who works from home becomes the one who “can,” every time. Fair by results quietly lands on them. More in More than two partners.
  • Different hours: One works nights and sleeps in the day. Sleep is part of their work, so the day jobs can’t all fall to them just because they’re “home.” More in Different hours.
  • When one of you is ill: Share by energy, not by hours. The partner with a long-term illness can own jobs that can be done sitting down, with an agreed backup for low days. More in When one of you is ill.
  • Grown-up children: A mum keeps “helping” in her daughter’s kitchen during visits. Agreeing who owns what in whose home stops the same small clash every time. More in Grown-up children.
  • Two faiths, one family: Two festival seasons means two lots of cooking, cards and visits. Fair might mean each person owns the work for their own tradition, with help from the other. More in Two faiths.
  • Friends: One friend always hosts. Nobody asked them to, and nobody thanks them anymore. More in Friends.

Words you could use: “I think we’re counting in different ways. Can we put both on the list?” “Could we pick an owner for this one, so neither of us has to keep remembering?”

Try this: Find the example above that sounds most like your home. Read it to the other person and ask, “Does this sound like us?”

Common mix-ups

  • “If it got done, there’s no problem.” Someone may be paying for that in time and energy.
  • “Holding the owner to it is being strict.” It’s just keeping the agreement you both made. That’s kinder than quiet resentment.
  • “Letting a comment land means ignoring it.” It’s about timing, not about swallowing your feelings.
  • “‘Give me a second’ is rude.” It’s a way of taking the other person seriously enough to answer well.
  • “One kind of fair is the right one.” Both are reasonable. What matters is agreeing which one you’ll use.
  • “The owner is the boss of that job.” The owner does the job. They don’t get to tell everyone else what to do about it. And the second name isn’t a supervisor either.
  • “I covered for months, so now I can bring it all up.” A long list of old covering lands as a bill. Mention covering as it happens, and look forward when you talk.
  • “If I pause, I have to come back with a perfect answer.” You only have to come back. “I’m still a bit upset, but I can talk now” is enough.

How to fix a mix-up: Name it gently, as a mix-up, not a fault: “I think we’ve been using different kinds of fair.” Then go back to the one thing you both agree on, such as who owns the job, and start again from there.

Words you could use: “I don’t think either of us is wrong. I think we’ve been using different rulers.”

A note on safety

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

What it means: “Fair by promises” is about promises both people chose, and either can ask to change. A list of rules one person sets for the other, with checking, punishment or money held back, is not a promise. That is control, and it’s not your fault. Money together explains the difference between normal limits and control.

For example: If you’re covering jobs because you’re afraid of what will happen if they aren’t done, that isn’t covering. That’s a sign to get support for yourself first.

Words you could use, with someone you trust: “Things at home don’t feel fair, and I’m scared to bring it up. Can I talk it through with you?”
