Two kinds of fair: equal vs fair at home

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

Agree on what “fair” means before you argue about it, and give a comment a moment to land before you answer it.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

  1. There are two kinds of fair.

    “Did everything get done?” and “Did each of us do what we said we’d do?” Both are reasonable. Friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realizing.

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind judges by outcomes: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy? It’s flexible and easy-going. Done carefully, it counts the cost to whoever covered, but done carelessly, it can hide who is always stepping in.

    Dig deeper: judging by results

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, but it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way.

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. (It can be the same person.) If it’s your job, the question is whether you did it, not whether someone else quietly covered for you. That protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    Dig deeper: agreeing which kind you’ll use

  5. Covering is kind. Say it out loud.

    Helping out when the owner can’t is lovely. Just mention it, so the help is seen: “I took out the trash this week, since you were away.” Help that stays silent turns into unseen work.

    Dig deeper: covering without resentment

  6. Let a comment land before you react.

    When a remark hits harder than it should, say “Give me a second.” This chapter’s formal name borrows “sensory gating” from brain science. In the lab, it’s automatic and takes a fraction of a second. This book’s pause is different: a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. Let the spike pass, then decide whether it needs an answer now, later or not at all.

    Dig deeper: giving a comment time to land

  7. Notice, name, decide.

    Notice it landed hard. Name it kindly: “That hit harder than you probably meant.” Then, once you’ve settled, decide how to reply. Say it so it lands (WP-09) helps with the words.

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    “Was the job done?” is a question about a fact. “Did that comment hurt?” is a question about a feeling. Both matter. Answer them one at a time, not in the same argument.

    Dig deeper: facts and feelings, separately

The weekly catch-up

Sam and Alex share a home. Alex owns the trash, but Alex works late on Tuesdays, and Sam has quietly taken it out three weeks running. At the catch-up, Sam mentions it. Alex says Tuesday nights aren’t realistic with the late shifts. They agree to try a swap for the rest of the month: Sam takes the trash, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order. They’ll settle it at the monthly look-back in Chapter V.

Wednesday, 7pm

“Thanks for doing the dishes,” one of you says, a little flatly. It was meant as a thank-you. It sounded like a criticism. Before you know it, you’ve replied to the criticism, and now there really is one.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Notice which kind of fair comes naturally to you, and notice the quiet habit of covering for others without saying so. When a comment stings, check your own state before you answer.
  • Between you and others: Give each job one clear owner instead of blaming. Watch how unowned jobs slide toward one person, and keep things steady by mentioning help out loud and saying thank you.

Two fields, one idea: Philosophy + Business. People can mean two different things by “fair.” A job list with one owner per job makes it simple: check the job, not the person.

In real life

  • Partners: One counts hours spent cleaning. The other counts all the planning that happened before anyone picked up a cloth. Both are real work, so put the planning on the list too, in minutes.
  • Parents: Older kids can own age-sized jobs too, like feeding the cat or packing their own lunch. Put their name on the list, with a parent as the second name.
  • Family: One sibling visits a parent every week. Another pays for extra help. Each thinks they’re doing their share, in a different currency.
  • Roommates: One wants a fixed schedule. The other prefers “whoever’s free does it.” Neither is lazy. They just use different kinds of fair.
  • Coworkers: One person quietly fixes everyone else’s slides before the meeting. Because it always gets done, nobody knows it was ever a problem.

Common mix-ups

  • “If it got done, there’s no problem.” Someone may be paying for that in time and energy.
  • “Holding the owner to it is being strict.” It’s just keeping the agreement you both made. That’s kinder than quiet resentment.
  • “Letting a comment land means ignoring it.” It’s about timing, not about swallowing your feelings.
  • “‘Give me a second’ is rude.” It’s a way of taking the other person seriously enough to answer well.
  • “One kind of fair is the right one.” Both are reasonable. What matters is agreeing which one you’ll use.

Try this: Agree on what counts as fair on a calm, easy day, not in the middle of a disagreement.

Start by each saying which kind of fair comes more naturally to you: results or promises.

Then pick three regular jobs and write an owner for each in the job list (WP-03): the person who does it. Add a second name, someone who notices if it slips. It can be the same person.

Finally, agree on a short phrase for when something lands hard, like “Give me a second.” Agree that the other person will wait, and that the person who asked for a second will come back to it.

A note on safety

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor, together or on your own.
