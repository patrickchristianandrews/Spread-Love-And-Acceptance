Part Two: Between us · Chapter IV

Two kinds of fair: equal vs fair at home

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Agree on what “fair” means before you argue about it, and give a comment a moment to land before you answer it.

In short

  • There are two kinds of fair, by results (did it all get done?) and by promises (did each of us do what we agreed?), so agree which one you are using.
  • Once a job has an owner, look to the owner, and when you cover for someone, say so out loud.
  • Let a comment land before you react by noticing it, naming it and then deciding what to say.
  • Keep the two questions apart, because whether the job was done is a fact and whether the comment hurt is a feeling.

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing in the first place. You are both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate. Usually on a Sunday evening. Usually, for some reason, about the oven.

  1. There are two kinds of fair.

    One kind asks whether everything got done. The other asks whether each person did what they said they would. Both are reasonable. Friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realising.

    Nisha and Kezia share a room in halls. Nisha thinks a week was fair if the room is tidy by Friday, whoever tidied it. Kezia thinks it was fair if each of them kept her own half clean. For most of the first term each thought the other was being unreasonable, and said so to other people, and neither of them was. It came out at two in the morning over a shared packet of noodles, and they both laughed until the girl next door banged on the wall.

    You could say “When you say fair, do you mean it all gets done, or that we each do our bit?”

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind of fair judges by outcomes, by whether the right things got done by whoever had the time or energy. It’s flexible and easy-going. Done carefully it counts the cost to whoever covered. Done carelessly it hides who is always stepping in.

    Marjorie is raising her grandchildren. Her grown-up son helps with whatever’s needed when he’s home, cheerfully and well. The house runs. And she is always, always the one who notices what’s needed. She didn’t see it herself until she marked, for one week, every job she picked up that wasn’t planned. Forty-one. She showed her son. He said, “Forty-one?” and then, after a bit, “Right.”

    Dig deeper: judging by results

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, and it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way. Two business partners run a café. One owns the ordering, the other owns the staff rota. When the rota is late (it’s the Thursday before a bank holiday, it is always late then) they ask the rota owner about it. Not “the team”. And nobody takes it personally, or not for long.

    Words you could use “It’s my job, and I can’t do it this week. Could you take it, and I’ll take the bins?”

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job from start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop, who notices if it slipped and checks in gently without supervising, and it can be the same person. If the job is yours, the question is whether you did it, and somebody quietly covering for you doesn’t answer that. It also protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    In one stepfamily, Ethan, who is thirteen, owns feeding the dog. His stepmum Claire is the second name. When the bowl is empty at seven and the dog is staring at it with enormous meaning, Claire says “Dog’s dinner?” once. Just that. She doesn’t do it for him and she doesn’t lecture. For the first month this nearly killed her. Ethan now feeds the dog most nights before anyone mentions it, and the dog has stopped staring at Claire.

    Dig deeper: agreeing which kind you’ll use

  5. Covering is kind. Say it out loud.

    Helping out when the owner can’t is lovely, and it helps to mention it, so the help is seen. “I put the bins out this week, since you were away.” Help that stays silent turns into unseen work, and unseen help slowly becomes expected (see Complacency).

    Two sisters take turns driving their dad to his appointments. When one covers for the other, she texts the same day. Took Dad Thurs, you were on lates. All fine, bloods normal, he flirted with the receptionist. Ten seconds. It stops the help going invisible, and the second half of the message is, they both agree, the important bit.

    Dig deeper: covering without resentment

  6. Let a comment land before you react.

    When a remark hits harder than it should, say “Give me a second.” The idea is borrowed loosely from brain science, where the brain turns down sounds and sensations automatically in a fraction of a second, while this pause is a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. Let the spike pass, then decide whether the remark needs an answer now or later, or at all.

    Two in the afternoon. Fran has come downstairs after a night shift, hair everywhere. Her husband looks up from the table. “Oh, you’re up.” She hears at last. She’s halfway into a reply before she stops and says, “Give me a second, I’m really tired.” He looks puzzled. He’d meant he was glad to see her. He’d saved her the last of the soup (see Different hours). Agree your own “give me a second” phrase today, while things are calm.

    Dig deeper: giving a comment time to land

  7. Notice, name, decide.

    First notice that a comment landed hard. Then name it, kindly (“That hit harder than you probably meant”). Once you’ve settled, decide how to reply. Say it so it lands helps with the words.

    “You never listen,” his fifteen-year-old said, on the stairs. Andy felt his chest go tight. He wanted very much to list the times he had listened. What he said was, “Ouch. That stings. And I do want to hear you properly.” Then he decided it needed a real talk, the next evening, after her swimming, in the car, which is where they talk best, both looking at the road.

    Ways to say it “I want to answer that, just not this minute. Can we talk at eight?”

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    Whether the job was done is a question about a fact. Whether the comment hurt is a question about a feeling. Both matter, and each goes better in its own conversation.

    Separated parents share their children across two homes. The bag came back on Sunday without the school shoes, and with it a text about the shoes that felt, to the one who received it, very cold. They sorted the shoes first, on the phone, in about two minutes. They talked about the text the following Sunday, which took longer, and was more awkward, and was better for waiting a week (see Co-parenting). When a talk tangles, “Fact or feeling?” is a good question to ask out loud. Finish one before you start the other.

    Dig deeper: facts and feelings, separately

Your part, and only your part. You control whether, when and how you bring something up, and whether you cover a job out loud. How the other person responds is theirs. If you cover again and again with no thanks and no change, match the energy: cover less, and say so kindly. Fair by promises only works when both people keep theirs. The weekly catch-up

Sam and Alex share a home. Alex owns the bins but works late on Tuesdays, and Sam has quietly taken them out three weeks running. At the catch-up Sam mentions it, not very smoothly. Alex goes a bit red and admits that Tuesday nights aren’t realistic with the late shifts. They agree to try a swap for the rest of the month, with Sam taking the bins and Alex taking the recycling and the weekly grocery order, and to settle it at the monthly look-back in Chapter V.

Wednesday, 7pm

“Thanks for doing the dishes,” one of you says, slightly flat. It was meant as a thank-you. It came out sounding like a criticism. Before you know it you’ve answered the criticism, and the person who said thank you is now defending a thank-you, and the dishes are still in the rack.

On your own: your own kind of fair

Fair starts with knowing what it means to you, and which jobs are truly yours. Write down what fair means to you in one sentence, and list the jobs you own alongside the ones you picked up without ever agreeing to. Errol lives alone and has his children every other weekend. His sentence, after a lot of crossing out, is that fair means not doing their jobs for them just because he misses them (see On my own). He finds this very hard on Sunday afternoons, when he’d happily do everything if it meant another hour. Pick one job you took on without agreeing, and either keep it on purpose or raise it on a calm day.

Common mix-ups

If it got done, there may still be a problem, because someone may be paying for it in time and energy. Holding the owner to the job isn’t being strict. It’s keeping the agreement you both made, which is kinder than quiet resentment. Letting a comment land doesn’t mean ignoring it. It’s about timing, and you still get to answer. And please don’t save up months of covering to bring out all at once. A long list lands like a bill. Mention covering as it happens.

Money between you

Money is where the two kinds of fair meet most often. Some couples put everything into one pot. Some keep their money separate and pay agreed bills, often half each, which is fair by promises, the same rule for both. Others each pay into a shared pot in line with what they earn and keep the rest, which is fair by results, since each is left with a similar share. Many couples mix them, with a joint account for bills and food and some money each that nobody has to explain. The right way is the one you both chose with your eyes open. To talk about it, start with your money stories, how money worked at home when you were growing up, then move on to the numbers and to a “talk first” amount for big purchases that applies to both of you. A limit you both chose and both keep is care, not control.

Niamh takes home £2,600 a month and Tariq £1,900, and their rent and bills will be £1,500. They split it 58/42, in line with their pay, so she pays £870 and he pays £630.

Words to try “Can we each say how money worked in our families growing up?” “Would half each, or in line with our pay, feel fairer to you?”

A couple and a housemate

A couple sharing a home with a housemate who isn’t a partner is a different shape from three equals. It helps to keep two lists, house jobs for all three of you, such as the bins, the bathroom, the bills and the landlord, and couple jobs for the two of you. Decide house questions with all three of you there, not in bed the night before. Hear the housemate first on anything that affects them, keep couple arguments out of shared rooms, and never ask them to referee. Ollie and Hamza share a flat with their friend Bex. They split the rent by room, since the couple has the bigger one, and the bills by person, and all three hold a short house meeting on the first Sunday of the month, with biscuits, which Bex insists is the only reason she turns up.

You could say “That’s a house question. Let’s decide it when Bex is here.”

A note on safety

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about what’s fair ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A promise is something both people chose and either can ask to change. Rules one person sets for the other, with checking, punishment or money held back, are control, and that is not your fault. See normal limits and control.

Words you could use, with someone you trust “Things at home don’t feel fair, and I’m scared to bring it up. Can I talk it through with you?”
