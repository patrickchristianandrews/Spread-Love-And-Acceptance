How communication works: meaning, listening, repair and messages

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Talking feels simple because we do it all day, yet every message has to travel from one mind to another, and a lot can change on the way. This chapter follows that journey and looks at what helps a message arrive the way you meant it.

In short

  • Meaning is made between two people. What you say and what they hear can differ without either of you being wrong.
  • Every message says two things, the words themselves and what they seem to say about the two of you.
  • A short question saves a long misunderstanding, so check before you guess.
  • Mix-ups are normal, and repairing them is the skill. Small talk is allowed to be short.

Thursday, 8:15am

Ana texts her brother. Can you call me when you’re free?

She means no rush, small question about the weekend. He reads it on the top deck of the 8.10, and for eleven stops, past the hospital, which doesn’t help, he works out who is ill.

Small habits help a message arrive. Listening, checking, starting softly, mending quickly. A few ready-made lines make small talk easier for anyone who dreads it. And three tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. Say it so it lands (Part Two, Chapter IV) asks for what happened, how you feel and one clear request, because every message carries a second message about the relationship. The 90-second daily check-in (Part Two, Chapter I) keeps repairs small and frequent. And the Chat analyzer’s whole-conversation view shows where a written conversation lost its tone.

  1. Meaning is made between two people.

    Words don’t carry meaning on their own. The speaker has a meaning in mind, and the listener builds their own from the words, the tone, the timing and everything they already expect. Most of the time the two match well enough. Sometimes they don’t, and neither person can see the gap from where they’re standing.

    “Let’s chat tomorrow,” the manager wrote to the new starter at ten to five, meaning a friendly catch-up and possibly a biscuit. The new starter went home and reread his last three emails looking for the mistake. He found two, neither of which existed. In the morning there was a biscuit. He couldn’t eat it.

    You could say “I think what I meant and what you heard might be two different things. Can we check?”

  2. Every message says two things.

    There is what you say, and there is what it says about the two of you. “You’re late” is a fact about time. It can also mean “I was worried” or “I don’t matter to you.” People argue about the fact when the hurt is in the second layer.

    I take one eight-word sentence apart on the website’s About page: “don’t worry about it, I’ll sort it out.” Said kindly, in passing, it’s reassurance. Said for the third time that month about the same job, it also says a job has no owner and a decision has been put off again. Both layers are in the sentence.

    Or take Tomasz, who wants to know whether his housemate used his good pan. On the surface it’s a question about a pan. Underneath, it’s a question about whether his things are respected in this house, and no amount of talk about the pan is going to settle that. “Honestly,” he said in the end, “I don’t care about the pan.” His housemate looked baffled. “Then why have we been talking about it for ten minutes?”

  3. Listening is something you do.

    Hearing happens by itself. Listening is a choice, and most of us do a lot of it partly in order to reply. To fix, or defend, or advise, or tell our own story. Real listening means following what the other person is trying to say, and letting them know you got it, before you add anything of yours.

    “School is pointless,” Mollie announced, thirteen, coat still on. Her dad started explaining, at some length and with examples, why it wasn’t. She stopped talking and went upstairs. The next time she said it, a week later, he bit his tongue. Hard. “Pointless how?” he said. “Tell me about today.” She talked for twenty minutes, mostly about a supply teacher and a seating plan, and he said almost nothing, which nearly killed him.

    Ways to say it “So what I’m hearing is... Have I got that right?” “Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?”

    Dig deeper: what good listening is

  4. Check, don’t guess.

    Once we know something it’s very hard to imagine not knowing it. So we leave out steps, assume the other person can tell what we mean, and guess what they meant instead of asking. We do it most with the people we know best. A short check costs a sentence. A wrong guess can cost a whole evening.

    “Something low-key,” they agreed, for Fliss’s fortieth. Fliss pictured a Chinese takeaway on the sofa, in pyjamas, with the good crackers. Her best friend booked a quiet table at the Italian, ironed a shirt, and turned up at seven with flowers. Fliss opened the door in her pyjamas. Both were disappointed. Both had thought it was obvious. They ate the takeaway in the end, the friend in her ironed shirt, and it was a good night, eventually.

    Words to try “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean...?” “When you say soon, do you mean today or this week?”

  5. Hard things land better when they start soft.

    How a hard conversation starts often decides how it ends. A complaint about one thing (“The bins weren’t taken out”) is easier to hear than a criticism of the person (“You never help”). A simple shape works for most hard things, which is to say what happened, how you feel and what you would like.

    A woman wants her eighty-one-year-old father to stop driving at night. She has “You’re not safe any more” on the tip of her tongue. She leaves it there and says, “You had two near-misses last month and I lie awake worrying. Could I drive us in the evenings?” He says he’ll think about it, which in his language is nearly a yes.

    Dig deeper: saying hard things kindly

  6. Mix-ups are normal. Repair is the skill.

    Even close, kind people misunderstand each other constantly. What keeps a relationship steady is noticing it and fixing it. “That came out wrong.” “Can we start again?” Small repairs made often build trust much faster than big apologies made rarely.

    “Excuse me!” a woman snaps in the supermarket, because your trolley is blocking the tins. “I’m moving,” you snap back. A second later you hear yourself add, “Sorry. Long day.” And she sags a bit and says hers has been too, and somehow you end up talking about the price of coffee for five minutes by the chopped tomatoes.

    Words you could use “Can I try that again?” “I think I took that the wrong way. What did you mean?”

  7. Messages lose their tone on the way.

    A text or email carries the words without your face or your voice, so the reader fills in the tone with however they feel right now. A tired reader hears a tired, cross message. Short replies, missing greetings and late-night timing all get read as meaning something.

    The group chat for the school summer fair. Somebody proposes the tombola goes by the gate. One parent replies, “Fine.” Full stop and everything. Within four minutes two other parents have privately decided she’s furious and started composing careful messages. She was stirring a pan of soup, typing with one thumb, and genuinely thought it was fine.

    Before you send your next tricky message, read it out loud in the grumpiest voice you can manage. If it sounds harsh, add a warm word. This is my favourite test in the whole book, and it takes four seconds.

  8. Different styles aren’t wrong styles.

    Some people say exactly what they mean. Others hint, and expect you to read between the lines. Some talk fast and overlap. Others need a pause to think. Families and cultures and different kinds of mind all shape this, and a clash between two styles is usually a translation problem. Nobody has to be difficult.

    “Is the report ready?” “No. I need two more days.” Her manager heard that as rude, for about a month, until they talked about it over a coffee he didn’t want. She said she’d rather tell him the truth plainly than make him guess. After that he started finding her clarity a relief, and then, slightly to his surprise, a model.

    Words to try “I’m quite direct, so if I sound blunt, it doesn’t mean I’m cross.” “It helps me if you say exactly what you need.”

  9. Small talk has a job, and it can be short.

    Small talk (“Cold today, isn’t it?” “Busy weekend?”) isn’t really about the weather. Its job is to say that you see the other person and that the two of you are okay. In 1923 the anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski called this “phatic communion”, talk whose main purpose is bonding more than information. Small moments like this matter more than people expect. Sandstrom and Dunn (2014) found that people who had a short, friendly exchange with a coffee-shop barista felt more belonging and a better mood than people who kept it purely efficient.

    Small talk is hard work for many people, though, including many autistic people, shy or anxious people and people speaking a second language. The rules are unwritten, and it uses energy that some people would rather keep. A few ready-made lines, sometimes called social scripts, take the guesswork out, and keeping it short is allowed. For what it’s worth, this is the part I had to learn on purpose.

    Phil dreads the school gate. He has three lines ready. “Morning! How’s your week going?” Then “Oh, nice.” Then “Right, I’d better get going, see you Friday.” Some mornings he still sits in the car with the radio on until the bell goes. Most days he gets through it. One of the other dads now saves him a spot by the railings, and has never once asked him how his week is going, which Phil appreciates more than he can say.

    You could say “Morning! How’s your week been?” “How do you know Nico?” “Oh, how was that?” “Lovely to see you. I’d better get on.” And to opt out kindly, “I’m not great at chit-chat, but it’s really nice to see you.”

In everyday life

On your own: how you talk to yourself

You send yourself messages all day, from “Typical, I always mess this up” to “That was hard and I handled it.” The same things apply. Notice the tone, check whether it’s fair, and mend it when it’s harsh. Catch one harsh sentence you say to yourself today and rewrite it the way you’d say it to someone you like.

With other people

Between partners, “We never go out any more” sounds like a complaint about evenings, and underneath it may mean “I miss you”, so answering that layer goes further than defending the calendar. “Stop that!” tells a child what not to do without saying what to do instead. “Feet on the floor, please” is easier to follow. In meetings, silence gets taken as agreement until a quieter colleague says afterwards that she never agreed, and a round of “Anyone see it differently?” catches that early. “Are you eating properly?” is care in one generation’s language and checking up in another’s.

  • Feedback at work. Talk about the work rather than the person, with one recent example of what good looks like. Then ask, “What’s been getting in the way?” When you’re the one receiving it, “Thanks. Can I think about it and come back tomorrow?” is a fine first answer. Common mix-ups

“If they loved me, they’d just know” is the big one. Closeness makes us check less without making us understand any more, so saying it clearly is a kindness, and no test of love. Listening isn’t agreeing, either. You can understand someone completely and still see it differently, and the understanding comes first. A repair doesn’t mean you’re confessing to being the bad one. It just says that didn’t go the way you wanted, and either person can make one. And texts feel safer for hard things because they’re easier to send. They’re also much easier to misread, so for anything tender, add warmth, or ring.

If you want to help someone else with this

Maybe a friend keeps having the same argument with their partner, or your teenager feels nobody listens. You can’t talk for them. You can be the one person who really hears them, without fixing, which is often the most useful thing anyone does all week. You can help them pull the two layers apart, by asking what it was about and then what it seemed to say about the two of them. You can offer the simple shape (what happened, how they feel, what they’d like) and let them find their own words. And stay out of judging the other person’s character, since you only hear one side and they may make up next week. Notice if it’s more than a mix-up. If they seem afraid of the other person, gently share Not safe at home?

Words you could use “Do you want me to just listen, or help you think about what to say?”

When to get more help

Most mix-ups can be eased with the ideas here. Some situations need more.

  • When talking makes things worse every time, a couples or family counsellor can slow conversations down and help both people be heard.
  • When you’re afraid of how someone will react, that isn’t a communication problem. Clearer words are not the fix, and you don’t have to keep trying. Not safe at home? lists free, private help, and signs to look out for may help you name what’s happening.
  • When someone is always “misunderstanding” you in ways that make you doubt your memory or your sense of what happened, talk with someone you trust outside the relationship.
  • When speaking or understanding is hard for you every day, because of anxiety, a hearing or language difference, or how your mind works, a doctor, speech and language therapist or counsellor may help. Needing support is not a failing.
