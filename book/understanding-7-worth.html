Self-worth, shame and self-compassion

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why your worth isn’t a score you keep earning, how shame differs from guilt, and why being kind to yourself makes it easier to grow.

Thursday, 11:20pm

You sent the wrong file to the whole team. Someone noticed, fixed it and said there were no worries. Hours later you are still awake and replaying it, and the voice in your head has moved on from the file. It is telling you that you are useless, and that everyone knows it now.

This chapter looks at how self-worth and shame work and then at what you can do about them, whether you are on your own, at home, with friends or at work. Nothing here is a diagnosis, so take what helps and leave the rest.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    Many of us carry a quiet scoreboard on which we are worth something only when we are useful, liked, slim, busy, top of the class or never a bother. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth”, and the trouble with them is that any score can drop. If your worth rises and falls with each day’s results, every bad day feels like a verdict on you. A retired teacher may feel lost for months after she stops working, even though nothing is wrong, because for forty years being needed was how she knew she mattered.

    It can help to finish the line “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times. The endings you write are your scores, and for now it is enough to notice them. On a bad day, you might remind yourself that you matter on days when you achieve nothing at all.

    Dig deeper: worth that doesn’t have to be earned

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points towards fixing it by saying sorry or making it right. Shame is about who you are, which feels impossible to fix, and so it tends to make people hide, freeze, blame someone else or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is more likely to lead to repair. When a teenager breaks his mum’s favourite mug, guilt tells him to own up and offer to help pay for a new one. Shame tells him he ruins everything, and he wraps the pieces in newspaper and hides them at the bottom of the bin.

    Next time you feel bad about something, ask whether it is about what you did or about who you are. If it has become about who you are, try to turn it back into one thing you did, and then ask what would make that thing right.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    Shame shows up in the body first, as a hot face, lowered eyes, a sinking stomach and the wish for the floor to swallow you. It can also come out sideways, as sudden anger or a cold, closed face, which is how a small comment can start a big fight. In a meeting a coworker asks, “Didn’t we already cover this?”, and his colleague snaps back at him. Later she realises the snap had nothing to do with the question, because what she felt was stupid in front of everyone.

    Learning your own shame signs helps. Next time you suddenly want to disappear or to snap, you can name it quietly to yourself as shame, and sometimes say out loud, “I think I felt embarrassed just then, and it came out sharp. Can I try again?”

    Dig deeper: how shame works in the body

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh inner voice usually started as protection. If you criticise yourself first, perhaps nobody else can hurt you, and if you are hard on yourself, perhaps you will never fail again. It meant well. A voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, though, and it is tiring to live with. A young dad calls himself an idiot every time he forgets something for the baby, the same word his own father used about him, so the voice he hears is an old one that he borrowed long ago.

    Write down one thing your inner critic says often, and ask whether you would say it to a friend you love, and what you would say instead. Some people find it helps to answer the critic directly, with something like “Thanks for trying to protect me. I can take it from here.”

    Dig deeper: the critic and the soothing system

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you would treat a good friend who is struggling. One well-known model has three parts, which are kindness in place of attack, remembering that everyone struggles, and noticing the pain without drowning in it. Studies, such as one by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012), suggest that people who are kind to themselves after a failure are often more likely to try to improve. A woman who fails her driving test for the third time tells herself that this is hard, that lots of people need more goes, and that she will practise reversing round corners before the next one. Then she books another lesson.

    If it feels okay, put a hand on your chest or arm and say three lines slowly to yourself once today. “This is a hard moment.” “Other people feel this too.” “What do I need right now?”

  6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

    We all measure ourselves against others, because that is how people work out where they stand. Feeds full of other people’s best moments make that measuring unfair, and self-esteem that depends on being better than others goes up and down a great deal. Two old friends meet for dinner, one with a new house and the other renting a room. The renter spends the evening quietly ranking herself and misses the fact that her friend is lonely and had wanted to talk.

    When you notice a comparison today, try swapping it for a question about the other person, such as “How are you, really?” It also helps to remember that you are comparing your inside with their outside.

    Dig deeper: comparison and steady self-worth

  7. You can own a mistake without drowning in it.

    Owning a mistake is easier when it doesn’t mean you are worthless. A simple shape helps. Say what you did, say what it did to the other person, say what you will do now, and then stop, because beating yourself up proves nothing about how sorry you are. A housemate forgot to pay her share of the bill and the internet was cut off for the weekend. She tells the others, “I forgot to pay, and that left you without internet all weekend. I’ve paid it and set a reminder,” and that is the end of it.

    Think of one small thing you still feel bad about, write the three-part sentence, and decide whether to send it, say it or simply let it rest.

  8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they are treated, and adults go on learning it. How we talk to each other matters a great deal, because “you did a careless thing” leaves room to change and “you’re so careless” closes the door. Praise for effort and for who someone is, as well as for results, helps people feel they matter on bad days too. A team lead begins each project review by asking what the team learned instead of who got it wrong, and within a few months people are raising problems in week two instead of week ten.

    Today you could say one thing to someone that has nothing to do with their results, such as “I’m glad you’re here, whatever happened today” or “I’m glad I know you.”

    Dig deeper: how worth is given and learned

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control how other people judge you, or what you were taught about your worth growing up. You can control how you speak to yourself now, whether you repair what you can, and where you spend your time. Give your energy to people who treat you as someone who matters. Friday, 9:15am

You write one line to the team, saying sorry for the mix-up with yesterday’s file and attaching the right one. When the old voice starts up, you answer it the way you would answer a friend. You made a mistake and you fixed it, which is what a decent colleague does. Nobody mentions the file again, and by lunchtime you have stopped thinking about it too.

In everyday life

Shame turns up in ordinary places. Between partners, “You forgot to call” can land as “You’re a terrible partner”, so the reply comes out as a defence instead of a sorry. A child told “You’re so naughty” hears something about who they are, while “That was a naughty thing to do” leaves them a way back. A friend who says yes to everything may be quietly burning out because saying no feels like risking being dropped. At work, someone may hide a small error for weeks because owning it feels like proof that they don’t belong there.

On your own: a kinder voice to keep

You don’t need anyone else to start this, only to notice how you talk to yourself and to practise one gentler sentence. Catch the critic’s favourite words, such as “always”, “useless”, “pathetic” and “should”, and swap one of them for a plain fact, so that “I’m hopeless” becomes “I was late twice this week.” On hard days, keep a short list of things you did well, or simply did, and let getting up count. You are allowed to be disappointed in what you did and still be on your own side.

Common mix-ups

  • “Self-compassion is self-pity.” Self-pity says “only me, poor me.” Self-compassion says “this is hard, and other people go through it too,” which connects you to others.
  • “If I’m kind to myself, I’ll get lazy.” Research suggests the opposite is more common, because fear of your own attack makes people avoid trying and hide mistakes.
  • “Self-worth means thinking I’m great.” It means you matter even on the days you are not great.

If you want to help someone else with this

Talk about what happened, never about what kind of person they are. Arguing with someone’s shame (“You’re not useless!”) can feel like being told they are wrong to feel it, so you might try “It sounds like you feel really low about it. I don’t see you that way.” Helping them find one small thing they can do gives the feeling somewhere to go. Kindness on ordinary days, when nothing has gone wrong, teaches worth best of all.

You could say “That sounds like it’s weighing on you. Want to work out one thing that would help?” “You don’t have to earn being cared about.”

When to get more help

Everyone feels shame and self-doubt sometimes. It’s worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or therapist if harsh thoughts about yourself are there most days, if they stop you working, eating, sleeping or seeing people, or if you feel you deserve to be hurt. Therapies such as compassion-focused therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy are built to help with exactly this.

If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

If someone close to you keeps telling you you’re worthless, stupid or lucky to have them, that isn’t a self-esteem problem. It can be a way of controlling someone. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? have free, private help.
