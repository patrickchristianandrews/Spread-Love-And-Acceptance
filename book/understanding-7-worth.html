Self-worth, shame and self-compassion

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why your worth isn’t a score you keep earning, how shame differs from guilt, and why being kind to yourself makes it easier to grow.

Thursday, 11:20pm

You sent the wrong spreadsheet to the whole team. Last quarter’s, not this one’s. Priyanka spotted it in four minutes, sent the right one round and added “no worries!!” with two exclamation marks, which somehow made it worse.

It’s nearly midnight. The file stopped being the point some time around ten. The voice has moved on to bigger things now, like how you’ve always been a bit useless, really, and now they all know.

This chapter is about how self-worth and shame work, and then about what you can actually do with them. None of it is a diagnosis. Take what helps.

A wrong spreadsheet, read quickly, has layers. On the surface, a file sent to the whole team. Underneath, guilt about the file, which is useful. Underneath that, shame about yourself, which isn’t. The roots are signal and noise, what the mistake really says, and balance. The chapter slows down on the second and third layers.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    A lot of us carry a quiet scoreboard, on which we’re worth something when we’re useful, or liked, or slim, or busy, or top of the class, or never any bother to anybody. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth,” and the trouble with a score is that it can drop. If your worth goes up and down with each day’s results, then every bad day is a verdict.

    Pam taught third grade for forty years. In the September after she retired she found herself in the supermarket at ten on a Tuesday morning, holding a basket with nothing in it, feeling lost in a way she couldn’t have explained to anyone. Nothing was wrong. But for four decades being needed was how she knew she mattered, and the bell had stopped ringing.

    Try finishing “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times, then look at what you wrote. Those are your scores. Just noticing them is enough for now.

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points somewhere useful. Say sorry. Put it right. Shame is about who you are, which feels impossible to fix, and so it makes people hide, or freeze, or blame someone else, or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is the one more likely to lead to repair.

    Rhys is twelve and he has just knocked his mom’s favorite mug off the counter, the one with the hare on it that she bought on the seafront in Whitby years ago and drinks her first tea of the day from, and there are two ways this can go. Guilt says go and tell her, and maybe offer some birthday money. Shame says you ruin everything, and this time shame wins. He wraps the pieces in yesterday’s paper and pushes them right down to the bottom of the trash can, under the potato peelings, where his mom finds them on Sunday.

    It was the hiding that upset her. She told him so. It took him a while to see what she meant.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    You feel shame in the body before you think it. Hot face, eyes down, stomach dropping, a strong wish for the floor to open. It also comes out sideways, as sudden anger or a closed, cold face, which is how a small comment turns into a big fight.

    “Didn’t we already cover this?” It was a mild question, asked in a Tuesday meeting by a man who genuinely couldn’t remember. Becky snapped at him, properly, in front of nine people. That evening, scraping a plate into the trash, she worked out that it had nothing to do with him. She’d felt stupid in front of everyone, and stupid had come out as sharp.

    Learn your own signs, if you can. Next time you suddenly want to vanish, or bite, you can name it quietly to yourself, and now and then out loud. “Sorry, I think I felt embarrassed and it came out sharp. Can I try that again?”

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh inner voice usually started as protection. If you criticize yourself first, perhaps nobody else gets the chance. If you’re hard enough on yourself, perhaps you’ll never fail again. It meant well, in its way. But a voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, and it’s exhausting to share a head with.

    Perfectionism has been creeping up Students’ perfectionism scores rose between 1989 and 2016, according to a meta-analysis by Curran and Hill, with the sharpest rise in the feeling that other people expect you to be perfect. It’s questionnaire data from Western countries and it can’t say why, but if that feeling is loud for you, you have company.

    Curran and Hill (2019) · Moderate: questionnaire data from Western countries; does not explain the cause

    Every time Craig forgets something for the baby he calls himself an idiot. Out loud, sometimes. The wipes. The rabbit she won’t sleep without. It’s the word his own dad used about him, all through his childhood. Patting his pockets in the daycare parking lot for a rabbit that’s on the kitchen table, he hears it in his dad’s voice, exactly, and it strikes him as odd that he’s still using it.

    Write down one thing your critic says a lot. Would you say it to a friend you loved? Some people find it helps to answer the critic directly. “Thanks. I know you’re trying to protect me. I can take it from here.”

    Dig deeper: the critic and the soothing system

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you’d treat a good friend who’s having a rotten time. One well-known model has three parts. Kindness instead of attack. Remembering that everyone struggles. Noticing the pain without drowning in it. Studies, such as one by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012), suggest that people who are kind to themselves after a failure are often more likely to try to improve.

    Lindy failed her driving test for the third time, on the reverse around a corner again, clipping the same curb she had clipped in March, and afterward she sat in the DMV parking lot for a while with the engine off. Then she told herself what she’d have told her sister, more or less. This is hard. Loads of people need more goes. You’re fine everywhere except that one corner. On Sunday morning she was on the empty industrial estate by the beltway with her dad in the passenger seat, reversing around the same lamp post eleven times.

    If it feels all right, try putting a hand on your arm once today and saying, slowly, “This is a hard moment. Other people feel this too. What do I need?” It will feel daft the first time, and probably the second.

  6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

    Everyone measures themselves against other people. It’s how we work out where we stand. But a feed full of other people’s best moments makes the measuring wildly unfair, and self-esteem that depends on being better than others swings about all over the place.

    Why we compare at all Leon Festinger proposed in 1954 that people judge their opinions and abilities by comparing themselves with others, especially when there’s no objective standard to go by. Worth has no objective standard, which may be exactly why comparing goes so badly there.

    Festinger (1954) · Strong: a foundational theory with a large body of later research

    Two old friends met for a curry, one of them having just bought a house. The other rents a room in a shared apartment in Queens where the shower only works if you hold it. The renter spent the whole evening quietly ranking herself, and she missed what was right in front of her. Her friend was lonely in the new house, and had wanted to say so all evening, and kept waiting for a gap in the conversation that never came because the renter was so busy ranking herself. You’re comparing your inside with somebody’s outside. When you catch yourself at it, try a question instead. “How are you, really?”

    Dig deeper: comparison and steady self-worth

  7. You can own a mistake without drowning in it.

    Owning a mistake gets a lot easier once it doesn’t mean you’re worthless. Say what you did. Say what it did to the other person. Say what you’ll do now. Then stop. Beating yourself up afterward proves nothing about how sorry you are, it just pulls everyone’s attention back onto you.

    Jess forgot to pay her share of the broadband and it got cut off for the whole of the holiday weekend. On the Tuesday, after a weekend of the others tethering their laptops to their phones and one of them going to the library to finish an essay, she put her head around the living-room door. “I forgot to pay, and it left you all without internet for three days. I’ve paid it and set a reminder for the 28th.” Someone said “Cheers,” and that was more or less the end of it.

    If it went wrong in a conversation, Rewind the talk helps you find the words. Then decide whether to send them or let it rest.

  8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they’re treated, and adults never entirely stop. The words matter, because “You did a careless thing” leaves room to change, while “You’re so careless” shuts the door. Notice effort, and who someone is, as well as results, and people feel they matter on bad days too.

    Gill took over a team that hid its problems until week ten, and from her first month she started every project review with the same question, “What did we learn?” and refused, politely and repeatedly, to discuss who had got what wrong. Within six months people were bringing problems to her in week two, which saved everyone a great many Friday evenings, and the soppy question stayed.

    Today you could say one thing to someone that has nothing to do with how well they’ve done. Even just “I’m glad I know you.”

    Dig deeper: how worth is given and learned

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control how other people judge you, or what you were taught about your worth growing up. You can control how you speak to yourself now, whether you repair what you can, and where you spend your time. Give your energy to people who treat you as someone who matters. You got it wrong and you fixed it, which is what a decent colleague does. The voice is not convinced. Priyanka sends a thumbs-up, and by lunch you’ve mostly stopped thinking about it.

Two ways to read this

A mistake, read two ways

For some people a correction is just information: “slide four has a typo” means slide four has a typo. For others, the same sentence lands as a verdict on them, often because a lot of earlier corrections were. If you’re the first kind, it can help to say the plain part plainly and add one warm line. If you’re the second, it can help to ask, “Is this about the slide, or about me?” Usually it really is the slide.

Where the fields meet. The art of debating has a rule that fits this chapter well: separate the claim from the person. “I did a bad thing” is a claim about an act. Laughter, used gently, can help too, laughing with yourself and never at yourself. The research on laughter is young and modest, so treat that as a small help.

In everyday life

Shame turns up in very ordinary places. Between partners, “You forgot to call” can land as “You’re a terrible partner,” so what comes back is a defense instead of a sorry. A child who hears “You’re so naughty” learns something about who they are, while “That was a naughty thing to do” leaves a way back. A friend who says yes to everything may be quietly burning out, because no feels like risking being dropped. At work, someone may sit on a small error for weeks because owning it feels like proof they never belonged there.

On your own: a kinder voice to keep

You don’t need anyone else for this. Notice how you talk to yourself and practice one gentler sentence. Catch the critic’s favorite words (“always,” “useless,” “pathetic,” “should”) and swap one for a plain fact. “I’m hopeless” becomes “I was late twice this week.” On bad days, keep a short list of what you did, and let getting up count. You’re allowed to be disappointed in what you did and still be on your own side.

Feedback, criticism and put-downs are three different things.

Feedback is about something you did and what would work better, and it usually comes with a reason. Criticism aims at you as a person (“You always…,” “You never…”). It’s common in tired, stressed moments. In couples studied by Gottman (1994), criticism went with more unhappiness later, while a plain complaint about one specific thing did not. A put-down is something else again, and it’s worth being exact about it, because the label hurts if it’s wrong.

For something to be a put-down, these are usually all present:

  • it’s about who you are, not one thing you did,
  • it keeps happening over weeks or months,
  • it carries on after the thing is fixed or explained,
  • it often lands in front of other people or on what you care about most.

Not enough on its own:

  • one sharp comment on a bad day,
  • plain words without padding, or a family where everyone talks bluntly,
  • a fair complaint that came out badly,
  • teasing that both of you enjoy and either of you can stop.

If you raise it, describe what was said and how it landed, and leave the label out. “When you called me useless in front of your sister, I felt small. What was really bothering you?” gives the other person something to answer.

Common mix-ups

People worry that self-compassion is just self-pity. They feel quite different from the inside. Self-pity says “only me, poor me.” Self-compassion says “this is hard, and other people go through it too,” which joins you to other people instead of shutting you away from them. Others fear that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. The research leans the other way, because it’s fear of your own attack that makes people stop trying and hide their mistakes. And self-worth doesn’t mean thinking you’re great, only that you go on mattering on the days when you plainly aren’t.

If you want to help someone else with this

Talk about what happened, never about what sort of person they are. Arguing with someone’s shame (“You’re not useless!”) can feel like being told they’re wrong to feel it, so you might try “It sounds like you feel really low about it. I don’t see you that way.” Helping them find one small thing they can do gives the feeling somewhere to go. And kindness on ordinary days, when nothing has gone wrong, teaches worth far better than anything said in a crisis.

You could say “That sounds like it’s weighing on you. Want to work out one thing that would help?” “You don’t have to earn being cared about.”
