Part Two: Between us · Chapter III

Full tanks and different angles: stress and perspective

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why some reactions are bigger than what caused them, and why two people can see the same moment so differently.

In short

  • A full tank overflows, so stress left over from earlier makes small things feel big.
  • Check your tank in a minute, and say the band out loud so nobody has to guess.
  • Two people can see one moment from different angles, and both views can be true.
  • A full tank means later, so pause, name a time and come back.

Thursday, 5:15pm

“Did you remember to ring the dentist?”

A perfectly pleasant question. What comes out of you in reply is about four sizes too big for it, with a bit about the car thrown in for good measure, and you can hear it happening while it happens, like watching yourself trip on a stair.

  1. A full tank overflows.

    If you’re already tired or stretched from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction, which is simply human. Anything decided in that moment is worth coming back to once you’ve both calmed down.

    Eight in the morning. Wendy has just done a twelve-hour night shift and is standing in the kitchen in her coat. Her housemate, eating cornflakes, asks if she saw his message about the electricity meter. She bites his head off. Properly. Twelve hours on her feet did the biting. The meter just happened to be standing at the end of them. She apologised at four in the afternoon, when she woke up, via a note under his door.

    You could say “Sorry. That was bigger than the question. I’m running on empty.”

    Dig deeper: how full your tank is

  2. Some stress is left over from earlier.

    Ask yourself how much of this feeling is about the thing in front of you and how much you carried in with you. Being upset about the dishes and being exhausted, with the dishes as the thing you happened to notice, are different problems. Some of what we bring in is much older, like an understanding from growing up that whoever notices the mess has to clean it. If a feeling seems a lot older than the moment, Where your lens came from helps you find where it started.

    Corey is fourteen and has had a horrible day at school, the sort he won’t talk about. He comes home and finds his brother has taken the phone charger. Again. The explosion that follows can be heard two doors down. The charger was the last drop in a tank that had been full since lunchtime.

    Words you could use “I think I brought this in with me. Give me ten minutes.”

    Dig deeper: leftover stress

  3. Check your stress tank in a minute.

    How much are you carrying? measures how full your stress tank is, and a higher score means more stress in the tank. Score five things from 0 to 4, which are sleep, work elsewhere, any unsettled argument, how your body feels and time pressure today, then add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 is medium and 0.60 or more is high. If it’s been high for a long time it can start to feel normal, so a quick reading each week is worth it.

    Vince has been looking after his wife since her hip operation. He scored 0.65 three days running. That told him something he hadn’t let himself think, which was that he needed help more than he needed to try harder. He sat with it for an evening. Then he rang his son, who said, “Dad, I’ve been waiting for you to ask since March.”

    Dig deeper: the stress-tank score

  4. Three everyday states.

    Knowing whether you’re calm and connected, revved up or running on empty tells you whether now is a good time to talk. The Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check, helps you make a best guess. The labels come from polyvagal theory, whose main claims are still debated by scientists, and here they’re used only as everyday words. In one long-distance couple, he takes the video call at midnight his time and is usually running on empty by then, so they’ve moved the hard talks to Saturday mornings. It helps to know one body clue for each state. A tight jaw is a common one for revved up.

    Dig deeper: the three states

  5. There are seven angles on the same moment.

    The seven angles are tiredness, time pressure, history, what home was like growing up, your sense of fairness, how you communicate and stress from outside. Many arguments are two people looking from different angles, each sure the other sees what they see. The “See their side” step of Rewind the talk is built on these seven angles and helps you picture how the same moment might look from the other side, with questions to ask in place of guesses.

    Christmas, again. Sasha sees it from family habit. They always go to her mum’s. There’s a stocking with each child’s name on it, stitched in 2016, and her mum would be heartbroken. Kemi sees it from fairness. They went to her mum’s last year. And the year before. And, now that he counts, the year before that.

    Ways to say it “Which angle are you seeing this from?”

    Dig deeper: the seven angles

  6. Both views can be true.

    You can each be right about what you see and still be looking at different parts of the same thing, so the useful question is which angle each of you is seeing it from. Two brothers argued for a year about their late mum’s house. One saw thirty years of memories. Height marks on the kitchen door frame, the apple tree, the step where she sat to take her shoes off. The other saw a roof that needed £9,000 of work. Both of them were looking at the real house. In the end they sold it, and the one who saw the memories kept the door frame. He’s still not sure where to put it.

    Words to try “You’re right about what you see. Can I tell you what I see?”

    Dig deeper: two true views

  7. Say it instead of making each other guess.

    Before a hard conversation, each of you says how full your tank is and which angle you’re seeing from. It works best at the start.

    Two colleagues had a meeting about a missed deadline that both were dreading. One of them, feeling foolish, began with, “Before we start, I’m about half full today and I’m mostly seeing this from the time angle.” A pause. “Right,” said the other. “I’m pretty full. Fairness angle, if I’m honest.” It felt very odd. The meeting was shorter and calmer than the last one, and nobody cried in the toilets afterwards, which last time somebody had.

    You could say “Before we start, I’m about half full today, and I’m seeing this from the time angle.”

    Dig deeper: using both together

  8. A full tank means “later,” not “never.”

    A high score is a reason to pause and never a way out of the talk. Say when you’ll come back, and let the Calm-Down Kit help in the meantime. After your calming steps, check your tank again. Under 0.50, go back at the time you said. From 0.50 to 0.59, take another round if you still feel hot, and if you are still at 0.60 or more after two rounds, pick a new time and say when.

    Phone time. A dad and his fourteen-year-old, both shouting. “I’m too wound up,” he says, “I’ll talk to you at seven.” She slams her door. At seven he knocks. It matters more than anything he says once he’s inside, which isn’t much, and isn’t very good, and is a lot better than not knocking.

    Words you could use “I need a break. I’ll come back at seven. I promise.”

    Dig deeper: pausing without avoiding

Your part, and only your part. Your stress tank is the only one you can empty. You can’t calm someone else’s tank for them, and pouring yours into theirs leaves two empty tanks. Protect yours: rest, say “later,” and give energy where it’s met. When both tanks are full, the kindest thing you control is the timing. More in Part One: who you are today

Monday morning

The post. Eleven envelopes and a pizza menu, on the kitchen table, where they have been since Thursday. One of you sees a to-do list. The other sees proof that nobody in this house ever opens the post except them. You try saying your angles out loud, like the book says, and it helps for about a minute, and then the gas bill comes up.

The pile is still there at lunchtime.

Words this chapter uses

Your stress tank is how much stress you’re already carrying before this moment even starts. Leftover stress is the part of a feeling you brought in from somewhere else, like poor sleep or a hard day, while this-moment stress is caused by what is in front of you right now. An angle is the point of view you’re seeing a moment from, such as tiredness or fairness.

On your own: reading your own tank

On your own, the most useful habit is a one-minute reading of your tank before you react. Check it for a few mornings in a row and look for a pattern. Ask yourself before a hard moment how much of it is left over from earlier. Decide on a calm day what settles you, so it’s ready when you need it. Living alone after a breakup, Beth noticed she snapped at her sister on the phone every single Monday. Her morning readings showed why. Sunday night was her worst sleep of the week, every week, without fail. Now she asks for Tuesday instead, and her sister has stopped asking what’s wrong (see On my own).

Common mix-ups

A big reaction sometimes means it was a big deal. Quite often it means the tank was already full. A high score is never an excuse, either. It’s a note to come back later, never a pass to skip the talk. You can’t tell how full someone else’s tank is, however well you know them, which is why each person scores only themselves. And a pause isn’t the silent treatment, because a pause has a time to come back. That’s why you always say when.

Two homes: co-parenting after a split

Children carry two homes, and changeover day fills their tank. Being quiet, clingy or cross for a few hours after a handover is common and usually says nothing bad about the other home. Give them landing time, with a snack and something familiar and no questions about the other home for the first hour. The child is never the messenger. Adult news goes adult to adult. For money, follow any formal arrangement. Split the extras by a ratio you agree, and settle up once a month from one list. With an ex, share the band and keep the story, as in “I’m in a heavy week. Could we swap Wednesday?”, and your private life stays yours. If an ex won’t cooperate but isn’t dangerous, parallel parenting can work, with written messages only, short and business-like, handovers at school and your own list for your own home. When there are new partners, the parent stays the owner of anything involving the other home, and a step-parent starts as a friendly adult rather than the one who tells the children off.

Harriet gives Freya, who is eight, and Arlo, who is five, an hour of toast and blankets when they come back from their dad’s, and she has stopped asking “How was it?” at the front door. Sunday evenings are calmer. Arlo still cries at bedtime on changeover days, and she’s waiting to see whether that eases. Some weeks she thinks it is. Some weeks she isn’t sure.

Words you could use (a business-like message) “Hi Dylan. Freya’s school trip is on 6 November and costs £24. I’ve paid it and put it on the extras list. Thanks, Harriet.”

If only one of you wants to try this

You can do all of this from your own side. Check your own tank, name your own angle, pause and come back on time. The other person doesn’t have to use the words for it to help, and when one person starts saying it out loud, the other quite often follows. Lauren visits her dad every Sunday, and every week they clash about his health. She started opening each visit with, “I’m a bit tired today, so if I go quiet it isn’t you.” He never scored anything himself. He thinks the whole idea is daft. Still, the visits got calmer, and one Sunday in November he said, unprompted, while filling the kettle, “I’m a bit tired today and all, love.”

A note on safety: A full tank explains a reaction. It never excuses shouting, threats or hurting anyone. If someone’s “stress” often turns into fear for you, that isn’t a tank problem. These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If sharing how you feel ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.
