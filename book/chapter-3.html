Full tanks and different angles: stress and perspective

Paperback textRead the expanded version

Why some reactions are bigger than what caused them, and why two people can see the same moment so differently.

In the paperbacks: Book 4, Share the Load, Chapter 3.

At a glance

  • A full tank overflows, so stress left over from earlier makes small things feel big.
  • Check your tank in a minute, and say the band out loud so nobody has to guess.
  • Two people can see one moment from different angles, and both views can be true.
  • A full tank means later, so pause, name a time and come back.

Thursday, 5:15pm

“Did you remember to call the dentist?”

A perfectly pleasant question. What comes out of you in reply is about four sizes too big for it, with a bit about the car thrown in for good measure, and you can hear it happening while it happens, like watching yourself trip on a stair.

Thursday at 5:15, quickly. On the surface, a question about the dentist. Underneath, twelve hours on your feet. Underneath that, two people seeing one moment from different angles. The roots are state and setting, and signal and noise. The chapter slows down on the tank, then the angles.

  1. A full tank overflows.

    If you’re already tired or stretched from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction, which is simply human. Anything decided in that moment is worth coming back to once you’ve both calmed down.

    Stress from outside leaks in Daily diaries from newlywed couples showed that spouses under more stress outside the relationship reacted more strongly to everyday irritations inside it. Marnie’s morning is a fair example.

    Neff and Karney (2009) · Moderate: daily-diary studies of newlyweds; correlational

    It’s eight in the morning and Marnie has just done a twelve-hour night shift and is standing in the kitchen in her coat. Her roommate, eating cornflakes, asks if she saw his message about the electricity meter. She bites his head off, though really it was twelve hours on her feet that did the biting. The meter just happened to be standing at the end of them. She apologized at four in the afternoon, when she woke up, via a note under his door. The note said, “Sorry. That was bigger than the question. I’m running on empty.”

    Dig deeper: how full your tank is

  2. Some stress is left over from earlier.

    Ask yourself how much of this feeling is about the thing in front of you and how much you carried in with you. Being upset about the dishes and being exhausted, with the dishes as the thing you happened to notice, are different problems. Some of what we bring in is much older, like an understanding from growing up that whoever notices the mess has to clean it. If a feeling seems a lot older than the moment, Old rules, new choices helps you find where it started.

    Corey is fourteen and has had a horrible day at school, the sort he won’t talk about. He comes home and finds his brother has taken the phone charger, again. The explosion that follows can be heard two doors down. The charger was the last drop in a tank that had been full since lunchtime.

    Words you could use “I think I brought this in with me. Give me ten minutes.”

  3. Check your stress tank in a minute.

    How much are you carrying? measures how full your stress tank is, and a higher score means more stress in the tank. Score five things from 0 to 4, which are sleep, work elsewhere, any unsettled argument, how your body feels and time pressure today, then add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 is medium and 0.60 or more is high. If it’s been high for a long time it can start to feel normal, so a quick reading each week is worth it.

    Vince has been looking after his wife since her hip operation. He scored 0.65 three days in a row. That told him something he hadn’t let himself think, which was that he needed help more than he needed to try harder. He sat with it for an evening, in the chair by the window with the radio on low. Then he sat down with his son, who said, “Dad, I’ve been waiting for you to ask since March.”

    Dig deeper: the stress-tank score

  4. Three everyday states.

    Knowing whether you’re calm and connected, revved up or running on empty tells you whether now is a good time to talk. My weather today, a one-minute check, helps you make a best guess. The labels come from polyvagal theory, whose main claims are still debated by scientists, and here they’re used only as everyday words. In one long-distance couple, he takes the video call at midnight his time and is usually running on empty by then, so they’ve moved the hard talks to Saturday mornings. It helps to know one body clue for each state. A tight jaw is a common one for revved up.

    Dig deeper: the three states

  5. There are seven angles on the same moment.

    The seven angles are tiredness, time pressure, history, what home was like growing up, your sense of fairness, how you communicate and stress from outside. Many arguments are two people looking from different angles, each sure the other sees what they see. The “See their side” step of Rewind the talk is built on these seven angles and helps you picture how the same moment might look from the other side, with questions to ask in place of guesses.

    Naive realism Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described the quiet assumption that we see things as they really are, so anyone who disagrees must be uninformed or unreasonable. Saying your angle out loud is a small way of admitting you have one.

    Ross and Ward (1996) · Strong: a foundational account whose predictions are well replicated

    Christmas, again, and Sasha sees it from family habit, because they always go to her mom’s. There’s a stocking with each child’s name on it, stitched in 2016, and her mom would be heartbroken. Kemi sees it from fairness: they went to her mom’s last year, and the year before, and, now that he counts, the year before that.

    Dig deeper: the seven angles

  6. Both views can be true.

    You can each be right about what you see and still be looking at different parts of the same thing, so the useful question is which angle each of you is seeing it from. Two brothers argued for a year about their late mom’s house. One saw thirty years of memories. Height marks on the kitchen door frame, the apple tree, the step where she sat to take her shoes off. The other saw a roof that needed $12,000 of work. Both of them were looking at the real house. In the end they sold it, and the one who saw the memories took the door frame with the height marks on it, which is now leaning against a wall in his garage.

    Words to try “You’re right about what you see. Can I tell you what I see?”

    Dig deeper: two true views

  7. Say it instead of making each other guess.

    Before a hard conversation, each of you says how full your tank is and which angle you’re seeing from, ideally right at the start.

    Two colleagues had a meeting about a missed deadline that both were dreading. One of them, feeling foolish, began with, “Before we start, I’m about half full today and I’m mostly seeing this from the time angle.” After a pause the other said, “Right. I’m pretty full. Fairness angle, if I’m honest.” It felt very odd, like reading out your own weather forecast. The meeting was shorter and calmer than the last one, and nobody cried in the restroom afterward, which last time somebody had.

    Tidbit

    Say your number out loud. “I’m running high today.” Two seconds, and nobody has to guess.

  8. A full tank means “later,” not “never.”

    A high score is a reason to pause and never a way out of the talk. Say when you’ll come back, and let the Calm-down plan help in the meantime. After your calming steps, check your tank again. Under 0.50, go back at the time you said. From 0.50 to 0.59, take another round if you still feel hot, and if you are still at 0.60 or more after two rounds, pick a new time and say when.

    Flooding John Gottman’s long-term studies of couples gave a name to the point where someone is so physically overwhelmed in a conflict that listening and thinking become very hard. A full tank means later partly because a flooded person can’t really do now.

    Gottman (1994) · Moderate: large observational studies; some specific predictions are debated

    It’s about phone time, and a dad and his fourteen-year-old are both shouting. “I’m too wound up,” he says, “I’ll talk to you at seven.” She slams her door, and at seven he knocks. It matters more than anything he says once he’s inside. That isn’t much, or very good, and it is a lot better than not knocking.

Monday morning

The mail, eleven envelopes and a pizza menu, on the kitchen table, where they have been since Thursday. One of you sees a to-do list. The other sees proof that nobody in this house ever opens the mail except them. You try saying your angles out loud, like the book says, and it helps for about a minute. Then the gas bill comes up.

The pile is still there at lunchtime.

Words this chapter uses

Your stress tank is how much stress you’re already carrying before this moment even starts. Leftover stress is the part of a feeling you brought in from somewhere else, like poor sleep or a hard day, while this-moment stress is caused by what is in front of you right now. An angle is the point of view you’re seeing a moment from, such as tiredness or fairness.

On your own: reading your own tank

On your own, the most useful habit is a one-minute reading of your tank before you react. Check it for a few mornings in a row and look for a pattern. Ask yourself before a hard moment how much of it is left over from earlier. Decide on a calm day what settles you, so it’s ready when you need it. Living alone after a breakup, Beth noticed she snapped at her sister on the phone every single Monday. Her morning readings showed why. Sunday night was her worst sleep of the week, every week, without fail. Now she asks for Tuesday instead, and her sister has stopped asking what’s wrong (see On my own).

Two ways to read this

A full tank, two ways

Some people can say “I’m running high today” easily. Others only realize how full they were once they’ve snapped. If you can name it, the other person may genuinely not know their own number yet. Give them time. If you can’t, the one who names it isn’t making excuses in advance. They’re giving you a weather report so you can plan around it.

Where the fields meet. Neurobiology says your state sets the volume. Psychology adds that mood filters what we hear, and aromatherapy, read modestly, that the setting matters, a quieter room or a later hour. The evidence for particular oils is weak. The room and the timing are what help.

Common mix-ups

A big reaction sometimes means it was a big deal. Quite often it means the tank was already full. A high score is never an excuse, either. It’s a note to come back later, never a pass to skip the talk. You can’t tell how full someone else’s tank is, however well you know them, which is why each person scores only themselves. And a pause isn’t the silent treatment. The silent treatment is ignoring someone for days, again and again, to punish them, with no time to come back. A pause has a time to come back, which is why you always say when: “I need twenty minutes. Can we pick this up at eight?” If a pause of yours is being read as the other kind, describe it out loud rather than defending yourself.

Sometimes a reaction is far bigger than the moment, and part of it may belong to something older, from childhood or a past relationship or a frightening time. You can’t know someone’s history from outside, and guessing out loud (“This is about your ex”) rarely helps. Having lived through hard things is never the person’s fault, and nothing here is for diagnosing anyone. What you can change is your own half. Slow your pace, lower your voice, sit down, keep the doorway clear and use fewer words. If it’s still climbing, say when you’ll come back, step away, and then come back when you said you would. The same list works when the big reaction is yours.

Two homes: co-parenting after a split

Children carry two homes, and changeover day fills their tank. Being quiet, extra close or cranky for a few hours after a handover is common and usually says nothing bad about the other home. Give them landing time, with a snack and something familiar and no questions about the other home for the first hour. The child is never the messenger. Adult news goes adult to adult. For money, follow any formal arrangement. Split the extras by a ratio you agree, and settle up once a month from one list. With an ex, share the band and keep the story, as in “I’m in a heavy week. Could we swap Wednesday?” and your private life stays yours. If an ex won’t cooperate, parallel parenting can work, with written messages only, short and business-like, handovers at school and your own list for your own home. When there are new partners, the parent stays the owner of anything involving the other home, and a stepparent starts as a friendly adult rather than the one who scolds the children.

Harriet gives Freya, who is eight, and Arlo, who is five, an hour of toast and blankets when they come back from their dad’s, and she has stopped asking “How was it?” at the front door. Sunday evenings are calmer. Arlo still cries at bedtime on changeover days, and she’s waiting to see whether that eases. Some weeks she thinks it is. Some weeks she isn’t sure.

Words you could use (a business-like message) “Hi Dylan. Freya’s school trip is on November 6 and costs $30. I’ve paid it and put it on the extras list. Thanks, Harriet.”

If only one of you wants to try this

You can do all of this from your own side. Check your own tank, name your own angle, pause and come back on time. The other person doesn’t have to use the words for it to help, and when one person starts saying it out loud, the other quite often follows. Lauren visits her dad every Sunday, and every week they clash about his health. She started opening each visit with, “I’m a bit tired today, so if I go quiet it isn’t you.” He never scored anything himself. He thinks the whole idea is silly. Still, the visits got calmer, and one Sunday in November he said, unprompted, while filling the kettle, “I’m a bit tired today too, sweetheart.”

A full tank explains a reaction, and the reaction still needs a repair. “I was running on empty” is a real reason the first time. It stops working as a reason when it closes the conversation every time and nothing about the week changes. Ask yourself whether you’re using the tank to start a conversation or to end one.
