Both tools in this chapter are self-report tools. That means each person fills them in about themselves. Neither one measures anything automatically, and neither should ever be filled in by one person about the other. That would turn a reflection tool into a way of watching someone, which is exactly what this book is trying to avoid.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar III, Read your state first, with help from Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive. (Borrowed from neurobiology and psychology.)

  • In you: Know how full your stress tank is, and which angle you are seeing from, before you judge a moment.
  • Between you and others: Choose the timing, pause and come back. Your state shapes how the other person’s words land, so say it out loud, and ask which angle they are seeing from.

Autonomic Saturation

Two words, both plain once unpacked. Autonomic refers to the part of your nervous system that runs things without you deciding: heart rate, breathing (though you can also slow it on purpose), and how alert or braced your body feels. Saturation means how full something is, like a sponge that can’t take any more water.

So Autonomic Saturation just means: how full your stress tank already is before this conversation even starts. When it’s nearly full, a small drop makes it overflow. That’s why a pleasant question about the dentist can get a reaction much bigger than the question deserved.

A short version lives on the site as the Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check. The fuller version is the Battery & Stress Meter (WP-02), which measures how full your stress tank is. A higher score means more stress in the tank.

Leftover stress and this-moment stress

Any strong reaction is made of two parts:

  • This-moment stress: the part caused by the thing in front of you right now.
  • Leftover stress: the part you carried in from somewhere else, such as a poor night’s sleep, a hard day at work, a worry about money, or an argument that never got settled.

From the outside, the two look the same. From the inside, they need very different things. “I’m upset about the dishes” is a problem about dishes. “I’m exhausted, and the dishes are what I noticed” is a problem about exhaustion. If you treat the second one as the first, you can sort out the dishes perfectly and still feel just as bad.

The Buddha’s Second Arrow fits here. The first arrow is what happened. The second is the extra hurt we add to it: the story we tell ourselves, replayed. A full tank makes that second arrow easier to add, which is one more reason to notice what you brought in with you.

The three everyday states

The Nervous System Ladder describes three states that most people will recognize:

StateWhat it can feel likeWhat it’s good for
Calm and connectedEasy breathing. You can hear something hard without getting defensive.Hard conversations, planning, making agreements
Revved upQuick heartbeat, fast thoughts. Small things feel urgent or personal.Getting things done. Not a good time to settle a disagreement.
Running on emptyFlat, foggy or far away. Words are hard to find.Rest. Big decisions can wait.

These labels come from polyvagal theory (ventral vagal, sympathetic and dorsal vagal), whose main claims are still debated by scientists. Here they are only everyday words, not a medical model. You never need the terms. “Running on empty” just means everyday tiredness. Any self-check is a best guess, not a reading. Today’s Weather, a one-minute self-check, offers the same check in weather words: “clear” (calm), “gusty” (revved up) and “fogged in” (running on empty).

The stress-tank score, step by step

In the Battery & Stress Meter (WP-02), you score five things from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very true), thinking about the last day or two:

  1. Sleep debt: I’m less rested than usual.
  2. Workload elsewhere: work, study or caring for someone.
  3. Unresolved conflict: from any part of life.
  4. Physical state: hungry, unwell or in pain.
  5. Time pressure: today in particular.

Add the five scores and divide by 20. That gives a number from 0 to 1:

  • Under 0.30, low load: Whatever comes up is probably about the thing itself.
  • 0.30 up to 0.60, moderate: Say it out loud before a hard conversation: “Heads up, I’m carrying more than usual today.”
  • 0.60 or more, high: Be gentle with yourself, and put off anything that doesn’t need deciding in the next hour.

Example. Sam slept badly (3), had a heavy week at work (3), has a small unsettled disagreement with a friend (1), feels fine physically (0), and has a lot on today (4). That’s 11. 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55, the moderate band. Alex slept okay (1), has an ordinary work week (2), no unsettled conflict (0), feels fine (0), and has a fairly normal day (2). That’s 5. 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25, the low band.

So before they talk about the dentist, Sam says, “Heads up, I’m carrying more than usual today.” That one sentence can change how Alex hears everything that follows.

How it feeds the Chapter II check

The average of both people’s stress-tank scores is the stress input in Chapter II’s Solvency Read. In the example above, the average is (0.55 + 0.25) ÷ 2 = 0.40. It counts against the result, as 1 − 0.40 = 0.60, which is then weighted at 25%. A way of sharing the work that only holds up when everyone is rested isn’t one that can last.

When “full” has become normal

A tank that stays full for months can start to feel like how things are. “I’m fine” may then mean “I stopped noticing.” Minds get used to whatever stays the same, including a heavy load, so a reading each week, rather than only on a bad day, can show a baseline you have adapted to. It is a quiet form of complacency with yourself. When you share a reading, add a line of context (“this has been building since March”) so the other person can tell a bad day from a long stretch.

What a high reading asks of you

A high reading isn’t an excuse, and it isn’t a diagnosis. It’s a note to both people: whatever gets decided in the next ten minutes should probably be looked at again later, when the tank is less full.

It means “later,” never “never.” The Calm-Down Kit (WP-11) helps you step away well. Say how you feel, how long you need and when you’ll be back. Use your calming steps. Then check your stress tank again. The kit’s guide is simple: under 0.50, go back at the time you said. 0.50 or more, do another round. Still 0.50 or more after two rounds? That’s okay. Pick a new time, and say when. (The kit’s 0.50 sits a little below the high band on purpose, so you go back with some room to spare.)

The 7 Ocular Vectors

“Ocular” means “to do with the eye,” and a “vector” is a direction. Put together, the Ocular Vectors are the angles you might be looking at a moment from. Many arguments happen because two people are each looking at the same event from a different angle, and each assumes the other sees what they see.

Here are the seven, each with a question to ask yourself. The Perspective Shifter, a free page built on these seven angles, helps you picture how the same moment might look from the other side, with questions to ask instead of guesses. To learn your own usual angle, and someone else’s, Wavelength gives each of you a four-letter Wave Code you can compare.

  1. Physical tiredness. How rested am I, really, right now?
  2. Time pressure. Whose week is genuinely fuller? Am I seeing this through a lack of time?
  3. History. Has this exact friction come up before? Am I reacting to today, or to the last five times?
  4. Family script. What did “normal” look like in the home I grew up in? Who did this job there?
  5. Sense of fairness. Does the split feel fair to me, whatever the hours say?
  6. Communication style. Do I say things directly, or do I hint and expect the other person to pick it up? Some people say exactly what they mean. Others lean on tone and context. Neither is wrong, but they often miss each other.
  7. Outside stress. Is money, work, health or anything outside this relationship shaping how I see this?

Two true views of the same thing

In the old story of the Blind Men and the Elephant, each man touches one part of an elephant and describes it honestly. One says it’s like a pillar, another like a rope. They argue, because each thinks his part is the whole animal. In one old telling, a king who can see watches the whole elephant. Each man is right about his part and wrong about the whole, and there is still only one elephant. Putting your two views side by side is how you get closer to the king’s view.

The pile of mail in the Monday scene is the same. One person is looking from the angle of time pressure: “This is a to-do list, and I don’t have time.” The other is looking from the angle of history and fairness: “I’m always the one who opens it.” Both are right about what they see. Naming the angles turns an argument about who is right into a talk about two real concerns.

Using both together

Autonomic Saturation asks, how full is each person’s stress tank? The Ocular Vectors ask, what does each person think this is really about?

Used before a conversation, not during one, they replace two people guessing at each other’s inner state with two people simply saying it. A two-minute opening might sound like this:

“Before we start: my stress tank’s at about 0.55 today, so I’m carrying more than usual, and I think I’m mostly seeing this from the time-pressure angle.”

“Thanks. I’m at about 0.25. For me it’s more the history angle. This has come up a few times.”

Now both people know that one of them is stretched and the other is tired of a repeating pattern. The conversation that follows is about both of those things, not about who raised their voice.

A worked example: the dentist, replayed

Back to Thursday at 5:15pm. Alex asks whether Sam called the dentist. Sam snaps.

Without the tools: Alex hears the snap and thinks, “All I did was ask.” Sam feels guilty and defensive. The dentist, the snap and an old argument about who remembers things all get tangled together.

With the tools: Sam notices the reaction was too big and says, “Sorry, that was more than the question deserved. I’m carrying more than usual today.” Alex says, “Okay. Is now a bad time?” Sam says, “Can we talk at eight? I’ll have eaten by then.” At eight, Sam explains the angle: time pressure. The dentist call gets a named owner and a day. It takes five minutes.

Your part, and only your part

Your stress tank is the only one you can empty. You can’t calm someone else’s tank for them, and pouring yours into theirs leaves two empty tanks. Protect yours: rest, say “later,” and give energy where it’s met. When both tanks are full, the kindest thing you control is the timing.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

On your own: reading your own tank

A full tank makes small things feel big. On your own, the most useful habit is a one-minute reading of your battery before you react.

  • Check your battery for a few mornings in a row and look for a pattern.
  • Before a hard moment, ask yourself how much of this is leftover from earlier.
  • Decide on a calm day what settles you, so it’s ready when you need it.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, one person says, “You’re obviously stressed,” as a way of dismissing what the other said. Or someone uses a high score every time a hard subject comes up, so the subject never gets talked about.

When it goes right, each person names their own tank and their own angle. High scores lead to a named time to come back, and that time is kept. Over a few weeks, you both learn which angles you tend to see from, and you start to allow for them.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t this just making excuses for bad behavior?

No. A full tank explains a reaction. It doesn’t excuse it. A kind repair, such as “That was more than the question deserved,” is still part of the job.

What if my score is high every day?

Then the load itself may need to change, which is what Chapter II and One owner per job (WP-03) help with. If you feel run down most days for weeks, or often feel numb, far away or panicky, it’s also worth talking to a doctor or counselor.

Can I guess which angle the other person is using?

You can ask, gently: “Which angle are you seeing this from?” But let them answer. A guess about someone else’s angle often turns into a judgment of them.

Limits and cautions

  • The stress-tank score is a quick gut-check, not a medical test. It can’t tell you anything about your health.
  • Fill it in about yourself only. Never score the other person, and never use a score to win a point.
  • A high reading is a reason to pause, not a reason to end a conversation for good. Always name a time to come back.
  • The seven angles are a helpful list, not a complete one. If you’re seeing something from an angle that isn’t on the list, name it anyway.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own.
