Preface

Unbilled Debt

IN PLAIN TERMS

Some of the work of running a shared life never gets seen: remembering the appointment, noticing the milk is low, smoothing over the tense moment. It still costs the person doing it. This preface gives that unseen work a name and explains why writing it down changes the argument.

Do this: Read this first. It is short, and everything else rests on it.

Every household runs a set of books nobody agreed to keep.

Someone notices the milk is low. Someone remembers the dentist appointment three weeks out. Someone feels the temperature of the room change before an argument starts and quietly redirects it. None of this shows up on a calendar. None of it gets a thank-you, because thank-yous require the work to be visible first, and this work is built to be invisible — that's what makes it work.

Over months and years, that invisible work accumulates like a debt that only one party can see on their books. The other party isn't necessarily refusing to pay it. They usually don't know it's owed. That gap — between labor performed and labor acknowledged — is what this book calls unbilled debt.

This is not a book about keeping score to win an argument. A ledger that only one person gets to read isn't a ledger, it's a grudge with better formatting. The point of writing anything down is the opposite of scorekeeping: it's making the invisible column visible to both people, in the same units, at the same time, so the conversation can start from shared facts instead of competing impressions.

You'll notice this book borrows vocabulary from accounting, systems engineering, and nervous-system science. That's deliberate, not decorative. These fields are good at one specific thing: describing a system's actual state without moralizing about who's to blame for it. A ledger that's out of balance isn't evil. A nervous system in a stress response isn't broken. Both are just information — a starting point, not a verdict on either person's worth.

What follows is structured as a working ledger, not a lecture. Read the chapters, use the tools, and close the books together at the end of each week. The goal isn't a perfect 50/50 split measured to the decimal. It's a household where both people can see the whole picture, and neither one is quietly carrying debt the other doesn't know exists.
