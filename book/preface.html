Preface to Part Two · Between us

The work nobody sees: invisible labor at home

Paperback textRead the expanded version

Part Two of the book is about the people you share a life or a home with, and it opens here. Running a life takes a lot of work that nobody sees. This preface helps you make that work visible, to yourself and to anyone you share it with, so you can talk about it fairly and kindly.

At a glance

  • Running a home takes unseen work, the noticing, remembering and planning that many people call the mental load.
  • The other person usually just hasn’t seen it. This work hides by nature, and missing it says little about how much someone cares.
  • Each of you writes only your own side of one ordinary week, and you read the two lists together.
  • The list is for seeing. Thank each other for the past, and plan next week.

Sunday, 9pm

The laundry has been in the machine since Saturday morning and will need doing again, because it has that smell. The school trip form is due Tuesday. The birthday card for Aunt Jean is on the hall table, still in its cellophane, unsigned. Somebody in the house has noticed all three of these things, and it is probably you.

Kin work The anthropologist Micaela di Leonardo called the cards, calls, visits and presents that keep a wider family connected “kin work.” In the families she studied it was done mostly by women and treated as if it took no effort at all. The unsigned card on the hall table is a small piece of it.

di Leonardo (1987) · Moderate: an influential qualitative study

Somebody has also noticed that nobody else has noticed. From the hallway the house looks fine, which is exactly the trouble.

Quickly: a birthday card on the hall table is the surface. Underneath, the noticing that it needs signing. Underneath that, a whole list held in one head. The roots are balance and limited capacity. The chapters that follow slow down on each part.

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment before it turns into a fight. None of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you, and over months it piles up. This book calls the pile unbilled debt, because nobody ever sends a bill for it. (It’s a ledger word, and I make no apology for it. I spent my career as a government auditor, where a control is just an agreement about how a thing gets done, who owns it and how anyone would notice if it stopped. Who notices that the milk is low is a control in your home too, and if it only works because the same person quietly catches it every time, it was never really set up.) You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled.

    The second shift Arlie Hochschild’s interviews and home visits with working couples found mothers doing far more of the housework and childcare, a second shift on top of the paid one, and many couples telling themselves a story in which the split was fair. Large time-use surveys since show the gap persisting, and narrowing.

    Hochschild with Machung (1989) · Moderate: an influential qualitative study; its figures describe its own sample

    Marcus remembers every school form, every dentist appointment and every bit of lunch money for their two children. His husband, Theo, sees two children who always seem to have what they need, gym clothes included, and genuinely has no idea how. When Marcus went to Baltimore for three days in March, Theo found out. Both children went to school on dress-down day in uniform, and one of them went without a coat. Theo called Marcus on the second night and said, “How do you know?” He isn’t careless, and never was; the work had simply been out of his sight for eleven years.

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Four parts of the thinking Interviewing 35 couples, Allison Daminger split household thinking work into anticipating needs, identifying options, deciding and monitoring. Women in the study did more of it overall, especially the anticipating and the monitoring, deciding was shared more equally, and much of it went unseen by both partners.

    Daminger (2019) · Moderate: a rich interview study of mostly college-educated couples; not population figures

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check that it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, though nobody can see it. In a shared apartment above a laundromat, Yasmin noticed the internet deal ended in three weeks. She compared six plans on a spreadsheet, picked one, set it up, and checked the next morning that it had gone through. Paying took two minutes, while the thinking took most of a Thursday evening, and as far as she knows nobody else in the apartment has any idea it happened. When the internet kept working, one of them said, “Oh good, the Wi-Fi’s fine.”

    Pick one job at home and split it into its steps (noticing, planning, doing, checking), then ask who usually does each one.

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen, so the other person is rarely ignoring it. From where they stand, things look as though they take care of themselves. Edna watches her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own hospital appointments to fit, and her daughter thinks it all slots in easily. It doesn’t. Edna has canceled her eye clinic twice. A gentle question often opens it up (“What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?”), followed by listening without explaining.

    Sometimes it only becomes visible when the person who carried it has gone. Over his first winter alone, a widower found out how many jobs his wife had quietly held, from the birthday cards and the water heater service to knowing who to turn to when someone was ill. He didn’t see any of it until it stopped getting done, and the water heater broke in January. If that’s where you are, the section for people on their own, further on in this chapter, was written with you in mind.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time, so the talk starts from facts you share. This book calls that shared record a ledger, a plain list of who did what, kept for seeing.

    Two colleagues who job-share each listed a week of tasks. It turned out every single difficult client call had landed on Tamsin, every one of them, and nobody had decided that. It had just happened, the way these things do, because she was better at them. They left last year alone and agreed to take turns with Friday calls. A quick look at who does what is a gentle place to start. Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself honestly whether it’s to plan next week or to prove a point. If it’s the second, keep it in the drawer for now.

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did and then read the two lists together, which keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, a long week at work, children off school with colds, so you both understand what you read.

    Arran, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a rented house in Somerville. Each wrote only their own list, in their own handwriting, which in Dani’s case needed some translating. Reading them together at the kitchen table, Sol learned for the first time that Arran had called the landlord about the damp every single month since February. “Every month?” said Sol. “Every month,” said Arran. Dani said, “I thought it had just stopped.” It hadn’t; it had spread behind the dresser.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what is happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    Two brothers share their mom’s care, and one of them had been about to say “You never help” for about a year. What he said in the end was “There’s some work here neither of us has looked at properly.” His brother, braced for a fight, found he didn’t need to have one. If words like “ledger” or “debt” feel heavy, use your own. “Our list” and “the stuff nobody sees” do the same job.

    Dig deeper: why the book borrows these words

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool, and one week of notes is enough to start. Later come a 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying that day, and a monthly look-back that needs only a few lines a week. Precious is a nurse on nights and her partner works days, and they hardly see each other awake. They started with one week of notes stuck to the fridge with a magnet from Cape Cod, and changed nothing at all for a week. That was the whole plan. Your first step can be as small as picking a start day and setting one reminder.

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, families, roommates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, and the ideas fit anyone who shares a load. In a foster home, the caregiver keeps track of family visits and social worker calls, while a fifteen-year-old in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week, usually pasta, and both kinds of work deserve counting.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

Your part, and only your part. You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back toward your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. Friday, after work

“You didn’t have to do all that,” they say, and they mean it kindly.

Someone did, though. The list only ever lived in your head, so to them it looked as if it took care of itself, the way a house seems to clean itself if you’re never in it on a Saturday morning. You don’t say this. You say “It’s fine,” which it isn’t, quite.

The mental load: what the research says

Many people call the thinking side of home life the mental load, meaning the noticing and planning that come before a job gets done and the checking after. It includes knowing the baby needs the next size of clothes, and remembering the form and the party present. It is real work, even when it doesn’t look like any.

Studies of couples find some patterns, on average. In couples of a man and a woman, women tend to do more of the noticing and checking (Allison Daminger, 2019). After a first baby a gap often opens, and in one study of working couples, mothers took on most of the new childcare while keeping their paid hours (Jill Yavorsky and colleagues, 2015). Arlie Hochschild called the home work that starts after paid work ends “the second shift.”

There is a fair point on the other side too. Sometimes the parent who knows how things are done holds on to that, without meaning to, which researchers call gatekeeping. When a parent openly invites the other in and lets them do it their own way, the other parent tends to do more of the care (Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins, 1999; Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, 2008).

These are averages across many homes, and they say nothing certain about yours. In your home it may be the other way around, or already even, or shared between two men or two women, which is why the book asks you to look at your own week on paper instead of guessing.

Hollie and Gareth have a toddler and a baby, and Gareth works shifts. Their week’s minutes come out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked anyone to do. Their week is in Chapter II.

You could say “You do lots of the doing. It’s the remembering I’d love you to take some of, whole jobs, start to finish.” Or the other way around, “Show me once how you like it done, then let me do it my way.”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing it. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember (the appointments, the smoothing-over) and write them down for yourself. Count it without charging for it. The point is to see your real load so you can plan your energy. Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which just landed on you, and pick one you could hand back or do less often.

Nicola has the children every other week. Four years after the divorce, she sat down with a pen and found she was still buying the birthday presents for both sides of the family. His mom, his sister’s twins and his godson in Portland were all still on her list. She handed his side back in one friendly message, and braced herself. He replied with a thumbs-up and, to her surprise, “fair enough.” More for people living alone or after a breakup is on the On my own page.

One household, followed for a month

Three grown-up siblings share the care of their dad, who lives on his own in the house they grew up in. On paper the visits are split evenly, two days each. When each of them wrote a week of their own side, the picture changed. Ffion, the eldest, also held the prescriptions, the hospital letters and the calls to the doctor, none of which counted as a visit. Their brother was surprised and a bit defensive, and the first conversation ended early, with someone leaving to “check the car.” A month later they tried again. He took over the prescriptions completely, with the reminders on his own phone. Their sister still finds the hospital letters too much to face, so Ffion keeps those for now, and they’ve agreed to look again in the spring. Help by relationship has a page for many other kinds of shared life.

Two ways to read this

The last minute, two ways

Some people start a task as soon as it appears, and a form left for a month feels like a weight. Others find that interest and urgency switch the engine on, and a far-off deadline simply doesn’t. If you start early, the night-before person usually does care. Their brain starts late. If you’re the late starter, agreeing on an earlier “pretend deadline” together takes the worry off the other person without asking you to change how you work.

Where the fields meet. Finance calls this unbilled debt: every entry has two sides, and work nobody sees still goes in the ledger. Economics adds that attention is a budget, and business that work belonging to everyone belongs to no one.

Common mix-ups

“If it mattered, they’d have asked for help” misses how hidden this work is, often from the person doing it, until it’s written down. Nobody needs exactly 50/50, either. What you’re after is a split you can both see and both agree to. Please don’t start by going over the last ten years. Start with one ordinary week, and thank each other for the past without trying to settle it. And the one doing less usually isn’t the one who cares less. Usually they just can’t see it yet, and once you’ve both seen it, what happens next is a choice you make together.

To start, each of you can list the things you did this week that nobody saw, then swap lists and read the other one slowly. Say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed. Pick one item and thank them for it in a specific way. If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a coffee with the same questions works too. Fixing comes later. Who did what turns this into a gentle week-long log, and How to say a specific thank-you has more ideas.

Limits and cautions

A shared list and keeping tabs on someone can look alike on paper. A shared list is something you both chose. Each of you writes your own side, and either of you can stop. Keeping tabs is one person asking for the other’s notes, checking them, or using them to track where the other goes or what they spend. Some people just like a lot of detail, and that’s fine when it’s about their own side. If a list starts to feel like the second kind, set it aside and talk about how it’s being used before you go back to it.
