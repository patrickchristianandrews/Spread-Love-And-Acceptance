Preface to Part Two · Between us

The work nobody sees: invisible labour at home

Simple versionRead the full preface

Part Two of the book is about the people you share a life, a home or a job with, and it opens here. Running a life takes a lot of work that nobody sees. This preface helps you make that work visible, to yourself and to anyone you share it with, so you can talk about it fairly and kindly.

In short

  • Running a home takes unseen work, the noticing, remembering and planning that many people call the mental load.
  • The other person usually just hasn’t seen it. This work hides by nature, and missing it says little about how much someone cares.
  • Each of you writes only your own side of one ordinary week, and you read the two lists together.
  • The list is for seeing. Thank each other for the past, and plan next week.

Sunday, 9pm

Someone notices that the laundry is still in the washer, the school forms are due on Tuesday and the birthday card never got posted. They also notice that nobody else has noticed. From the hallway the house looks fine, and that is exactly the trouble.

Across town, a widower is finding out how many jobs his wife quietly carried, from the birthday cards and the boiler service to knowing who to ring when someone was ill. He never saw them until they stopped getting done.

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing that the milk is low and calming a tense moment never show up on a calendar, so they rarely get a thank-you, and over months they pile up. This book calls the pile unbilled debt, because nobody ever sends a bill for it. You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled. Marcus remembers every school form, dentist visit and bit of lunch money for their two children. His husband, Theo, sees two children who always seem to have what they need, because the work is out of his sight.

    Tonight you could write down three things you did today that nobody saw, and leave the list alone for now. If you show it to someone later, something like “I’m not asking you to pay anything back. I just want you to see them” sets the right tone.

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check that it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy even though nobody can see it. In a shared flat, Yasmin notices that the internet deal is ending, compares plans, picks one and checks it went through. Paying took two minutes, and the thinking took her most of an evening.

    Pick one job at home and split it into its steps, noticing, planning, doing and checking, and then ask who usually does each step.

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen, so the other person is rarely ignoring it. From where they stand, things appear to take care of themselves. Edna minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own doctor’s appointments to fit, while her daughter believes it all fits in easily. A gentle question often opens it up, such as “What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?”, followed by listening without explaining.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time, so that the talk starts from facts you share. This book calls that shared record a ledger, a plain list of who did what, kept for seeing. Two coworkers who share one job each list a week of tasks and find that every client call has landed on one of them. They leave last year alone and agree to swap the Friday calls.

    Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself whether it is to plan next week or to prove a point, and share it only if the answer is planning.

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did and then read the two lists together, which keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, such as a long week at work or children off school with colds, so that you both understand what you read. Arran, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a home, and each writes only their own list. Reading them together, Sol learns for the first time that Arran has been ringing the landlord every month.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what is happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here. Two brothers share their mum’s care, and one of them swaps “You never help” for “There’s some unseen work here we haven’t looked at,” which his brother finds much easier to hear. If words like “ledger” or “debt” feel heavy, use your own, since “our list” and “the stuff nobody sees” do the same job.

    Dig deeper: why the book borrows these words

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool, and one week of notes is enough to start. Later come a 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying that day, a weekly catch-up and a monthly look-back, which needs only a few lines a week. A nurse on nights and a partner on days hardly see each other awake, so they start with one week of notes on the fridge and change nothing yet. Your first step can be as small as picking a start day this week and setting one reminder on your phone.

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, families, roommates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, and the ideas fit anyone who shares a load. In a foster home, the carer tracks family visits, social worker calls and school meetings, while a teenager in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week, and both kinds of work deserve to be counted.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

Your part, and only your part. You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back towards your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. Friday, after work

“You didn’t have to do all that,” they say, and they mean it kindly. You think that someone did have to. The list only ever lived in your head, so to them it looked as if it took care of itself.

The mental load: what the research says

Many people call the thinking side of home life the mental load, meaning the noticing and planning that come before a job gets done and the checking after. It includes knowing the baby needs the next size of clothes, and remembering the form, the jab and the party present. It is real work, even when it doesn’t look like any.

Studies of couples find some patterns, on average. In couples of a man and a woman, women tend to do more of the noticing and checking (Allison Daminger, 2019). After a first baby a gap often opens, and in one study of working couples, mothers took on most of the new childcare while keeping their paid hours (Jill Yavorsky and colleagues, 2015). Arlie Hochschild called the home work that starts after paid work ends “the second shift.”

There is a fair point on the other side too. Sometimes the parent who knows how things are done holds on to that, without meaning to, which researchers call gatekeeping. When a parent openly invites the other in and lets them do it their own way, the other parent tends to do more of the care (Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins, 1999; Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, 2008).

These are averages across many homes, and they say nothing certain about yours. In your home it may be the other way round, or already even, or shared between two men or two women, which is why the book asks you to look at your own week on paper instead of guessing.

Hollie and Gareth have a toddler and a baby, and Gareth works shifts. Their week’s minutes come out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked anyone to do. Their week is in Chapter II.

You could say “You do lots of the doing. It’s the remembering I’d love you to take some of, whole jobs, start to finish.” Or the other way round, “Show me once how you like it done, then let me do it my way.”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing the unseen. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember, such as the appointments, the restocking and the smoothing-over, and write them down for yourself. Count it without charging for it, since the point is to see your real load so that you can plan your energy. Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which simply landed on you, and pick one you could hand back, drop or do less often. Nicola, divorced, has the children every other week, and on paper she sees that she still buys the presents for both sides of the family. She hands his side back in one friendly message. More for people living alone or after a breakup is on On my own.

One household, followed for a month

Three adult siblings share the care of their father, who lives alone in the house they grew up in. On paper the visits are split evenly, two days each. When each of them writes a week of their own side, the picture changes. The eldest, Ffion, also holds the prescriptions, the hospital letters and the calls to the GP, none of which counts as a visit. Their brother is surprised and a little defensive, and the first conversation ends early. A month later they try again, and he takes over the prescriptions completely, with the reminders on his own phone. Their sister still finds the hospital letters too much to face, so Ffion keeps them for now, and they agree to look again in the spring. The ideas in this chapter fit friendships, teams and shared houses in the same way, and Help by relationship has a page for many kinds of shared life.

Common mix-ups

  • “If it mattered, they would have asked for help.” Unseen work is often hidden from the person doing it too, until it is written down.
  • “It has to be exactly 50/50.” The goal is a split you can both see and both agree to.
  • “We should start by going over the last ten years.” Start with one ordinary week. You can thank someone for the past without trying to settle it.
  • “The one doing less doesn’t care.” Usually they just can’t see it yet. Once you have both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

To start, each of you can list the things you did this week that nobody saw, then swap lists, read the other one slowly and say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed before. Pick one item from their list and say thank you for it in a specific way. If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a cup of coffee with the same questions works too. Fixing comes later, once you have both seen the same picture. Who did what turns this into a gentle week-long log, and How to say a specific thank-you has more ideas.

Limits and cautions

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A list should never be demanded, checked over your shoulder, or used to keep track of where you go or what you spend. If that is happening, it isn’t a ledger. It’s control, and it’s not your fault. Signs to look out for. If you are a counsellor or group leader using this book with others, see For counsellors.
