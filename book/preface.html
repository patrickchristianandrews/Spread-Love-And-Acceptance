Part Two: Between us · Preface

The work nobody sees: invisible labor at home

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full preface

Running a shared home takes a lot of work that nobody sees. This book helps you make that work visible to both of you, so you can talk about it fairly and kindly.

Five Pillars: This preface mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load, with help from Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives. What are the Five Pillars?

Sunday, 9pm

Someone notices the laundry is still in the washer, the school forms are due on Tuesday, and the birthday card never got mailed. They also notice that nobody else has noticed. The house looks fine. That’s the problem.

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment: none of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you. Over months, it piles up. The book calls this pile unbilled debt. Nobody sends a bill for it. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past.

    For example: Marcus remembers every school form, dentist visit and bit of lunch money for their two kids. His husband, Leo, sees two kids who always seem to have what they need. The work is just out of sight.

    Words you could use: “Can I show you some of the things I keep track of? I’m not asking you to pay anything back. I just want you to see them.”

    Try this: Tonight, write down three things you did today that nobody saw. Don’t do anything with the list yet. Just notice.

    Dig deeper: the work that goes unseen

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, even though nobody can see it.

    For example: In a shared flat, Amira notices the internet deal is ending, compares plans, picks one and checks it went through. Paying took two minutes. The thinking took an evening.

    Words you could use: “Doing it is the quick part. Remembering it and planning it is the part I’d love some help with.”

    Try this: Pick one job at home and split it into four steps: notice, plan, do, check. Who usually does each step?

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen. So the other person isn’t ignoring it. From where they stand, it looks like things take care of themselves.

    For example: Rose minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own doctor visits to fit. Her daughter thinks it all “fits in easily.”

    Words you could use: “I don’t think you’ve been ignoring it. I think it’s been hard to see from where you are.”

    Try this: Ask one person, “What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?” Then just listen, without explaining.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time. Then the talk starts from facts you share, not from two different memories.

    The book calls this shared record a ledger: a plain list of who did what. It’s a way to see, not a bill.

    For example: Two coworkers sharing one job each list a week of tasks. Every client call landed on one person. They don’t argue about last year; they swap Friday calls.

    Words you could use: “I’m not trying to win anything. I’d like us both to be looking at the same list.”

    Try this: Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself: is this to plan next week, or to prove a point? Share it only if the answer is “plan.”

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did, then read the two lists together. That keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, like what the week was like, so you both understand rather than judge.

    For example: Kai, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a home. Each writes only their own list. Reading them together, Sol learns Kai has been calling the landlord every month.

    Words you could use: “I’ve written my side. Would you like to write yours, in your own words, when you’re ready?”

    Try this: Add one line of context to your own list, like “long week at work” or “the kids were ill.” It helps the other person understand, not judge.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what’s happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    For example: Two brothers share their mum’s care. Instead of “you never help,” one says, “There’s some unseen work here we haven’t looked at.” That’s much easier to hear.

    Words you could use: “Let’s look at how it’s set up, not at each other.”

    Try this: Swap one blaming phrase you use, like “you never,” for a plain one, like “this job keeps landing on me.”

    Dig deeper: why the book borrows these words

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool. One week of notes is enough to start. Later come a daily 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying today, a weekly catch-up and a monthly look-back. If you use the monthly look-back later, a few lines a week is all it needs.

    For example: A nurse on nights and a partner on days hardly see each other awake. They start with one week of notes on the fridge. No changes yet.

    Words you could use: “Could we just try one week of notes and see what we notice? We don’t have to change anything yet.”

    Try this: Pick a start day this week and set one reminder on your phone. That’s the whole first step.

    Dig deeper: how the book fits together

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, family, roommates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, but the ideas fit anyone who shares a load.

    For example: A foster carer tracks family visits, social worker calls and school meetings. A teenager in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week. Both count.

    Words you could use: “Whatever we are to each other, we share some work. Could we look at it together?”

    Try this: Name one shared load in your life that isn’t a couple: a family group chat, a shared flat, a team at work.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

Your part, and only your part. You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back toward your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. Friday, after work

“You didn’t have to do all that,” they say, and they mean it kindly. You think: someone did. The list only ever lived in your head, so to them it looked like it took care of itself.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Notice everything you carry, including the thinking and feeling work you have never counted. Notice, too, the work others do that you may have missed.
  • Between you and others: Put both lists on one shared, fair page, so nobody has to argue about whose work “counts.” Jobs nobody claims tend to drift to whoever notices first, and a specific thank-you helps keep things steady.

Words you could use: “I want to see what you carry, too, not just what I carry.” “Could we put both our weeks on one page?”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing the unseen. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember: the appointments, the restocking, the smoothing-over. Write them down for yourself, not as a bill.

  • Count it, don’t charge it. The point is to see your real load, so you can plan your energy.
  • Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which just landed on you.
  • Pick one job you could hand back, drop or do less often, and say so kindly.

For example: Dee, divorced, has the children every other week. On paper, she sees she still buys presents for both sides of the family. She hands his side back, kindly, in one message.

Words you could use: “I’ve noticed I’ve been doing this for both of us. From next month, could you take it on?” Or, to yourself: “That was real work, even if nobody saw it.”

Try this: Circle one job on your list that is yours by choice. Enjoy it. Then circle one that just landed on you, and decide what you’ll do about it. More for people living alone or after a breakup: On my own.

The fields behind this chapter

Christian, who wrote this book, worked as a government auditor and is a polymath: someone who studied thirteen fields deeply (psychology, philosophy, behavioral science, neurobiology, economics, business, finance, holistic therapies, aromatherapy, the art of debating, politics, music and the healing power of laughter). This chapter grows from 5 of them:

  • Finance (Pillar I): The idea of an unbilled hour: work that was done and logged, but never invoiced, so the client never sees it.
  • Economics (Pillar I): Attention is limited. Every reminder you hold in your head takes up some of it.
  • Psychology (Pillar I): We remember our own effort best.
  • Philosophy (Pillar I): Telling what we know from how things look. In Plato’s Cave, people mistake shadows for the real thing.
  • Business (Pillar V): Work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, and it lands on whoever notices first.

Two fields, one idea: Finance + Psychology. In double-entry bookkeeping, every amount is recorded in two places, and the two must agree. And we each remember our own effort best. Put together: each person keeps their own record, and the two records are read side by side.

Where this comes from: the polymath way

This book is built on a polymath’s habit: looking at very different fields and noticing that the same few roots keep turning up. Those roots are balance, signal and noise, feedback loops, limited capacity, ownership, quiet incentives, and state and setting. A ledger, a nervous system and a conversation look nothing alike, yet each one has a limit, each one can lose a message in the noise, and each one stays steady only when what goes out is matched by what comes back.

Christian saw those roots across thirteen fields. The four newest to the list are the art of debating, politics, music and the healing power of laughter. They shape the gentler parts of the book: how to disagree without a fight, how a small group decides fairly, how tone and tempo change what lands, and how a shared laugh can repair a hard moment.

In real life

  • Partners: One of you keeps track of birthdays, presents and thank-you notes for both families. The other has never had to think about it.
  • Family: One grown-up child handles a parent’s appointments and prescription refills each week. The others see a parent who is “doing fine.”
  • Roommates: One roommate always buys the toilet paper and the dish soap. Nobody asked them to. It just keeps happening.
  • Coworkers: One person books the meetings, takes the notes and follows up afterward. It isn’t in anyone’s job description.

Words you could use: “I didn’t know you did that. Thank you.” “Can we look at who does the remembering, not just the doing?”

More kinds of shared life

Unseen work turns up wherever people share something:

  • Two mums: one holds the baby’s dates and forms in her head; the other does lots of hands-on care.
  • More than two partners: a job nobody owns lands on whoever notices first. More than two
  • Friends: one friend always makes the plans and sends the “how are you?” Friends
  • Teens: walking the dog and minding a sister feels like a lot to them, and like “helping a bit” to adults. For teens
  • Grandparents and grown-up children: childcare comes with planning work, too. Grown-up children
  • Adult siblings: one sorts a late parent’s paperwork for months. Family rifts

Common mix-ups

  • “If it mattered, they would have asked for help.” Unseen work is often hidden from the person doing it, too, until it’s written down.
  • “Writing it down means I’m angry.” It means you want you both to see the same picture.
  • “It has to be exactly 50/50.” The goal is a split you can both see and both agree to, not a perfect half.
  • “Mental work doesn’t count, only chores do.” Remembering, planning and checking take real time and energy. Put them on the list.
  • “We should start by going over the last ten years.” Start with one ordinary week. You can thank someone for the past without trying to settle it.
  • “The one doing less doesn’t care.” Usually they just can’t see it yet. Nobody here is the villain. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

Words you could use: “I don’t think either of us is the bad guy here. We just haven’t seen the same picture yet.”

Try this: Each of you, on your own, list the things you did this week that nobody saw. Then swap lists.

Next, read the other list slowly and say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed before.

Then pick one item from their list and say thank you for it, in a specific way.

If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a cup of coffee with the same questions works too.

Keep it light. This is a first look, not a verdict. The fixing comes later in the book.

Limits and cautions

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A list should never be demanded, checked over your shoulder, or used to keep track of where you go or what you spend. If that is happening, it isn’t a ledger. It’s control, and it’s not your fault. Signs to look out for. If you are a counselor or group leader using this book with others, see For counselors.
