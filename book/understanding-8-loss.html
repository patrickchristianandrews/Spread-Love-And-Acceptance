Loss, grief and big life changes

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What grief is, why it comes in waves, how it shows up in the body, and how to carry a loss, or a big change, without having to “get over it.”

In short

  • Grief is love with nowhere to go. It hurts because the person, place or life mattered.
  • It comes in waves. A good week followed by a bad day is part of grieving, and it doesn’t send you back to the start.
  • You don’t have to let go. Many people keep a bond with the person they lost, and that is healthy.
  • Most people find a way through. Support helps, and some losses need more help than friends can give.

Saturday, 9:05am

Six months on, you are doing fine, or so you tell people. Then in the supermarket you see the cereal your dad always bought, the one with the faded blue box, and you have to leave your trolley in the aisle and go and sit in the car. Nobody around you knows why.

This chapter is for anyone who has lost someone or something, whether a person, a pet, a marriage, a friendship, their health, a home, a job, a country or a future they had pictured. It looks at how grief works and then at what can help. There is no right way to grieve, and nothing here is a timetable.

  1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone, and it isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted away or children leaving home can all bring real grief. Its size usually matches the size of the bond, whatever other people think the loss ought to mean. A man may be surprised by how hard he takes his dog’s death while his friends tell him it was only a dog, yet that dog walked beside him every morning for twelve years, through two jobs and a divorce.

    It can help to say a loss out loud to yourself, especially one you have never called grief. “I’m grieving the friendship” or “I’m grieving the life we planned” is a small sentence that often brings some relief, because it gives the feeling a name and a reason.

    Dig deeper: what grief is, and what counts

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages of grief, running from denial through anger, bargaining and depression to acceptance. They came from listening to people who were dying, and they were never meant as steps that everyone follows in order. Most people swing back and forth, spending some hours deep in the loss and some hours getting on with life, and that swing is healthy. A widow may feel guilty for enjoying lunch with her sister and then cry all evening, and both the lunch and the tears are part of grieving.

    Letting yourself have a small break from grief, such as a walk, a programme you like or a meal with a friend, takes nothing away from the person you lost. When a wave comes, it usually passes, even if it knocks you over first.

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, poor sleep, no appetite or extra appetite, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there has gone, and that learning takes time and uses energy. A teacher whose mother has died keeps losing her keys and forgetting meetings, and she starts to worry that something is wrong with her memory. It is grief fog, and it eases as the months pass, though nobody can tell her exactly when.

    While it lasts, protect a few basics, such as one regular meal, a set bedtime and a short walk outside. It is fine to tell people at work or at home, “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

    Dig deeper: the grieving body and brain

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain to anyone else. Someone may still be alive but changed, through dementia, addiction or illness, or gone without having died, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Other losses go unrecognised, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pregnancy loss or a friend you only ever knew online. A woman whose father has dementia still visits him every Sunday, but the dad who knew her is fading, and she is grieving someone who hasn’t died. Grief without a ceremony, or without anyone noticing, can feel very lonely.

    If a loss of yours had no ceremony, you can make a small one of your own by lighting a candle, writing a letter, planting something or visiting a place that mattered. You might also tell one person, “I’m grieving something people might not see as a loss. It is one for me.”

    Dig deeper: ambiguous and unrecognised grief

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying, and others by doing, whether that means fixing things, organising, working or building something. Neither way is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks another isn’t grieving when they are grieving differently, and culture, faith, age and how your mind works all shape the way it comes out. After their son’s death, one parent wants to talk about him every evening while the other spends every weekend in the garage restoring his old bike. Each feels the other is getting it wrong, and each is grieving the same boy.

    A question can bridge the gap. “What has helped you most, even a little?” asked of someone who shares your loss, and listened to without correcting, often shows that the two of you want the same thing by different roads.

    Dig deeper: grieving styles and families

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said that healthy grief meant moving on and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently. People often keep a bond with the person they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head and values they carry forward, so the relationship changes shape without having to end. A teenager wears her grandfather’s watch to every exam. She doesn’t believe in luck, and she would find it hard to explain why the watch matters, except that it feels as if he is in the room.

    You might choose one way of keeping a bond, such as cooking their recipe, telling a story about them at a family meal or keeping one of their habits. Some people find it helps to ask, in a hard moment, what the person they lost would have said.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retirement, moving country, a child starting school, coming out or getting well after a long illness can all bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts, an ending, a confusing time in between and a new beginning, and the middle part is both the hardest and the easiest to rush. A couple moves to a new city for a job they both wanted. Six months in, one of them feels low and then guilty for feeling low, because this was the plan. What they also lost, though, was a circle of friends, a favourite café and a whole way of life.

    You can be glad about a change and still miss what came before. For one change you are going through, it may help to write down what ended, what still feels unclear and one small thing that is new and good.

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that, over time, many people find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, even though they never forget. Being with people who let you talk, or let you stay quiet, helps. For some people, though, grief stays at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard.

    Doctors call this prolonged grief. It means a strong longing for the person, or being taken over by the loss, most days, still going many months after the death (6 to 12 months or more, depending on the guideline) and getting in the way of life. It has nothing to do with weakness, and there is specific help for it. A year after his wife died, a man still can’t go into their bedroom or see his friends, and his doctor refers him to a grief counsellor. Things begin to move slowly, starting with one evening a week at his brother’s.

    On a good day, write down two people you could text on a hard one, and one place you could call if those people aren’t enough. If it has been a long time and nothing is easing, it is reasonable to say, “I think I need more help.”

    Dig deeper: resilience, support and stuck grief

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control the loss, how long grief lasts, or how other people react to it. You can control small things: eating something, resting, telling one person how you are, and saying no to what you can’t manage right now. That is enough for a hard season. You sit in the car for ten minutes and let the wave come. Then you go back in, find your trolley where you left it and put the cereal in. At home you text your sister that you saw Dad’s cereal and cried in the car park. She replies straight away that the same thing happened to her with his aftershave in a chemist’s, and asks whether you can call tonight.

Losing a partner in later life

After a long marriage the loss is in everything, from the empty chair to the jobs that were always theirs, the paperwork done alone and the first birthday and anniversary without them. Do one small thing at a time, and let one drawer count as a day’s work. It is fine to ask someone to sit with you while you fill in the forms.

Your grown-up children have lost a parent while you have lost a partner, and you may grieve on different timetables. If they start checking on you every day, that comes from love and worry, not control. You can still say what helps: “I love that you ring. Could we make it two evenings a week, and I’ll ring you if I need you?”

In everyday life

After a miscarriage, one partner may want to talk while the other goes quiet, and each can end up worrying that the other doesn’t care. A child may laugh and play the day after a funeral and then cry at bedtime, and both belong to grief. A friend who stops calling after your diagnosis is often stuck for words more than cold, and colleagues who avoid the subject when someone returns after a death can leave that person feeling as if the loss has been erased. A whole community can grieve together too, when a town loses its library or its factory.

On your own: carrying it gently

Grief can be lonely even with people around you. When you are carrying it alone, it helps to give the day a small shape, with one meal, one walk and one person you message. Expect harder days around birthdays and anniversaries, and plan something gentle for them. Writing to the person you lost, or about what you lost, can help too, and nobody else ever needs to read it. Some days the only goal is to get to tonight, and that is allowed.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Show up, and keep showing up. Support often floods in for two weeks and then stops, so a text at three months, or on a birthday, can mean a great deal.
  • Offer something specific. “Can I drop off dinner on Thursday?” is easier to say yes to than “Let me know if you need anything.”
  • Say the name. Many grieving people find it a comfort to hear the name of the person they lost, and to hear a memory of them. If you are unsure, ask.
  • Leave out the explanations. “Everything happens for a reason” and “At least…” tend to hurt, while “I’m so sorry. I’m here” rarely does.
  • Comfort in, vent out. If you are upset too, lean on people further from the loss rather than on those closest to it.

Words you could use: “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.” “I was thinking about your mum today. I remember her laugh.” “I’m going to the shop. What can I bring you?”

When to get more help

Grief is not an illness. But it’s worth talking to a doctor or a grief counsellor if, after many months, the pain isn’t easing at all, you can’t manage daily life, you feel life has no meaning without the person, or you are drinking or using other things to get through. Many hospices, faith communities and charities offer free bereavement support, including for children.

If you are having thoughts of not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

If a loss involved violence, or you are grieving a relationship you left because you weren’t safe, Not safe at home? has free, private help, including after you separate.
