Loss, grief and big life changes

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What grief is, why it comes in waves, how it shows up in the body, and how to carry a loss, or a big change, without having to “get over it.”

At a glance

  • Grief is love with nowhere to go. It hurts because the person, place or life mattered.
  • It comes in waves. A good week followed by a bad day is part of grieving, and it doesn’t send you back to the start.
  • You don’t have to let go. Many people keep a bond with the person they lost, and that is healthy.
  • Most people find a way through. Support helps, and some losses need more help than friends can give.

Saturday, 9:05am

Six months on, you’re doing fine, or that’s what you tell people, and on most days it’s even true.

Then, in aisle seven, there’s the cereal, his cereal, the faded blue box he always said tasted of cardboard and ate every single morning for thirty years. You leave the cart where it is, next to the oatmeal, and walk out to the car and sit in it with the engine off. A woman loading her trunk two spaces down glances over and then, kindly, doesn’t.

This chapter is for anyone who has lost someone or something. It might be a person, or a pet, or a marriage or a friendship, their health or a home or a job, a country they had to leave or a future they’d pictured. It looks at how grief works and then at what can help. There’s no right way to grieve. Nothing here is a schedule, and if any of it doesn’t fit you, leave it.

Aisle seven, in quick strokes. On the surface, a box of cereal. Underneath, six months of being fine. Underneath that, love with nowhere to go. The roots are balance and state and setting, what was given and is now missing, and where the wave happens to land. The chapter goes through them slowly.

  1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone, and it isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted off, children leaving home. Its size usually matches the size of the bond, whatever anyone else thinks the loss ought to mean.

    When Bramble died, his owner was shaken by how hard he took it, and his friends at the bar told him, kindly enough, that it was only a dog. That dog walked beside him along the same stretch of river every morning for twelve years, through two jobs and a divorce. The lead is still on its hook by the back door, and he can’t move it yet.

    It can help to say a loss out loud to yourself, especially one you’ve never called grief. “I’m grieving the friendship.” “I’m grieving the life we planned.” They’re small sentences, and they can bring a surprising amount of relief.

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages of grief, running from denial through anger, bargaining and depression to acceptance. They came from listening to people who were dying, and they were never meant as steps that everyone goes through in order. Most people swing back and forth, some hours deep in the loss and some hours getting on with life, and researchers who study grief see that swing as healthy.

    Back and forth is normal The dual process model describes healthy grief as moving back and forth between facing the loss and getting on with life, with breaks from grief included. In this model Val’s laugh at lunch isn’t a lapse. It’s part of the grieving.

    Stroebe and Schut (1999) · Moderate: a widely used framework with partial support

    Three months after Gerald died, Val laughed properly at lunch. Her sister had told a story about a man at the bowls club and a wasp, and Val laughed till she had to put her fork down. On the bus home she felt terrible about it. That evening she cried for two hours. The lunch was grief too, and so was the bus.

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, bad sleep, no appetite or too much, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there has gone, and the learning takes time and uses a lot of energy.

    An assistant principal whose mother has just died loses her car keys three times in one week. She drives to school on a Saturday, and walks into a school board meeting that is happening on Thursday when it is in fact Tuesday. She starts to wonder, quietly, if something is wrong with her memory. It’s grief fog. It lifts as the months go on, though nobody can tell her when, and she’d really like somebody to.

    While it lasts, look after a few basics, like one real meal a day and a regular bedtime and a short walk outside, and it’s fine to tell people, “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain to anyone else. Someone may still be alive but changed by dementia or illness, or gone without having died, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Other losses go unrecognized, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pregnancy loss or a friend you only ever knew online.

    A loss without an ending Pauline Boss named this ambiguous loss: someone gone but still present in the mind, or present but changed, as with dementia. Part of what makes it so hard is that there’s no clear ending to grieve.

    Boss (1999) · Moderate: a widely used framework, backed by clinical and interview research

    Every Sunday at two, Teresa visits her dad at the nursing home. He’s always pleased to see her. He thinks she’s his sister, Connie, and asks after their mother, who died in 1987. Teresa has stopped correcting him, and they talk about Connie’s life, which Teresa now knows a surprising amount about. She is grieving someone who hasn’t died. There’s no funeral for that, no cards, and at work nobody thinks to ask.

    If a loss of yours had no ceremony, you can make a small one, a candle or a letter or a bulb in the yard, or just going to stand for a while somewhere that mattered.

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying. Others grieve by doing, fixing things, organizing, working, building. Neither is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks another isn’t grieving, when they’re grieving differently, and culture and faith and age and how your mind works all shape how it comes out.

    After their son died, Janet wanted to talk about him every evening. Steve went into the garage every weekend and worked on the old Raleigh that Ryan had never got around to fixing. She thought he was hiding. He thought she was making it worse. It took a bereavement counselor about forty minutes to point out, gently, that they were both doing exactly the same thing. In March Steve brought the bike out, finished. Janet cried and he let her. Then she asked him what was wrong with the gears, and he told her, for some time.

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said healthy grief meant moving on and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently. People often keep a bond with the person they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head and the values they carry on, so the relationship changes shape without having to end.

    Continuing bonds This is the book that challenged the cut-the-ties view of mourning, showing that inner bonds with someone who has died are common across many groups and often healthy. Later research adds a fair caveat: for some people the bond comforts, and for others it hurts.

    Klass, Silverman and Nickman (1996) · Moderate: influential; effects differ from person to person

    Ama wears her grandpa’s watch to every exam. It’s far too big. She has to push it up her arm to write. She doesn’t believe in luck, and if you asked her why she does it she would probably shrug and say “dunno,” but the honest answer is that it feels as if he’s in the room.

    You could pick one way of keeping a bond, cooking their recipe, or telling one of their stories at Christmas, or keeping one of their habits going. Some people find it helps, in a hard moment, to ask what the person would have said. Often it’s something rude.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retiring, moving country, a child starting school, coming out, getting well after a long illness. All of them can bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts, an ending, a confusing middle bit, and a new beginning. The middle is the hardest part and also the easiest to rush.

    Ifeoma and Olu moved to Denver for a job they both wanted. They toasted it with fizz in the empty apartment. Six months in, Olu felt low, and then guilty for feeling low, since this was the plan. What nobody had toasted was what they’d left behind, a circle of friends, a café where nobody had to ask what they wanted, and a whole Saturday that used to run itself. You can be glad about a change and still miss what came before.

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that over time many people find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, though they never forget. Being around people who let you talk, or let you stay quiet, helps. For some people, though, grief stays at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard.

    Doctors call this prolonged grief. It means a strong longing for the person, or being taken over by the loss, most days, still going many months after the death (6 to 12 months or more, depending on the guideline) and getting in the way of life. It has nothing to do with weakness, and there is specific help for it.

    A year after his wife died, Dennis still couldn’t go into their bedroom. He slept on the couch and had stopped answering his friends’ calls. His doctor referred him to a grief counselor and he went, mainly so his daughter would stop asking. Things moved, slowly, starting with one evening a week at his brother’s, watching the snooker and not saying much. He’s back in the bedroom now, on his own side of the bed.

    Dig deeper: resilience, support and stuck grief

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control the loss, how long grief lasts, or how other people react to it. You can control small things: eating something, resting, telling one person how you are, and saying no to what you can’t manage right now. That is enough for a hard season. Losing a partner in later life

After a long marriage the loss is in everything, in the empty chair and the jobs that were always theirs and the paperwork, done alone, and in the first birthday and the first anniversary without them. Do one small thing at a time, and let one drawer count as a day’s work. It’s fine to ask someone to sit with you while you fill out the forms.

Your grown-up children have lost a parent while you’ve lost a partner, and you may grieve on different clocks. If they start calling every day, that comes from love and worry. It isn’t control, and you can still say what helps. “I love that you call. Could we make it two evenings a week, and I’ll call you if I need you?”

Two ways to read this

Grief, read two ways

Some people grieve by talking, crying and remembering out loud. Others grieve by doing, mending, fixing the bike. If you talk, the doer may look as if they’ve moved on. Usually they haven’t, and the doing is the grief. If you do, the talker isn’t wallowing. Telling the story again is how they carry it.

Where the fields meet. Philosophy has asked for a long time what makes a life go well, and part of its answer is that the people we lose stay part of it. Laughter, oddly, belongs here too: a shared laugh can tell a grieving body it’s safe for a minute. The research on laughter is still young, so read that as a small comfort.

In everyday life

After a miscarriage one partner may want to talk while the other goes quiet, and each can end up worrying the other doesn’t care. A child may laugh and play the day after a funeral and cry at bedtime, and both are grief. A friend who stops calling after your diagnosis is usually stuck for words more than cold. Colleagues who avoid the subject when someone comes back after a death can leave that person feeling as if the loss has been rubbed out. And a whole town can grieve together, when it loses its library or its factory.

On your own: carrying it gently

Grief can be lonely even with people around you. When you’re carrying it on your own, give the day a small shape, with a meal and a walk in it and one person you message. Expect harder days around birthdays and anniversaries, and plan something gentle for them. Writing to the person you lost, or about what you lost, can help, and nobody ever needs to read it. Some days the only goal is to get to tonight, and that is enough of a goal.

If you want to help someone else with this

Show up, and then keep showing up. Support floods in for about two weeks and then stops, so a text at three months, or on a birthday, can mean a great deal. Offer something specific, because “Can I drop dinner round on Thursday?” is far easier to say yes to than “Let me know if you need anything.” Say the name, too. Many grieving people find it a comfort to hear it, and a memory with it, and if you’re not sure, ask. Leave out the explanations, since “Everything happens for a reason” and “At least…” tend to hurt. “I’m so sorry. I’m here” rarely does. And if you’re upset too, lean on people further from the loss, not the ones closest to it. Comfort goes in toward the middle and venting goes out.

You could say “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.” “I was thinking about your mom today. I remember her laugh.” “I’m going to the shop. What can I bring you?”
