About this book, and how to use it

This book starts with you. It looks at where your way of seeing things came from, how you are wired today, how feelings work, why your worth was never a score, how personality works and how minds can work differently.

It is for anyone who wants to understand themselves a little better, at their own pace. You don’t need a partner, a problem or a diagnosis to read it, and nothing in it is a test.

Read the chapters in any order. Each is a short version of a chapter that is free to read online, where the full version has more research, more examples and more to try. The code at the end of each chapter opens it on your phone.

Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

Sunday, 10:15am

Last night’s pan is soaking on the hob, there’s a tin of tomatoes open by the kettle, and one of the three mugs has grown something. Your partner is asleep. You haven’t had a single thought yet, not one, and already there’s a voice going of course it’s you, it’s always you.

Nobody said that, and nobody else is even awake yet.

Across town Ray is drying a plate that nobody will ever see. His wife died two years ago, and he still can’t sit down with the paper until the last fork is back in the drawer. His mother couldn’t either.

  1. You’re not one thing.

    Dot is seventy-one and her son is forty minutes late for Sunday lunch. When he finally comes in, cold air and apologies and a bunch of garage-forecourt carnations, she says, “Well, the beef’s ruined,” in a voice she will regret by pudding.

    Three things went into that sentence. She has liked things on time since she was a girl; that’s wiring, and it isn’t going anywhere. Her father treated lateness as a kind of insult, and she picked that up without ever deciding to. And she was awake at four listening to the wind rattle the side gate. A strong reaction is usually all three at once, and it helps to know which one is shouting loudest. This chapter is about the middle one, what life taught you, and Chapter 2 (page ) deals with the other two.

    Dot doesn’t say any of this. She says, “Sit down, then,” and gives him the end bit, which he likes, and the rest of lunch is all right.

    Dig deeper: the three layers

  2. Your lens came from somewhere.

    You learned what to expect from people long before you had words for it, at home mostly, but also at school and from whatever was on the telly at teatime. By the time you’re grown up the lens doesn’t feel like a lens any more, it feels like simply seeing what’s there.

    Birthdays give it away. Imogen grew up with a party every year, a sheet cake, and cousins driving down from Glasgow with a balloon tied to the wing mirror. Stuart got a card propped against the cornflakes and a slightly better tea. So when Stuart books a quiet table for two on her thirtieth, he thinks he has nailed it. She spends the starter trying not to look at the door.

    It took them two more birthdays to say it out loud. “In my family this was a big deal,” she said in the end. “What was it like in yours?” He thought about it. “Mum did a trifle.” Where your lens came from walks through the idea slowly, and you can read one section and stop.

  3. Each stage of growing up asks its own question.

    A small child is working out whether the world is safe. A bit older and the questions change to whether you’re any good at things, and where you fit, and whether you can be close to someone without disappearing into them, and later still grown-ups start to wonder whether any of it has meant anything. What sticks is the answer you landed on at each stop, because you can go on using it for decades without noticing you’re using it.

    Jago moved schools four times between eleven and sixteen. He’s forty-four now, a site manager, and on the first morning of every new contract he still stands by the portakabin feeling like the new kid with the wrong shoes. He only clocked why last year. I suspect most of us have one of those questions still running somewhere.

    Dig deeper: the stages

  4. When something happened matters.

    An experience goes deeper when it lands on the question you were busy with at the time. Moving house at four is mostly about a new bedroom. Moving at fourteen, in the thick of working out where you belong, can leave you feeling on the outside for years.

    Two sisters were six and fifteen when their parents split up. The younger one remembers glow-in-the-dark stars on her new ceiling and a different bus to school,, and remembers it fondly, which drives her sister up the wall. The older one remembers every other Friday, standing in the hall with a holdall, being torn in two. They are both right, which is why that year still comes up at Christmas (see Family rifts, spreadloveandacceptance.com/family-rifts). Deeper doesn’t mean permanent, mind. People go on learning all their lives.

  5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts.

    Plenty of adults carry a sentence about themselves that they picked up before they were old enough to check it. I’m only all right when I’m useful. My needs are a nuisance. If I get it wrong, I’m bad. A child can’t see what is going on for the grown-ups, so they go by how the room felt, and they draw the only conclusion a child can draw, which is usually about themselves.

    Sheila has fostered for eleven years and she’s very good at it. She also can’t ask for respite, the planned weekend when someone else has the children, not even in the week she put the cereal in the fridge and the milk in the cupboard. “They’ve got enough on,” she told her link worker, who had rung specifically to offer it.

    Looking again as an adult doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine back then. It means you’re allowed the full picture now. Try writing down one old belief about yourself, then underneath it what you’d say to a friend who believed the same thing about themselves.

    Dig deeper: old answers and fairer readings

  6. An old rule can read other people for you.

    If silence at home meant someone was angry, then a partner’s quiet evening can feel like weather coming in. And a rule nobody has ever said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only break it. If you loved me you’d just know. Whoever notices, does it. Each is somebody’s family rule, enforced on someone from a different family.

    Gemma’s housemate goes out at ten past seven without saying goodbye. Gemma, who grew up in a house where the door going without a word meant a row was coming, spends the whole morning at work going over what she might have done. The housemate was on the 7.14 to a shift she’d nearly slept through.

    If you catch yourself reading a silence, you can just ask. “You went off quick this morning. Everything all right?” Nine times out of ten it’s a train.

    Write down the rulebook from home.

    If you only do one exercise in this book, I’d make it this one. Finish these six lines the way the home you grew up in would have finished them.

    “In my family, love looked like…” “When someone was upset, we…” “The jobs were split like this…” “Resting was okay when…” “Asking for help meant…” “The unforgivable thing was…”

    The last one is usually the interesting one. The rulebook page has room to write them, and nothing is saved anywhere except on your own device.

    Amira and Daniel did it one December, after Daniel’s mum had asked about Christmas again and Amira had gone very quiet. Somewhere on each of their pages, in different words, was the same rule. Holidays belong to my family. It explained four years of tug of war in one go, and they laughed, a bit, before the actual negotiating started (see Two faiths, one family, spreadloveandacceptance.com/two-faiths). One line today is plenty. If you show it to someone, you might say, “This is a rule I grew up with. I’m not saying it’s right. I just want you to know it’s there.”

  8. You get to choose which rules to keep.

    Go back over your lines and ask of each one whether you still want it. Some you’ll keep. Some you’ll look at and think, well, that one can go.

    When a reaction feels too big for the moment, wait till you’ve settled and give it a minute’s questioning. Where did I learn this? How old does it feel? Is it true here, with this person, today?

    Bev runs a team at a building society and realised, eventually, that she hid problems from her manager until they’re too big to hide, because at home a mistake meant a mood that lasted days. She wrote a new rule on a yellow sticky note and stuck it to the bottom of her monitor. Mistakes are information. We fix them together. Three years on it has gone curly, and she won’t take it down.

    Pick one of your rules and write the version you’d rather live by. “I used to think ___. Now I’m choosing ___.” Later, if you like, swap rulebooks with someone you trust and pick some shared rules on purpose.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, and choosing together

  9. What you carry forward is yours.

    You didn’t choose the home you grew up in, or what anyone else did, or what you concluded at seven about how things work. What’s in your hands now is smaller than that and a good deal sturdier. You decide which rules you keep. You decide how you talk to yourself about them, and where your energy goes, and that includes people. Give your care where it’s met with care. Where it’s all one way, let it shrink back to something that feels fair, so you have some left.

    Marcia has organised every meet-up with an old school friend for nine years, and the friend always comes, always seems pleased, and has never once in all that time suggested a date. Last spring Marcia stopped chasing. She sent one message (“I’d love to see you, let me know when works”) and then nothing, which was harder than it sounds. The friend didn’t reply for five weeks, and in those five weeks Marcia went to a quiz, twice, with people who did reply (see Always the one reaching out?).

    Is there somewhere you give far more than comes back? You could give a little less there this month and see what happens. (If you are afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has people to talk to.)

Tuesday, half past eleven

Two people get the same text from the same friend. Can we talk tonight? Fern is pleased, because she likes a plan. Callum feels his stomach go, because in his house “we need to talk” never once meant anything good, and he spends his lunch hour sitting in the car rehearsing answers to accusations nobody has made.

The friend wanted to know if they were still up for splitting a cottage in Pembrokeshire in August. Callum said yes too quickly and then had to ring back about the dates.

You, in all your relationships

Your old rules come with you everywhere, starting with the voice you use on yourself after a mistake. At home they decide who notices, who plans and what a quiet evening means. Two housemates can share a fridge for a year before finding out they’ve been living by different rulebooks about mess. In families, the old roles switch back on before you’ve got your coat off (the helper, the peacemaker, the one who makes it funny). At work, someone praised mainly for being useful says yes to everything and then quietly seethes, and seeing the rule is often enough to buy them a pause before the next yes. After a breakup old rules can get louder, which makes it a good time to look at them kindly (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own). How you connect is a two-minute quiz on the talking rules you brought with you. Help by relationship lists pages for many more situations.

Common mix-ups

The worry people bring to this chapter most often is that looking back means blaming their family. It doesn’t have to. Many of the adults who raised us had lenses of their own, and if that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit. Then there’s the fear that anything which started in childhood is fixed for good. Many old patterns soften once you can see them and feel safe enough to try something else, and some leave a lasting sensitivity that you learn to carry and explain. Not every big reaction is an old wound, either. Quite often it’s just a full tank (Book 4, Chapter 3, page 53, helps you tell leftover stress from this-moment stress). And some people decide there’s no point looking at their own rules if the other person won’t look at theirs. I don’t agree with that one at all. Knowing your own rules changes how you react whether or not anyone else reads a word of this.

A note on care: This part looks back at growing up. It is general education, not a diagnosis or therapy, and it can’t tell you what anyone has. Not every home felt calm or kind, so take only what is useful. If looking back feels heavy, you can stop and come back another day, or explore it with a qualified professional, if and when you want that. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared.

Your friend pauses the film. “You all right? You’ve been quiet all day.”

“Fine.”

Which isn’t a lie, exactly. You genuinely don’t know if you’re fine, or tired, or still chewing on something somebody said on Thursday. You couldn’t explain it to yourself, never mind to her. You reach for the remote and she lets you.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part, your temperament and pace and senses and the way words go in, and you work with that, by building a life that suits it. Today’s conditions are the passing part (sleep, hunger, the hour) and you work around those, usually by eating something or sleeping on it. Most of the frustration here comes from mixing the two up. (Chapter 1, page , covers the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    Dean works nights at a distribution centre. For months the school-run chatter at ten past eight was unbearable to him, all of it, the who-sat-next-to-who and the who-said-what in PE, and he started quietly wondering whether he was any good at being a dad. Then his partner swapped with him, so he does the half-three pick-up now, after he’s slept, and the same chatter is funny again (see Different hours, spreadloveandacceptance.com/different-hours). His daughter told her teacher that her dad has “got nicer”, which he found out about at parents’ evening.

    Write down one thing that always bothers you, which is probably wiring, and then one that only bothers you when you’re tired.

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Everything you hear goes through your own state on the way in. Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 53) looks closely at the stress tank in a shared home. This chapter only adds the self side, a one-minute reading on your own before you decide what a hard moment means, and for that My weather today is the quick check and How much are you carrying? the fuller one, on a short worksheet.

    A couple living five hours apart worked out, eventually, that every bad call they’d had came at the end of her late shift, around midnight her time. Every single one. They moved anything serious to Saturday mornings, with coffee, and found they mostly agreed with each other (see Long distance, spreadloveandacceptance.com/long-distance).

    Words you could use “I’m running on empty tonight. Can we pick this up tomorrow morning?”

    One state, many words. The chapters and tools use a few everyday pictures for one thing, how much room you have today.

    • The three states (Book 2, Chapter 1, page 9, calls them the ladder) are calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty.
    • My weather today uses sky words for the same three. Clear is calm and connected, gusty is revved up, and fogged in is running on empty. Its pressure is how much you were already carrying.
    • The stress tank is the score from How much are you carrying?, from 0 to 1, where higher means fuller.
    • The battery is the other side of the tank, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery.

    “Gusty”, “revved up”, “a fairly full tank” and “a low battery” can all describe the same Tuesday afternoon. I’d pick one set and stick with it.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear says watch out. Anger says this seems unfair, or this is in my way. Sadness says something that mattered has gone.     Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down. In brain-scan research by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), simply putting a name to a feeling (“I feel scared”) turned down the brain’s alarm response. A name gives a big, cloudy rush some edges, so you can see it and talk about it and decide what to do.

    Two housemates spent a whole evening passing each other in the hall without a word. Nobody had done anything, as far as either of them knew. At about ten, one of them stopped on the stairs. “I think I’m embarrassed about the noise complaint,” she said, “and I’ve been taking it out on you by being weird.” The other one, who had spent two hours going back over everything she’d said that week, laughed so hard she had to sit down. Then she put the kettle on.

    Once a day, name one feeling, out loud or on paper. A guess like “something like sad” counts.

  4. More words, better tools.

    “Bad” is a blunt instrument. Disappointed is different from left out, and both are different from overwhelmed, or ashamed, and each one needs something different. A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need a fancy vocabulary. A number does the job. So does a colour.

    Stephen told his partner he felt bad about his mum and they went round the houses for half an hour. Bad how? Bad about what? Then, out of nowhere, staring at the fridge: “I feel guilty I haven’t been up since Easter. And I’m scared she’s getting worse.” Within ten minutes they were looking at trains to Carlisle, and the cheapest one turned out to leave at 6.12 in the morning, which led to a whole different conversation about who was going to drive them to the station.

    Words you could use “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a grey day.”

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the one you felt first. Anger very often sits on top of hurt or fear, because anger is a stronger place to stand. And we have feelings about our feelings (ashamed of being sad, cross about being scared) and that second layer can sting more than the first.

    Half-term has been cancelled again, for the second time. Brenda’s daughter rings to say so, with a lot of reasons, all of them good ones, and Brenda says, “Fine. Well. Another time,” in a voice like a closing door. Under that is hurt, and under the hurt, if she let herself look, there is a quieter worry that she isn’t needed much any more by anyone, which is the one she has spent the last two years carefully not looking at. She doesn’t look. She puts the phone down and sits next to it for a while with her coat still on.

    Next time you’re angry, you might ask whether there’s hurt or worry underneath. You don’t have to tell anyone what you find.

    Dig deeper: first feelings and feelings about feelings

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise and peak and settle, as long as you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are something else. A mood is more like weather, a background feeling that hangs about for hours or days and colours everything without announcing itself.

    By eleven o’clock on a Saturday morning Rachel had decided her whole life was going nowhere. In fairness it had been a morning. The boiler, a letter from the council, and a dream about her ex in which he was, for some reason, a dentist. By seven that evening, after a walk along the canal and an hour on the phone to her sister, the same life looked fairly ordinary and mostly all right, with an actual dentist on Tuesday and a library book she wanted to finish. Nothing in her life changed between eleven and seven.

    So if you can, leave big decisions until the wave has gone past. Write it down. Look at it tomorrow.

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We catch each other’s feelings from a tight voice, a sigh, a door shut slightly too hard, usually without noticing we’ve caught anything. It’s part of how people bond. It also means stress can go round a house or a team like a cold in February.

    Bryony came into the Monday meeting late and rattled, and within ten minutes six people who had been perfectly fine at nine o’clock felt behind on everything. The following Monday she stood outside the door for two slow breaths before going in. The meeting finished on time. In that office this was regarded as a minor miracle and nobody could work out why.

    You can care about someone’s feeling without catching the whole of it. Good moods are catching too, and Stay close has small ideas for passing one on. Notice who you tend to catch things from, and give yourself a minute before you next talk to them.

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Lots of people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private. “Don’t make a fuss.” “Big boys don’t cry.” “We don’t talk about that.” It’s especially common among men and older people, and in some cultures keeping your feelings to yourself is a sign of respect and strength.

    It isn’t a flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every day by doing things, fixing the tap or driving someone to A&E at two in the morning and waiting in the car park till it’s over. They turn up. Caring by doing is real caring. Words can be added slowly, a few at a time, if you want them.

    Len was a builder for forty years and his wife died in the spring. He has never in his life said how he feels about anything, apart from Newcastle United. On the touchline at his grandson’s match, during a long dull bit of the first half, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this. But I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder and leaves it there, and neither of them says anything else for the rest of the match, and they watch the second half, which is also dull, and it’s one of the better afternoons either of them has had all year.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    If one of those fits, you could say it once this week, to one person, or just out loud in the car. A text counts.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually arrives with an urge attached, to shout or leave or hide or send the text you’ll regret by teatime. You don’t choose the feeling, but you get rather more say over the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to bring it back bigger, and acting on every urge hurts people, so the middle way is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    A crash from the garden. Through the kitchen window, Imran’s son is standing very still beside the greenhouse with a football, and there is a lot less greenhouse than there was. Imran feels the heat go up his neck. He opens the back door, says, “I’m too cross to talk about this. Ten minutes,” shuts it again, and goes to find the dustpan. By the time he comes out his son has picked up most of the big bits, very carefully, in gardening gloves two sizes too large.

In everyday life

If you live on your own, nobody is going to notice how you are, so a one-word check-in with yourself each day matters more (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own). With a partner, “What are you feeling?” can sound like an exam. “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is much easier to answer. Children learn feeling words from the grown-ups near them, and hearing you say “I’m frustrated, so I’m going to take a breath” teaches more than any lesson. At work, “I’m a bit flat today and I’m not upset with anyone” can stop a whole team guessing. And if someone close to you shows care by doing, like clearing the drive on your bad day, you can thank them for it in words. They may not know what to do with it the first time.

Common mix-ups

Some feelings hurt and some actions do harm, but a feeling on its own isn’t bad. It’s information. Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid, either, so treat a feeling as something to check before you obey it. People also expect to know straight away what they feel, and plenty can’t, some of them most days, which is why body words and numbers count as answers here. The most stubborn mix-up is the idea that a face tells you what someone feels. Faces are a poor guide. People show the same feeling in very different ways, and asking is kinder than guessing and a lot more accurate.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can land like a label. “You seem upset, am I reading that right?” leaves them room to put you straight. Then help the feeling make sense (“Of course you’re upset, that was a lot”), which does more than advice and doesn’t mean you agree with everything they did. Ask whether they want ideas or just company. A lot of people only want the second and are relieved to be asked. Take other ways of saying it, a number or a text or “I’m at a seven”. And settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go in, or they’ll end up looking after you.

You could say “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”

When to get more help

Everyone has hard days and big feelings. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or another trained professional if:

  • a low, numb, anxious or angry feeling stays most days for two weeks or more;
  • feelings are stopping you from sleeping, eating, working or seeing people;
  • you are using alcohol, drugs or other things to stop feeling;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that’s happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. You deserve help today.

If you feel afraid of someone you live with or are close to, that fear is an important signal. It is not something to “manage” on your own. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help.

Thursday, 11:20pm

You sent the wrong spreadsheet to the whole team. Last quarter’s, not this one’s. Priyanka spotted it in four minutes, sent the right one round and added “no worries!!” with two exclamation marks, which somehow made it worse.

It’s nearly midnight. The file stopped being the point some time around ten. The voice has moved on to bigger things now, like how you’ve always been a bit useless, really, and now they all know.

This chapter is about how self-worth and shame work, and then about what you can actually do with them. None of it is a diagnosis. Take what helps.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    A lot of us carry a quiet scoreboard, on which we’re worth something when we’re useful, or liked, or slim, or busy, or top of the class, or never any bother to anybody. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth”, and the trouble with a score is that it can drop. If your worth goes up and down with each day’s results, then every bad day is a verdict.

    Pam taught Year 4 for forty years. In the September after she retired she found herself in the supermarket at ten on a Tuesday morning, holding a basket with nothing in it, feeling lost in a way she couldn’t have explained to anyone. Nothing was wrong. But for four decades being needed was how she knew she mattered, and the bell had stopped ringing.

    Try finishing “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times, then look at what you wrote. Those are your scores. Just noticing them is enough for now.

    Dig deeper: worth that doesn’t have to be earned

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points somewhere useful. Say sorry. Put it right. Shame is about who you are, which feels impossible to fix, and so it makes people hide, or freeze, or blame someone else, or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is the one more likely to lead to repair.

    Rhys is twelve and he has just knocked his mum’s favourite mug off the side, the one with the hare on it that she bought on the seafront in Whitby years ago and drinks her first tea of the day from, and there are two ways this can go. Guilt says go and tell her, and maybe offer some birthday money. Shame says you ruin everything, and this time shame wins. He wraps the pieces in yesterday’s paper and pushes them right down to the bottom of the bin, under the potato peelings, where his mum finds them on Sunday.

    It was the hiding that upset her. She told him so. It took him a while to see what she meant.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    You feel shame in the body before you think it. Hot face, eyes down, stomach dropping, a strong wish for the floor to open. It also comes out sideways, as sudden anger or a closed, cold face, which is how a small comment turns into a big row.

    “Didn’t we already cover this?” It was a mild question, asked in a Tuesday meeting by a man who genuinely couldn’t remember. Becky snapped at him, properly, in front of nine people. That evening, scraping a plate into the bin, she worked out that it had nothing to do with him. She’d felt stupid in front of everyone, and stupid had come out as sharp.

    Learn your own signs, if you can. Next time you suddenly want to vanish, or bite, you can name it quietly to yourself, and now and then out loud. “Sorry, I think I felt embarrassed and it came out sharp. Can I try that again?”

    Dig deeper: how shame works in the body

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh inner voice usually started as protection. If you criticise yourself first, perhaps nobody else gets the chance. If you’re hard enough on yourself, perhaps you’ll never fail again. It meant well, in its way. But a voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, and it’s exhausting to share a head with.

    Every time Craig forgets something for the baby he calls himself an idiot. Out loud, sometimes. The wipes. The rabbit she won’t sleep without. It’s the word his own dad used about him, all through his childhood. Patting his pockets in the nursery car park for a rabbit that’s on the kitchen table, he hears it in his dad’s voice, exactly, and it strikes him as odd that he’s still using it.

    Write down one thing your critic says a lot. Would you say it to a friend you loved? Some people find it helps to answer the critic directly. “Thanks. I know you’re trying to protect me. I can take it from here.”

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you’d treat a good friend who’s having a rotten time. One well-known model has three parts. Kindness instead of attack. Remembering that everyone struggles. Noticing the pain without drowning in it. Studies, such as one by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012), suggest that people who are kind to themselves after a failure are often more likely to try to improve.

    Lindy failed her driving test for the third time, on the reverse round a corner again, clipping the same kerb she had clipped in March, and afterwards she sat in the test centre car park for a while with the engine off. Then she told herself what she’d have told her sister, more or less. This is hard. Loads of people need more goes. You’re fine everywhere except that one corner. On Sunday morning she was on the empty industrial estate by the ring road with her dad in the passenger seat, reversing round the same lamp post eleven times.

    If it feels all right, try putting a hand on your arm once today and saying, slowly, “This is a hard moment. Other people feel this too. What do I need?” It will feel daft the first time, and probably the second.

  6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

    Everyone measures themselves against other people. It’s how we work out where we stand. But a feed full of other people’s best moments makes the measuring wildly unfair, and self-esteem that depends on being better than others swings about all over the place.

    Two old friends met for a curry, one of them having just bought a house. The other rents a room in a shared flat in Leyton where the shower only works if you hold it. The renter spent the whole evening quietly ranking herself, and she missed what was right in front of her. Her friend was lonely in the new house, and had wanted to say so all evening, and kept waiting for a gap in the conversation that never came because the renter was so busy ranking herself. You’re comparing your inside with somebody’s outside. When you catch yourself at it, try a question instead. “How are you, really?”

  7. You can own a mistake without drowning in it.

    Owning a mistake gets a lot easier once it doesn’t mean you’re worthless. Say what you did. Say what it did to the other person. Say what you’ll do now. Then stop. Beating yourself up afterwards proves nothing about how sorry you are, it just pulls everyone’s attention back onto you.

    Jess forgot to pay her share of the broadband and it got cut off for the whole of the bank holiday weekend. On the Tuesday, after a weekend of the others tethering their laptops to their phones and one of them going to the library to finish an essay, she put her head round the living-room door. “I forgot to pay, and it left you all without internet for three days. I’ve paid it and set a reminder for the 28th.” Someone said “Cheers,” and that was more or less the end of it.

  8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they’re treated, and adults never entirely stop. The words matter, because “You did a careless thing” leaves room to change, while “You’re so careless” shuts the door. Notice effort, and who someone is, as well as results, and people feel they matter on bad days too.

    Gill took over a team that hid its problems until week ten, and from her first month she started every project review with the same question, “What did we learn?”, and refused, politely and repeatedly, to discuss who had got what wrong. Within six months people were bringing problems to her in week two, which saved everyone a great many Friday evenings, and the soppy question stayed.

    Today you could say one thing to someone that has nothing to do with how well they’ve done. Even just “I’m glad I know you.”

You can control how you speak to yourself now, whether you repair what you can, and where you spend your time. Give your energy to people who treat you as someone who matters. Saying sorry all the time

In everyday life

Shame turns up in very ordinary places. Between partners, “You forgot to call” can land as “You’re a terrible partner”, so what comes back is a defence instead of a sorry. A child who hears “You’re so naughty” learns something about who they are, while “That was a naughty thing to do” leaves a way back. A friend who says yes to everything may be quietly burning out, because no feels like risking being dropped. At work, someone may sit on a small error for weeks because owning it feels like proof they never belonged there.

On your own: a kinder voice to keep

You don’t need anyone else for this. Notice how you talk to yourself and practise one gentler sentence. Catch the critic’s favourite words (“always”, “useless”, “pathetic”, “should”) and swap one for a plain fact. “I’m hopeless” becomes “I was late twice this week.” On bad days, keep a short list of what you did, and let getting up count. You’re allowed to be disappointed in what you did and still be on your own side.

Common mix-ups

People worry that self-compassion is just self-pity. They feel quite different from the inside. Self-pity says “only me, poor me”. Self-compassion says “this is hard, and other people go through it too”, which joins you to other people instead of shutting you away from them. Others fear that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. The research leans the other way, because it’s fear of your own attack that makes people stop trying and hide their mistakes. And self-worth doesn’t mean thinking you’re great, only that you go on mattering on the days when you plainly aren’t.

If you want to help someone else with this

Talk about what happened, never about what sort of person they are. Arguing with someone’s shame (“You’re not useless!”) can feel like being told they’re wrong to feel it, so you might try “It sounds like you feel really low about it. I don’t see you that way.” Helping them find one small thing they can do gives the feeling somewhere to go. And kindness on ordinary days, when nothing has gone wrong, teaches worth far better than anything said in a crisis.

You could say “That sounds like it’s weighing on you. Want to work out one thing that would help?” “You don’t have to earn being cared about.”

When to get more help

Everyone feels shame and self-doubt sometimes. It’s worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or therapist if harsh thoughts about yourself are there most days, if they stop you working, eating, sleeping or seeing people, or if you feel you deserve to be hurt. Therapies such as compassion-focused therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy are built to help with exactly this.

If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here, please tell someone today. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number.

If someone close to you keeps telling you you’re worthless, stupid or lucky to have them, that isn’t a self-esteem problem. It can be a way of controlling someone. Your aunt’s seventieth, in the function room above the cricket club. You’re standing by the cake, minding your own business, when she tells the whole room, “Oh, she was always the shy one!”

Everybody laughs, and you laugh along with them, and something stings.

You’re forty-two. Every weekday at twenty to nine you stand in front of thirty eight-year-olds and get them to sit down, which takes some doing. So are you still the shy one? Were you ever?

We describe each other by personality all the time, as laid-back, or a worrier, or the organised one, and descriptions like those have a way of setting hard. This chapter is about how personality actually works, what stays put and what moves. Two tools elsewhere build on it. The “How to talk to me” card and the style quiz (Chapter 2, page ) describe how you lean on a few dials, which is more use than knowing which box somebody once put you in. And One owner per job (Book 4, Chapter 2, page 27) arranges the jobs round people’s differences, so nobody has to turn into someone else to share a house.

  1. Traits work like dials.

    Personality is the usual pattern in how you think and feel and act. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”: openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organised and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are).

    The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them well. Each runs from low to high, and most people sit near the middle of most of them. That’s the bit online quizzes leave out. Two friends did one on the train to Brighton and were told, very confidently, that one was an introvert and the other an extravert. Then they looked at the actual numbers. Four points apart, both bang in the middle. They spent the rest of the journey arguing about which of them was more middle.

    If you’re curious, mark where you think you sit on each dial from one to ten, and ask a close friend where they’d put you. The gap is often the interesting bit.

    Dig deeper: the five dials

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality you can see very early on. Some babies are on the go from the moment they wake. Some are calm. Some take to a new routine straight away and some need a week. Some flinch at every loud noise. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences and the idea of “goodness of fit”, meaning how well a child’s temperament suits what the people and places round them expect.

    Isla is two and cried at every new playgroup. Her mum tried coaxing her into the circle, then bribing her, then (once, quietly) begging. In the end she just sat at the edge of the church hall with Isla on her lap, feeling a bit of a lemon, while everyone else did “The Wheels on the Bus”. It smelled of orange squash and floor polish. Week three, Isla climbed down on her own, walked to the sand table, and didn’t look back. Her mum, slightly put out, had a biscuit.

    You could say “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made.”

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality describes a pattern, and patterns have exceptions. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, endlessly patient with toddlers and very short with call centres. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across lots of situations and a good deal less about the one in front of you.

    Anita had decided her new colleague was cold. Polite, competent, cold. Then came the charity football match, and there he was in a borrowed bib two sizes too small, shouting encouragement at a goalkeeper who was letting in everything. On Monday Anita sat looking round the open-plan office with its grey partitions and humming lights and wondered how much of what she’d seen was the room.

    Dig deeper: person and situation

  4. Introvert and extravert is about energy, not liking people.

    People who are more introverted often like people a lot. What they find is that busy, noisy company uses energy up, and they need quiet afterwards to get it back. People who are more extraverted tend to be topped up by company. Most of us are a mix (sometimes called an ambivert), and every point on the dial just needs different things to be at its best.

    Eleven o’clock at a wedding in a barn outside Hexham. Fraser wants the after-party in the village pub. Luke wants bed and silence and possibly never to hear “Mr Brightside” again. They’ve had this exact standoff at four previous weddings. This time Fraser goes for an hour and Luke walks back to the hotel along the lane with his shoes in his hand, and they meet at breakfast, one of them hoarse and the other one smug, both of them happy. What Luke said, for once, was “I loved tonight, and I’m out of social energy,” which he’d practised on the walk from the car.

    Dig deeper: introversion and extraversion

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable. It also moves. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organised, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness went on growing right across the lifespan. Big life events play a part, and so do the roles people take on and who they love.

    At school Tunde lost his keys about once a fortnight and once turned up to a maths exam on the wrong day. He’s a charge nurse now, forty-six, the calm one people look for when it goes wrong at three in the morning. At the reunion his old friends kept staring at him across the room in the function suite, and eventually one of them came over with two pints and said, “What happened to you?” He thought about it. “Life kept asking me to cope,” he said, “and eventually I learned how.”

    Dig deeper: how personality changes with age

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    If you want to change a trait you can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways over and over. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended to move in the direction they wanted over sixteen weeks. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially towards more emotional steadiness.

    Noor wanted to be less quiet at work. She made a rule. One thing in every team meeting, even if it was only “I agree with Neil”. The first fortnight her heart thudded so hard she was sure people could see it through her jumper. By the end of the second month it had become ordinary, and somewhere in the third month she forgot it had ever been a rule. Pick one small thing that fits who you’d like to be more of, and do it three times this week. Book 2, Chapter 6 (page 103) has more on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    There’s no good personality. Every trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are lovely to be around and may find “no” almost impossible to say. Very conscientious people are the ones you’d leave your keys with, and they can’t always stop. People high in openness love anything new and can be bored to tears by routine.

    For most of a year Alys was irritated by her housemate. His trainers in the hall. His “it’ll be fine”. Then the boiler died on the coldest night in January, and while Alys stood in the kitchen in her coat reading the manual, he rang round until he found a plumber who’d come out at ten o’clock, and then he filled everybody’s hot water bottles from the kettle. “It’ll be fine,” he said, and it was, apart from the hot water bottle that leaked.

    A one-page card, “How to talk to me”, turns a trait people complain about into plain information about what helps.

  8. Differences are usually about fit.

    When two people with different personalities share a home or a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one can both be right in their own way, and so can a planner and someone spontaneous, or a talker and someone who needs quiet. What helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it, without either of you being the problem. Personality is also different from neurodivergence and from mental health conditions. In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you build a life that suits you, with about the right amount of company and quiet. “I’m just not a people person” might be true. It might also be an old label that deserves testing at least once. With a partner, a shared plan (one does the bills, the other does holidays) usually goes better than trying to change each other. A quiet friend who hardly texts may still care a great deal, so ask what kind of contact suits them. A child’s temperament says nothing about good or bad parenting, and adjusting to fit them is a skill you pick up. And family roles like “the clever one” or “the difficult one” can hang on long after they stop fitting. You’re allowed to grow out of them, whether or not your aunt has noticed.

Common mix-ups

You aren’t simply an introvert or an extravert. Most people are somewhere in the middle, and where you sit shifts with the setting and your mood. Personality isn’t set in stone by thirty, either. It keeps moving slowly all through life. Online type quizzes are good for fun and for starting a conversation, and that includes the website’s own Wave Code, which shows four leanings on four dials, but they sort people into a handful of types when real people are spread along each dial. “They’re just difficult” often means “they’re different from me”, and it can hide stress or illness or a need nobody has asked about. And practising something new isn’t being fake. It feels odd at first, like new shoes, and then it’s yours.

If you want to help someone else with this

Drop the old labels, and notice if you still describe someone the way they were twenty years ago. Ask instead of guessing. “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” will tell you more than any theory. Say it when you see someone grow, because “You handled that so calmly” can show a person a change they hadn’t spotted. And describe what happened, not who someone is. “You’re so uptight” shuts doors that a plain description would leave open.

Words to try “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits any more.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”

When to get more help

Personality differences are normal and don’t need fixing. It may be worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or psychologist if:

  • a pattern in how you feel, think or relate to people keeps causing you or others real distress, across many parts of life;
  • you feel very unsure who you are, or your moods swing so much it’s hard to cope;
  • worry, low mood or fear of people is stopping you from living the life you want.

Please don’t diagnose yourself or anyone else with a “personality disorder” from what you read online. Those are clinical terms that only a qualified professional can use, after a proper assessment.

A personality trait never excuses controlling, frightening or hurtful behaviour. If you’re afraid of someone close to you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help.

Monday, 8:15am

“Shoes, please, just put them on,” you say, for the third time.

Your son is lying on the hall floor with his hands over his ears. The new socks are wrong. From where you’re standing, they are socks. From where he is, the seam across his toes is sandpaper, and the alarm clock upstairs that nobody has switched off is going straight through the middle of his head. You are already late. So is he, in a way you can’t see.

This chapter is for neurodivergent people, diagnosed or not, and for the people who live and work with them. It can’t diagnose anyone, and only you get to choose the words you use for yourself. I should say that some of it is personal. I’m autistic, with ADHD in the mix too, and the About page on the website tells that story. I’ve tried to write this the way I’d want it written about me.

Two tools elsewhere rest on it. The “How to talk to me” card (Chapter 2, page ) works because different minds need different input, and saying things plainly helps almost everyone. One owner per job and the written job list (Book 4, Chapter 2, page 27) move memory out of anyone’s head and onto paper, which helps ADHD and autistic minds most and everybody a bit.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    Brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with people. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what gets treated as typical. A different mind isn’t a broken one. Differences can still be disabling, though, especially in a world built for one kind of mind, and that is just as true.

    Gary found out he was dyslexic at forty, and he sat in the car outside the assessment centre for twenty minutes afterwards with the report on the passenger seat, not reading it, obviously. What he mostly felt was relief. For thirty years he had half believed the teachers who wrote “lazy” in red pen.

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally. Many have deep, focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely than others do, and find predictability calming. Autism doesn’t run along one line from “mild” to “severe”. Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change from day to day, and the misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    “Your slides have three typos,” Kate told her colleague, kindly. “One on slide four and two on the summary.” It was exactly what she would have wanted to be told. He went quiet for the rest of the afternoon and she had no idea why until a mutual friend explained it to her in the pub, They were using two different sets of rules for what kindness sounds like, and neither set is wrong, though it would help if they came with subtitles. If you’re the direct one, it can help to say so up front, something like “I like it when people say exactly what they mean, so you can be direct with me.”

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD usually have plenty of attention, and the hard bit is pointing it. Boring tasks feel like pushing a car uphill, interesting ones swallow whole evenings, and time is slippery (there’s “now” and there’s “not now” and not much in between). Feelings arrive fast and big. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself. Effort and caring don’t come into it.

    Saffy forgot her best friend’s birthday. Completely. Not a text. Then she spent six hours making a card with pressed flowers from the garden, glued on one at a time with tweezers, and posted it four days late with a note that said “I KNOW”. The forgetting and the six hours came out of the same brain. If something keeps falling out of yours, move it into the world. A labelled alarm. A note on the door. A basket by the front door for the keys.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise, or a label in a jumper. Others need more input, like movement or strong flavours or firm pressure. Overload is a body at its limit, which is a very different thing from being fussy.

    For what it’s worth, mine is turned up high. A loud restaurant costs me a great deal, and so do supermarkets, and I notice the hum from a vent or a picture hanging half an inch off level whether I want to or not. Two housemates of the made-up kind fell out over the kitchen radio, because one found silence creepy and the other found Radio 2 at breakfast physically painful. They settled on the radio until nine and headphones after, which has lasted two years.

    Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you and one that calms you, and change one small thing today.

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, and none of them measures how clever someone is. Plenty of people have a “spiky” profile, very strong in some areas and struggling in others. The right tools, audiobooks or extra time in exams or a reader for the long letters, can level things a good deal.

    Every Wednesday afternoon on the football pitch, fourteen-year-old Ewan is called clumsy, usually by a PE teacher who should know better. The same term he wrote and directed the school play, which had a revolving set made out of a bike wheel. Ask the people you live or work with how they find it easiest to take things in, and then actually use it.

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences. They copy other people, force eye contact, hold back movements, rehearse small talk in the car. This is called masking. It can get someone through a day. It also uses a great deal of energy, and over years it can wear a person down into exhaustion and burnout. I spent years masking, and for a long time the cost was invisible even to me, which is rather the point of a mask.

    Mia seems completely fine at college, chatty in the canteen and helpful in class and laughing in all the right places. She gets home at quarter past four, goes straight up to her room, and can’t say a word to anyone for an hour. Her dad used to knock and ask what was wrong, and now he leaves a cup of tea outside the door and goes away.

    Notice one place where you could drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and it often lasts months. Rest helps, and so do fewer demands and less masking. Pushing harder usually makes it worse. If you think you might be there, list three demands you could drop or hand to someone else for a month. If it goes on, or you feel hopeless, talk to a doctor.

    Words you could use “I’m in burnout. What I need for a while is fewer demands.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much, and it can look like crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version: going still, losing speech, not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload.

    In my own case words go first. What helps is less of everything. Less noise, fewer words, no questions, more space. Talking can wait, sometimes until the next day. On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Ways to say it (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds. Rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing, fiddling with something. Most people stim a little. (Watch any meeting and count the jiggling feet.) For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping. It’s worth noticing what your own hands do when you’re stressed or focused or happy, because that’s yours, and nobody needs to train it out of you.

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds go deep into one thing at a time, often autistic and ADHD ones. That focus can be a real gift, because it builds expertise and brings a great deal of joy, and it also makes switching hard. Being interrupted can feel like being yanked out of water.

    “Dinner!” Nothing. “Tilly, dinner!” Still nothing. Third time, louder, and the fish fingers are going cold, and Dominic is getting properly cross. His daughter is upstairs building a castle and has reached a delicate stage with the drawbridge. The next night he tries a ten-minute warning, then a five. She comes down on time, still talking about the drawbridge, and tells him at length what the problem with it is. He doesn’t entirely follow, but he eats his fish fingers while they’re still warm, which is the main thing. The warning, for the record, goes “Dinner in ten minutes, I’ll come and get you at five to,” shouted up the stairs, and it works about four nights out of five.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by and engaged with their special interests reported higher wellbeing, though very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing.

    I am, by my own admission, a geek about a long list of things. Ask someone about their favourite subject and let them talk for five minutes without steering. It costs you five minutes. It’s one of the warmest things you can do for anyone.

    Dig deeper: special interests and wellbeing

  12. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, plain words help everyone. Say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. And changes made for one person (a quiet space, an agenda, flexible hours) usually help plenty of other people too.

    A team started sending meeting agendas a day early, for one autistic colleague. Within a month most of them said meetings had got shorter. Two people who had never said a word in a meeting started saying things.

    Today, swap one “how I work best” fact with someone. Tell them one thing that helps you and ask for one that helps them. The website can bend a bit to your mind too, with Read your way (bigger text, calmer pages, fewer surprises).

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, and sometimes support and a community too. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person” and others prefer “person with autism”. The kindest rule is to ask, use the words the person chooses, and never try to diagnose somebody else, least of all in an argument.

    The row was about the washing-up. It was going badly. “You’re so autistic,” Danny said, and the conversation ended right there, as it deserved to. In the morning, over toast that neither of them was eating, he said sorry, properly, and then asked what his partner actually needed from him when the kitchen was a mess. It was the conversation they should have had the night before.

    If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed and pick one small next step, like talking to someone or asking a doctor.

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they’re autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties or fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed the way they understood their own lives. Relief and grief and something like joy can all turn up together, on the same afternoon. If that’s you, look for one autistic-led group or online space, and give yourself time.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis later in life

  15. Getting things started and finished is a skill of its own.

    Planning, starting, switching, remembering what you were doing and keeping track of time are called executive function. Many autistic and ADHD people find some of these harder, and harder on some days than others. A review of 235 studies by Demetriou and colleagues (2018) found differences in executive function in autism on average, with a lot of variation between people. The brain’s self-management system is working differently. Laziness doesn’t come into it.

    Siân wrote a forty-page report for her board in a week, and it was brilliant. The same three weeks, she could not post one parcel. It sat on the hall table while she walked past it forty times. Then she put it on the doormat with a yellow sticky note on top (“POST ME”) and set an alarm for Tuesday at 8.10, and on Tuesday at 8.11 it went. See routines that help and Wired differently.

  16. When demands feel like a threat.

    Some people, often autistic, feel strong anxiety when asked or expected to do things, even things they want to do. Avoiding the demand can look like refusing, joking, changing the subject or a meltdown. The profile was first described as “pathological demand avoidance,” or PDA, by Newson and colleagues (2003).

    It isn’t in the main diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it. A review by Kildahl and colleagues (2021) found the evidence limited and mostly based on parent reports. Many people who identify with it prefer the phrase “persistent drive for autonomy.” Honestly, I’d hold this one loosely until the research settles. What helps tends to be the same whatever it’s called. Less pressure. Requests that feel like invitations. “Put your coat on now” gets a flat no from Reuben every morning. “It’s cold out. Coat or jumper, your choice?” gets the coat, usually.

    Dig deeper: demand avoidance, carefully

  17. Not recognising faces isn’t rudeness.

    Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, means finding it hard to recognise faces, sometimes even close friends seen out of context. It can be something you’re born with, and it often runs in families (NHS). Many autistic people also find remembering faces harder, as a review by Weigelt and colleagues (2012) found. People who don’t recognise you may be relying on your voice, your hair, your coat or where they usually see you.

    Clive walked straight past his neighbour in the supermarket without a flicker, and she went home feeling snubbed and told her sister about it on the phone. When he explained, a week later and very embarrassed, that he hadn’t known her without her spaniel, she laughed. Now she says “Hi Clive, it’s Jo from number twelve” every time, even when the spaniel is right there.

    Words to try “I’m bad with faces, so please say who you are when you say hi.” (to someone else) “Hi, it’s Jo from next door.”

    Dig deeper: face blindness

  18. Not speaking isn’t the same as having nothing to say.

    Some autistic people speak rarely, sometimes or not at all, and some lose speech when stressed. Many communicate through AAC (augmentative and alternative communication), which can mean typing, letter boards, picture cards, apps or sign. Using AAC doesn’t stop speech developing: a review by Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) found it did not hold speech back, and may help it. The safest place to start is to presume competence, which means assuming the person understands and has something to say, and giving them the time and the tools to say it.

    At Sunday lunch at her nan’s, fifteen-year-old Ruby types on her tablet. Her uncle used to talk about her across the table as if she wasn’t there (“Does she want gravy?”). Now he asks her, and waits, a bit longer than he’d like, while she types. Her answer was “Yes. Obviously.” Wait longer than feels natural, which will feel very long and isn’t.

    Dig deeper: non-speaking people and AAC

  19. Telling friends and dates is your choice.

    Whether to tell someone you’re autistic or ADHD, and when, is up to you. Telling can bring understanding. In a study by Sasson and Morrison (2019), people formed better first impressions of autistic adults when they knew they were autistic, especially if they knew more about autism.

    People don’t always respond well, though, and interviews by Au-Yeung and colleagues (2025) describe autistic adults weighing hope for support against fear of not being believed. Many share a little at a time, starting with what helps and leaving the label for later. Second date, outside a bar with a queue. “I’m autistic,” Nina said, “so I’m quite direct, and I’d rather walk than sit in there.” Her date looked at the bar, and the queue, and back at her. “Oh, thank God,” he said. They walked along the canal as far as the lock and back.

    Words you could use “I’m autistic. For me that means…” “I won’t always pick up hints, so please just tell me.” (if someone shares with you) “Thanks for telling me. What helps?”

    Dig deeper: disclosure in friendships and dating

  20. Finding your people.

    Lots of autistic people describe meeting other autistic people as a turning point, because at last they don’t have to translate or explain or mask. The research backs this up. Cooper, Smith and Russell (2017) found that autistic people who felt more connected to other autistic people had higher self-esteem, and through that, less anxiety and depression. Being autistic can be an identity and a community as well as a diagnosis.

    After his diagnosis at fifty-three, Bernard joined an online group of autistic adults who love old maps, specifically the Ordnance Survey kind with hand-drawn hachures. The first evening he talked to them for three hours about contour lines. When he logged off he had more energy than when he’d started, which hadn’t happened after a conversation in years,

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing how your mind works helps you build a life that fits it, and spot burnout before it’s on top of you (see Get to know your wiring, spreadloveandacceptance.com/self-path). Partners can swap “how I work best” notes about plans, messages, touch and time alone. With children, behaviour very often has a sensory or overload reason, so “What’s too much right now?” is worth asking before “Why are you doing this?” A friend who cancels at the last minute may just be out of energy, and offering something easier, like a walk, keeps the door open. At work, written agendas, clear deadlines and quiet spaces help far more people than the ones they were made for.

If you have ADHD and share a home, make the chore tools lighter. Log three days instead of seven, with a tally on the fridge. Own a few jobs completely, each with a labelled alarm, instead of helping a bit with everything. Forgetting is a setup problem to fix together. More in the full chapter.

Common mix-ups

“Everyone’s a bit autistic” is probably the commonest, and it makes real needs sound small. Everyone shares some traits, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life. ADHD doesn’t just mean not sitting still. Many people with it, girls and women especially, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside. “They seem fine to me” overlooks how much masking can hide, and seeming fine can cost someone their whole evening. Adjustments aren’t special treatment; they remove a barrier, the way glasses do. And the idea that autistic people lack empathy is simply out of date. Many feel other people’s feelings very strongly, and the differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

Believe them about what’s hard and what helps, even when it’s different for you. Be clear, which means saying what you mean, giving times and writing down anything important. Ask what helps, because every neurodivergent person is different and their answer will beat any list, this one included. Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places matter more than they look. And enjoy their interests with them, if you can.

Ways to say it “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you wonder whether a difference is part of ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, for you or someone you care for, a doctor, school support staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so start early and write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has free, private help.

Questions people ask

Some questions this book tends to raise, with short answers and where to read more.

Do I need to remember my childhood clearly for the first chapter to help?

Most people remember a handful of scenes and a general feeling, and that is plenty. The first chapter asks about the rules your home ran on, such as how feelings were handled or who did the worrying. Those rules tend to show up in how you react today more than in any one memory, so start with one rule you can name. Where your lens came from (Chapter 1, page ) walks you through it.

What if looking back brings up something painful?

Stop there, and come back another day if you want to. Nobody has to finish a page. If old memories keep pulling you under, or you find yourself avoiding more and more of everyday life, talk to a doctor or counsellor. Worry, low mood and trauma (Book 2, Chapter 3, page 49) describes the kinds of help there are, and the help page at the front of this book (page ) lists free places to start.

I think I might be autistic or have ADHD. Can this book tell me?

It can’t, and nothing in it is a test. Minds that work differently (Chapter 7, page ) describes how some minds work, so that you might recognise yourself or someone you love, and it says who can talk you through an assessment if you want one. Plenty of people find the descriptions useful whether or not they ever look for a diagnosis.

Can a grown-up really change their personality?

A little, slowly, and mostly by practising the thing you want to be more like. Personality, temperament and change (Chapter 6, page ) treats traits as dials that move a few notches over the years. Every setting has its gifts and its costs, so the aim is a better fit with your life more than a new you.

My partner isn’t interested in any of this. Is it still worth reading?

Yes. This book is about you, and it works if you are the only person in your home who ever opens it. Knowing your own wiring and weather makes it easier to say what you need, and the people around you often notice the change before they notice the book. If you later want to look at things together, Book 3 and Book 4 are written for that.

How do I know when a book isn’t enough?

Most chapters have a short part called “When to get more help”, with the signs to look out for. If you need someone now, the help page at the front of this book (page ) has numbers to call. Help beyond this book lists counsellors, helplines and support groups, and most of them are free.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Alexithymia
Finding it hard to notice, name or describe your own feelings. It is a trait on a scale, not an illness, and it is more common in some autistic people. Naming body signals first can help.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, how full your stress tank is: a fuller tank means a lower battery.
A bid
A bid is any small reach for attention or connection, like “look at this,” a sigh or a joke.
Calm-down plan
Your calm-down plan (once called the Calm-Down Kit) is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Double empathy
The idea, from the autistic researcher Damian Milton, that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways: each finds the other hard to read, so neither side is simply “missing” something.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
If-then plan
A small plan written as “If this happens, then I will do that”, which makes a new habit easier to start (Peter Gollwitzer).
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
Masking
Masking is hiding the way you’re wired to fit in, which can be very tiring over time.
Monotropism
A theory of autistic attention: attention gathers deeply on a few things at a time, rather than spreading thinly across many. Switching away can take real effort.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Person A and Person B
Person A and Person B are simply the two people on a two-person worksheet or tool. They are labels, not a ranking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
The rulebook from home
The rulebook from home is the unwritten set of rules you grew up with, about love, upset, chores and rest, that you may still expect others to follow.
Second arrow
The extra pain we add on top of a hard moment, such as being angry at ourselves for being sad. The first arrow is the event; the second is what we tell ourselves about it (a Buddhist image).
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Wave Code
Four letters from the style quiz in How you connect, one for each of four preferences about how you take in and send words. Each letter shows where you lean on a dial today: a shorthand for preferences, not a personality type.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill in on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
