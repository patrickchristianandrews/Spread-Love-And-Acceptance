About this book, and how to use it

This book starts with you. It looks at where your way of seeing things came from, how you are wired today, how feelings work, why your worth was never a score, how personality works and how minds can work differently.

It is for anyone who wants to understand themselves a little better, at their own pace. You don’t need a partner, a problem or a diagnosis to read it, and nothing in it is a test.

Read the chapters in any order. Each is a short version of a chapter that is free to read online, where the full version has more research, more examples and more to try. The code at the end of each chapter opens it on your phone.

Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed. The kitchen looks like a small storm went through it: last night’s pan soaking on the hob, a tin of tomatoes open by the kettle, three mugs. Before you have had a single thought, you feel it. Of course it’s always you. Nobody said that. But in the house you grew up in, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it, and that old rule has just made a decision on your behalf.

Across town, Ray’s house has been quiet since his wife died, and nobody is going to see his dishes. He still can’t sit down until every one of them is washed and dried and back in the cupboard, because a rule from the home he grew up in is still keeping watch.

  1. You’re not one thing.

    A strong reaction usually comes from three places at once, and it helps to know which one is doing most of the talking. Part of it is how you’re built, your wiring, which stays much the same through life. Part of it is what life taught you, the patterns that can soften with time. And part of it is just the kind of day you’re having. This chapter is about the middle layer; Chapter 2 (page ) takes the other two.

    Dot, who is seventy-one, snaps at her son when he turns up forty minutes late for lunch with the roast already drying out. Some of that is wiring, because she has liked things on time since she was a girl. Some of it is an old rule, picked up from her own father, that being late means you don’t care. And some of it is that she was awake at four listening to the wind. She could say, “Some of this is me being tired and some of it’s an old thing. Give me a minute,” and the lunch would probably recover.

    Dig deeper: the three layers

  2. Your lens came from somewhere.

    Family taught you what to expect of yourself and other people, and so did school, friends, culture, how much money there was at home and whatever was on the telly. By the time you’re an adult the lens no longer feels like a lens. It feels like seeing.

    Birthdays are a good test. One friend grew up with a party every year and cousins down from Glasgow; the other got a card on the breakfast table and a slightly better tea. A quiet dinner for two lands as a slight on one side of the table and as perfect on the other, and “In my family this was a big deal. What was it like in yours?” clears the air faster than any argument about what birthdays are for. Where your lens came from walks through the idea slowly, and you can read one section and stop.

  3. Each stage of growing up asks its own question.

    A small child is working out whether the world is safe and whether they can do things for themselves. Later the questions change to whether they’re capable, where they belong, and whether they can be close to someone without losing themselves. Later still, adults start asking what they’re building and whether any of it has meant something. The ages are rough, but the answer you landed on at each stop can become something you go on using for decades without noticing.

    Someone who moved schools four times as a teenager may still walk into every new job feeling like the new kid with the wrong shoes, and only later realise that part of them never stopped asking whether they belong. Which of those questions still feels loud for you?

    Dig deeper: the stages

  4. When something happened matters.

    An experience tends to go deeper when it touches the question you were answering at the time. A house move at four is mostly about a new bedroom. A move at fourteen, right in the middle of working out where you belong, can leave a much longer feeling of being on the outside.

    Two adult siblings remember their parents’ divorce in ways that hardly overlap. The younger one was six and remembers a new bedroom with glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling and a different bus route. The older one was fifteen and remembers feeling torn in half every other Friday. Both memories are true, which goes a long way to explaining why they still argue about that year (see Family rifts, spreadloveandacceptance.com/family-rifts). Deeper doesn’t mean permanent, though; people keep learning all their lives.

  5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts.

    Plenty of adults carry a sentence about themselves that they picked up before they could check it: “I’m only okay when I’m useful.” “My needs are a burden.” “Mistakes mean I’m bad.” A child can’t see what is going on for the grown-ups and only knows how it felt in the room. As an adult you’re allowed to look again with the full picture, which doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine.

    Sheila has fostered children for eleven years and is very good at it. She learned as a girl that her needs were a nuisance, and so she finds it almost impossible to ask for respite, a planned weekend when someone else cares for the children, even in the week she put the cereal in the fridge. The old voice says don’t ask; the adult in her knows the service exists for exactly this. Write down one old belief about yourself, and underneath it write what you’d say to a friend who believed the same thing about themselves.

  6. An old rule can read other people for you.

    If silence at home meant someone was angry, a partner’s quiet evening can feel like weather coming in. And an expectation nobody ever said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only fail it, whether it’s “if you loved me, you’d just know” or “whoever notices, does it”. Every one of those is somebody’s family rule.

    A housemate heads out at ten past seven without saying goodbye, and someone raised in a quiet, angry house spends the morning wondering what they did, while the housemate is on the 7.14 to a shift she nearly overslept for. This week, when you catch yourself guessing what someone’s silence means, try asking instead: “When you went quiet earlier I wasn’t sure what it meant. Everything okay?”

  8. You get to choose which rules to keep.

    Go back to your rulebook and ask of each line whether you still want it. When a reaction feels bigger than the moment, try a one-minute lens check once you’ve settled. Where did I learn this? How old does it feel? Is it true here, with this person, today? A team leader at a building society notices that her old rule about mistakes makes her hide problems from her manager until they’re too big to hide. She writes a new one on a yellow sticky note above her monitor (“Mistakes are information, and the team fixes them together”) and leaves it there long after she stops needing it. Pick one rule from your list and write the version you’d like to live by: “I used to think ___. Now I’m choosing ___.” Later, if you like, swap rulebooks with someone you trust and choose some shared rules on purpose.

  9. What you carry forward is yours.

    You couldn’t control the home you grew up in, or what other people did, or what you learned before you had all the facts. What’s in your hands now is smaller and a good deal stronger: which old rules you keep, how you treat yourself about them, and where you spend your energy today, and that includes people. Give your time and care where they’re met with care. Where the effort is all one way, let it shrink back to what feels fair, so you still have something left over.

    For nine years, someone who lives alone has organised every meet-up with an old school friend who never once asks back. They stop chasing, send one warm message (“I’d love to see you. Let me know when works for you”) and find over the next few months that they see more of the friends who reply (see Always the one reaching out?). Tuesday morning

Two people get the same text from the same friend: “Can we talk tonight?” One is pleased, because she likes knowing what’s coming. The other feels his stomach drop. In the house he grew up in, “we need to talk” meant trouble, and he spends the afternoon rehearsing answers in the car park and the queue at Greggs, for a conversation about splitting a cottage in Wales in August.

You, in all your relationships

Your old rules go wherever you go, starting with how you talk to yourself: the voice you use after a mistake is often an old one. At home they decide who notices, who plans and what quiet means, and two housemates can discover they’ve been living by different rulebooks about mess and who’s allowed round after ten. In families, old roles like the helper or the peacemaker switch back on before you’ve got your coat off. At work, someone who was praised mainly for being useful may say yes to everything and then quietly resent it, until seeing the old rule lets them pause before the next yes. After a breakup old rules can get louder, which makes it a good time to look at them kindly (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own). Common mix-ups

The biggest worry people bring to this chapter is that looking back means blaming their family. It’s about understanding, and many of the adults who raised us were working with lenses of their own; if that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit. The second is that if something started in childhood you’re stuck with it. Many old patterns soften once you can see them and feel safe enough to practise something new, and some leave a lasting sensitivity that you learn to carry and explain. People also assume every big reaction is an old wound, when quite often it’s just a full tank (Book 4, Chapter 3, page 51, helps you tell leftover stress from this-moment stress). And some decide there’s no point looking at their own rules if the other person won’t look at theirs. I’d disagree. Knowing your own rules changes how you react, even if nobody else ever reads a word of this.

A note on care: This part looks back at growing up. It is general education, not a diagnosis or therapy, and it can’t tell you what anyone has. Not every home felt calm or kind, so take only what is useful. If looking back feels heavy, you can stop and come back another day, or explore it with a qualified professional, if and when you want that. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared.

If you want to go on, think of one “always” or “never” you felt this week and ask whose voice it sounds like, and how old you felt when you heard it. Then write one sentence that the adult you would rather be could live by and stick it on the fridge. Where your lens came from, Roots & Wings and Know your own wiring go further.

Sunday, 7:50pm

“You okay? You’ve been quiet all day.” Your friend has paused the film to ask. You say you’re fine. The honest answer is that you can’t tell whether you’re fine, tired or quietly bothered by something someone said on Thursday, and you couldn’t explain it to yourself, let alone to her, so you press play.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part: your temperament, your pace, your senses, how you take in words. You work with it, by building a setup that suits you. Today’s conditions are the passing part, things like sleep and hunger and the time of day, and you work around them by eating first or sleeping on it. A lot of frustration comes from mixing the two up. (Chapter 1, page , covers the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    A dad who works nights finds the school-run chatter unbearable at ten past eight in the morning, and for a few months he quietly wonders whether he’s cut out for parenting at all. Then he swaps with his partner and does the half-three pick-up instead, and the same chatter about who sat next to whom at lunch is funny again (see Different hours, spreadloveandacceptance.com/different-hours). The children hadn’t changed. The hour had. Write down one thing that always bothers you, which is probably wiring, and one that only bothers you when you’re tired.

    You could say “It’s the hour, honestly. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Every conversation runs through your own state before it reaches anyone else. Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 51) looks closely at the stress tank in a shared home, so all this chapter adds is the self side: a one-minute reading on your own before you decide what a hard moment means.     One state, many words. The chapters and tools use a few everyday pictures for one thing, how much room you have today.

    • The three states (Book 2, Chapter 1, page 9, calls them the ladder) are calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty.
    • My weather today uses sky words for the same three. Clear is calm and connected, gusty is revved up, and fogged in is running on empty. Its pressure is how much you were already carrying.

    • The battery is the other side of the tank, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery.

    “Gusty”, “revved up”, “a fairly full tank” and “a low battery” can all describe the same Tuesday afternoon. I’d pick one set and stick with it.

  4. Ask where this feeling is coming from.

    Name the feeling first, even roughly (“tight, annoyed, a bit scared”). Then ask whether it’s the size of the moment or feels older, which layer it belongs to, and what it needs right now. Food, space, a heads-up, some reassurance, a proper conversation later: the answer is often more ordinary than you’d expect. It takes a minute and works best once the first wave has passed. A colleague leans over the partition at 4.45 on a Friday and says “Quick question?”, your heart starts going, and if you stop to look you might find it’s jumpy, with a former boss somewhere in it and a long week on top, and that what it needs is five minutes before you answer.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear warns that something might be dangerous. Anger says something seems unfair, or in your way. Sadness says you’ve lost something that mattered. A signal is worth listening to, but it can be wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm goes off for burnt toast with exactly the same urgency it would use for a fire.

    Say you feel jealous when a friend makes a new close friend. The jealousy proves nothing about you, and nothing about whether your friend is drifting off. What it shows is how much the friendship matters to you, which is worth knowing, and possibly worth telling her.

    Things to tell yourself “This feeling is telling me something matters. What is it?” “Is this the fire, or the toast?”

  2. Feelings start in the body.

    Often your body reacts before your mind has a word for what’s going on: a racing heart, a hot face, a heavy chest, a knot in the stomach. Then the brain tries to make sense of the signal, and it guesses. That’s why being hungry or tired or in pain can feel so like being upset.

    A teenager comes home furious with his mum over very little, something about a towel. After a cheese sandwich and twenty minutes on the sofa with the dog, most of the fury has gone, and he admits, with some reluctance, that he skipped lunch. A few times today, stop and ask what your body feels like right now. Is there hunger in it, or cold, or a sore back?

  4. More words, better tools.

A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need fancy words. A number works, and so does a colour.

    A dad tells his partner he feels bad about his mum, and they go round in circles for half an hour. Then he says, “Actually, I feel guilty that I haven’t been up to see her since Easter, and scared she’s getting worse,” and within ten minutes they’re looking at train times to Carlisle.

    Words you could use “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a grey day.”

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the one you felt first. Anger is very often a cover for hurt or fear, because anger feels stronger and safer to stand in. We also have feelings about our feelings, like being ashamed of being sad, and that second layer can sting more than the first.

    A grandmother is curt with her daughter on the phone after the half-term visit is cancelled for the second time. Under the curtness is hurt. Under the hurt, if she let herself look, is a quieter fear that she isn’t needed much any more. She doesn’t say any of that. She says “Fine, well, another time,” and then sits by the phone for a while. Next time you feel angry, ask yourself whether there’s hurt or worry underneath. You don’t have to tell anyone what you find.

    Dig deeper: first feelings and feelings about feelings

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise and peak and settle if you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are another matter. A mood is more like weather, a background feeling that lasts hours or days and colours everything, often without you noticing it’s there.

    A woman who lives alone has a bad morning (the boiler, a bill, a dream about her ex) and by eleven has decided her whole life is going nowhere. By evening, after a walk along the canal and a long call with her sister, the same life looks ordinary and fairly okay, with a dentist’s appointment on Tuesday and a library book she wants to finish. Nothing in her life changed between eleven and seven. That’s a good reason to leave big decisions until the wave has passed: write the decision down and look at it again tomorrow.

    Things to tell yourself “This is a wave. It will peak and pass.” “Is this a feeling about now, or a mood colouring everything?”

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We pick up each other’s feelings from a tense voice, a sigh or a door shut a bit too hard, usually without noticing. That’s part of how people bond. It also means stress can move round a home or a team like a cold in February.

    A team leader comes into the Monday meeting rushed and anxious, and within ten minutes the whole team feels behind on everything. The next week she stops outside the door for two slow breaths before she goes in, and the meeting finishes on time, which in that office counts as a minor miracle. You can care about someone’s feeling without catching all of it. Notice who you tend to catch feelings from, and before your next talk with them, give yourself one minute first.

    Things to tell yourself “Is this feeling mine, or did I pick it up?” “I can stay beside their storm without standing in the middle of it.”

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Lots of people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private. “Don’t make a fuss.” “Big boys don’t cry.” “We don’t talk about that.” It’s especially common among men and older people, and in some cultures keeping your feelings to yourself is a sign of respect and strength.

    It isn’t a flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every single day by doing things: fixing the tap, driving someone to the hospital at two in the morning, simply turning up. Caring by doing is real caring. Words can be added slowly, a few at a time, if you want them.

    A retired builder whose wife has died has never talked about his feelings in his life. On the touchline at his grandson’s Saturday match, during a dull bit of the first half, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder, and they watch the second half together without saying much else.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    If one of those fits, you could say it once this week, to one person or just out loud in the car. A text counts, and so does writing it down.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually comes with an urge attached, to shout or run or hide or check or fix or text. You don’t choose the feeling. You have rather more say over the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to bring it back stronger, and acting on every urge can hurt people, so the workable middle is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    A father hears glass go and looks out to see his son standing very still in the garden next to a football. He feels a hot rush of anger, says, “I’m too cross to talk right now. Let’s sort this out in ten minutes,” and goes to fetch the dustpan. If there’s one urge you often act on too fast, like the sharp text, make yourself a rule to wait ten minutes before you send it.

    Words to try “I’m feeling a lot right now. I’m going to take a minute before I answer.” “I want to reply when I’m calmer.”

In everyday life

If you live on your own, there’s nobody to notice how you are, so a one-word check-in with yourself each day matters more (see On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own). With a partner, “What are you feeling?” can sound like a test you might fail, while “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is much easier to answer. At work, a short “I’m a bit flat today, and I’m not upset with anyone” can stop a whole team guessing. And if someone close to you shows care by doing, say by clearing the drive on a bad day, you can thank them for it in words. They may not know what to do with it the first time.

Common mix-ups

Some feelings are painful and some actions are harmful, but a feeling in itself isn’t bad; it’s information. Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid either. Treat a feeling as a signal to check before you obey it. Lots of people expect to be able to name what they feel straight away, and plenty can’t, some of them most days, which is why body words and numbers count as answers here. The last one is the most stubborn. We think a face tells us what someone feels, but faces are a poor guide. People show the same feeling in very different ways, and asking is both kinder and more accurate than guessing.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can land like a label, while “You seem upset. Am I reading that right?” leaves room for them to correct you. Then help the feeling make sense (“Of course you’re upset, that was a lot”), which helps more than advice and doesn’t mean you agree with everything they did. Offer a choice between ideas and just listening; a lot of people only want the second, and are relieved to be asked. Accept other ways of saying it, like a number, a text, a drawing or “I’m at a seven”. And settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go to them, so they don’t end up looking after your feelings as well as their own.

You could say “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”

When to get more help

Everyone has hard days and big feelings. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or another trained professional if:

  • a low, numb, anxious or angry feeling stays most days for two weeks or more;
  • feelings are stopping you from sleeping, eating, working or seeing people;
  • you are using alcohol, drugs or other things to stop feeling;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that’s happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. You deserve help today.

If you feel afraid of someone you live with or are close to, that fear is an important signal. It is not something to “manage” on your own. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help.

Thursday, 11:20pm

You sent the wrong file to the whole team, last quarter’s figures instead of this one’s. Priyanka spotted it within four minutes, sent the right one round and said “no worries!” with an exclamation mark. Hours later you’re still awake and replaying it, and the voice in your head has moved on from the file altogether. It’s telling you that you’re useless, and that everyone knows it now.

This chapter is about how self-worth and shame work, and then about what you can do with them, on your own, at home, with friends or at work. None of it is a diagnosis. Take what helps.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    A lot of us carry a quiet scoreboard. We’re worth something when we’re useful, or liked, or slim, or busy, or top of the class, or never any bother. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth”, and the trouble with them is that any score can drop. If your worth goes up and down with each day’s results, every bad day feels like a verdict on you as a person.

    Pam taught Year 4 for forty years. In the September after she retires she feels lost in a way she can’t explain to anyone, wandering round the big Tesco at ten on a weekday morning. Nothing is wrong. For four decades being needed was how she knew she mattered, and the bell has stopped ringing. Try finishing the line “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times, and look at what you wrote; those are your scores. Noticing them is plenty for now.

    Dig deeper: worth that doesn’t have to be earned

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points somewhere useful: say sorry, put it right. Shame is about who you are, which feels impossible to fix, and so it makes people hide, freeze, blame someone else or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is the one more likely to lead to repair.

    A twelve-year-old knocks his mum’s favourite mug off the side, the one with the hare on it from Whitby. Guilt tells him to own up and offer some of his birthday money towards a new one. Shame tells him he ruins everything, and he wraps the pieces in yesterday’s newspaper and pushes them to the bottom of the kitchen bin. So next time you feel bad about something, ask which one it is. If it has turned into who you are, try to shrink it back to the one thing you did, and then ask what would make that thing right.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    You feel shame in the body before you think it: a hot face, eyes down, a sinking stomach, the wish for the floor to open. It can also come out sideways as sudden anger or a closed, cold face, which is how a small comment starts a big fight. In a meeting someone asks, mildly enough, “Didn’t we already cover this?” and his colleague snaps back at him. That evening she realises the snap had nothing to do with the question; she had felt stupid in front of everyone.

    It helps to learn your own shame signs. Next time you suddenly want to disappear, or to bite, you can name it quietly to yourself, and sometimes say it out loud: “I think I felt embarrassed just then, and it came out sharp. Can I try again?”

    Dig deeper: how shame works in the body

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh inner voice usually started as protection. If you criticise yourself first, perhaps nobody else can get in a blow. If you’re hard enough on yourself, perhaps you’ll never fail again. It meant well. But a voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, and it’s exhausting to live with.

    A young dad calls himself an idiot every time he forgets something for the baby, the wipes, the rabbit she won’t sleep without. It’s the word his own father used about him. The voice he hears in the car park at the nursery is an old one, borrowed years ago and never given back. Write down one thing your critic says often and ask whether you’d say it to a friend you love. Some people find it helps to answer the critic directly: “Thanks for trying to protect me. I can take it from here.”

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you’d treat a good friend who’s struggling. One well-known model has three parts: kindness in place of attack, remembering that everyone struggles, and noticing the pain without drowning in it. Studies, such as one by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012), suggest that people who are kind to themselves after a failure are often more likely to try to improve.

    Lindy fails her driving test for the third time, on the reverse round a corner again. In the test centre car park she tells herself that this is hard, that loads of people need more goes, and that she’ll practise that one manoeuvre on the industrial estate on Sunday mornings when it’s empty. Then she books another lesson. If it feels okay, put a hand on your chest or arm once today and say, slowly, “This is a hard moment. Other people feel this too. What do I need right now?”

  6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

    Everyone measures themselves against other people; it’s how we work out where we stand. Feeds full of other people’s best moments make the measuring wildly unfair, though, and self-esteem that depends on being better than others swings about a great deal.

    Two old friends meet for a curry, one with a new house, the other renting a room in a shared flat. The renter spends the whole evening quietly ranking herself and misses what’s in front of her, which is that her friend is lonely in the new house and had wanted to talk about it. When you catch yourself comparing today, try swapping it for a question about the other person, like “How are you, really?” You’re comparing your inside with their outside, after all.

  7. You can own a mistake without drowning in it.

    Owning a mistake gets much easier once it doesn’t mean you’re worthless. A simple shape helps. Say what you did, say what it did to the other person, say what you’ll do now, and stop. Beating yourself up afterwards proves nothing about how sorry you are; it just moves the attention back to you.

Think of one small thing you still feel bad about and write your own three-part sentence. Then decide whether to send it or let it rest.

  8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they’re treated, and adults never quite stop learning it. The words matter. “You did a careless thing” leaves room to change, while “you’re so careless” shuts the door. Praise for effort and for who someone is, as well as for results, helps people feel they matter on bad days too.

    A team lead starts every project review by asking what the team learned rather than who got it wrong. Within six months, people are raising problems in week two instead of week ten, which saves everyone a great deal of Friday evening. Today you could say one thing to someone that has nothing to do with their results, even just “I’m glad I know you.”

In everyday life

Shame turns up in very ordinary places. Between partners, “You forgot to call” can land as “You’re a terrible partner”, and so the reply comes out as a defence instead of a sorry. A child who hears “You’re so naughty” learns something about who they are, while “That was a naughty thing to do” leaves them a way back. A friend who says yes to everything may be quietly burning out, because saying no feels like risking being dropped. At work, someone may sit on a small error for weeks because owning it feels like proof they don’t belong there.

On your own: a kinder voice to keep

You don’t need anyone else to start this. Notice how you talk to yourself and practise one gentler sentence. Catch the critic’s favourite words (“always”, “useless”, “pathetic”, “should”) and swap one of them for a plain fact, so “I’m hopeless” becomes “I was late twice this week.” On hard days keep a short list of what you did well, or simply did, and let getting up count. You’re allowed to be disappointed in what you did and still be on your own side.

Common mix-ups

People worry that self-compassion is self-pity. They feel different from the inside: self-pity says “only me, poor me”, while self-compassion says “this is hard, and other people go through it too”, which connects you to others rather than shutting you off. Others fear that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. The research leans the other way, because fear of your own attack is what makes people avoid trying and hide mistakes. And self-worth doesn’t mean thinking you’re great. It means you matter on the days you aren’t.

If you want to help someone else with this

Talk about what happened, never about what kind of person they are. Arguing with someone’s shame (“You’re not useless!”) can feel like being told they’re wrong to feel it, so try “It sounds like you feel really low about it. I don’t see you that way.” Helping them find one small thing they can do gives the feeling somewhere to go. And kindness on ordinary days, when nothing has gone wrong, teaches worth better than anything said in a crisis.

You could say “That sounds like it’s weighing on you. Want to work out one thing that would help?” “You don’t have to earn being cared about.”

Everyone feels shame and self-doubt sometimes. It’s worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or therapist if harsh thoughts about yourself are there most days, if they stop you working, eating, sleeping or seeing people, or if you feel you deserve to be hurt. Saturday, 3:00pm

It’s your aunt’s seventieth in the function room above the cricket club, and you’re standing quietly by the cake when she announces, loud enough for the whole room, that you were always the shy one. Everyone laughs. You smile along while something stings. You’re forty-two. You stand in front of thirty children every weekday morning at twenty to nine. So are you still the shy one? Were you ever?

We describe each other by personality all the time (laid-back, a worrier, the organised one) and descriptions like those have a way of hardening into boxes. This chapter is about how personality actually works, and what stays put and what moves. Two tools elsewhere build on it: the “How to talk to me” card and the style quiz (Chapter 2, page ) describe how you lean on a few dials, which is more use than knowing which box someone put you in, and One owner per job (Book 4, Chapter 2, page 27) arranges the jobs around people’s differences so nobody has to become someone else to share a home.

  1. Traits work like dials.

    Personality is the usual pattern in how you think and feel and act. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”: openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organised and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are).

    The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them well. Each one runs from low to high, and most people sit somewhere near the middle of most of them. That’s the bit the online quizzes tend to leave out. Two friends do one on a train and get told, with great confidence, that one is an introvert and the other an extravert; when they look at the actual scores, they’re four points apart and both near the middle. If you’re curious, mark where you think you sit on each dial from one to ten, then ask where a close friend would put you. The gap is often the interesting part.

    Dig deeper: the five dials

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality you can see from very early on. Some babies are very active and some are calm. Some settle easily into new routines and some take their time. Some react strongly to noise or change. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences and the idea of “goodness of fit”, meaning how well a child’s temperament fits what the people and places around them expect of it.

    A toddler who is slow to warm up cries at every new playgroup. Her mum stops trying to coax her into the circle and lets her watch from her lap for ten minutes instead, a bit awkwardly, in a church hall that smells of squash. By the third week the little girl climbs down on her own and goes to the sand table.

    You could say “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made.”

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality describes a pattern, and patterns have exceptions. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, or endlessly patient with toddlers and very short with call centres. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across lots of situations. It tells you much less about this one.

    A manager decides that a new colleague is cold. Then at the team’s charity football match she watches the same colleague in a borrowed bib, chatty, shouting encouragement at the goalkeeper, and on Monday she sits looking at the open-plan office wondering how much of what she’d seen was the room. Before you decide what someone is “like”, try to see them somewhere else, or at least ask them about somewhere else.

    Dig deeper: person and situation

  4. Introvert and extravert is about energy, not liking people.

    People who are more introverted often like people very much. What they find is that busy, noisy social time uses up energy, and they need quiet afterwards to get it back. People who are more extraverted tend to be topped up by company and busy places. Most of us are a mix, sometimes called an ambivert, and every point on the dial just needs different things to feel at its best.

    At about eleven at a wedding in a barn near Hexham, one partner wants to go on to the after-party in the village pub and the other wants bed and silence. So one goes for an hour and the other walks back to the hotel, and they meet at breakfast, one of them a bit hoarse, both of them happy.

    Words you could use “I loved tonight, and I’m out of social energy.” “Shall we go together, and I’ll head home at ten?”

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable. It also moves. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organised, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness went on growing right across the lifespan. Big life events play a part, and so do the roles people take on and who they love.

    A man who was a nervous, disorganised student, the one who lost his keys every fortnight, is a calm, steady nurse in his forties, and his old school friends barely recognise him at the reunion. When they ask, he says that life kept asking him to cope and he slowly learned how. Write down one way you’ve changed in the last ten years, and what helped it happen.

    Thoughts you could try “Who I was at fifteen isn’t who I have to be now.” “People I knew years ago may have changed too.”

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    If you want to change a trait, you can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways again and again. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended to move in the direction they wanted over sixteen weeks. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially towards more emotional steadiness.

    A woman who wants to be less shy at work makes one small plan: say one thing in each team meeting, even if it’s only agreeing with someone. For the first fortnight her heart pounds every time. After two months speaking up has become ordinary, and she has started to forget it was ever a plan. Pick one small action that fits who you’d like to be more of, like replying to messages the same day or saying hello first, and do it three times this week. Book 2, Chapter 6 (page 105) has more on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    There’s no good personality. Each trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are kind and easy to be with, and may find no almost impossible to say. Very conscientious people are the ones you’d trust with your keys, and they may struggle to rest. People high in openness love new things and can be bored rigid by routine.

    A very organised woman spends most of a year irritated by her relaxed housemate, his trainers in the hall and his “it’ll be fine”. Then the boiler packs in on the coldest night in January, and he’s the one who stays calm, finds an emergency plumber at ten o’clock and makes everyone hot water bottles from the kettle. Pick one trait of yours that people sometimes complain about, and write down a time it helped.

    Things to tell yourself “What is the gift in this trait, and what is the cost?” “What they do that annoys me might be the flip side of something I value.”

  8. Differences are usually about fit.

    When two people with different personalities share a home or a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one can both be right in their own way, and so can a planner and someone spontaneous, or a talker and someone who needs quiet. What helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it, without either of you being the problem. Personality is also different from neurodivergence and from mental health conditions; if something feels bigger than personality, Chapter 7 (page ) may help.

    A couple argue about every single holiday, because one wants every hour planned and the other wants to wander about and see what happens. This year they plan the mornings and leave the afternoons free. They still argue about the mornings. I’d count that as progress.

    Ways to say it “I think we’re just made differently here. What would work for both of us?” “I’m a planner and you’re not. Can we plan half and leave half?”

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you build a life that suits you, with about the right amount of company and quiet. “I’m just not a people person” might be true, or it might be an old label worth testing at least once. With a partner, a shared plan (one of you does the bills, the other the holidays) usually works better than trying to change each other. A quiet friend who hardly texts may still care a great deal, so ask what kind of contact suits them. A child’s temperament says nothing about good or bad parenting, and adjusting to fit them is a skill you learn. And family roles like “the clever one” or “the difficult one” can stick long after they stop fitting. You’re allowed to grow out of them, even if your aunt hasn’t noticed.

Common mix-ups

You aren’t simply an introvert or an extravert. Most people are somewhere in the middle, and where you sit can shift with the setting and your mood. Personality isn’t set in stone by adulthood either; it keeps moving slowly across the whole of life. Online type quizzes are good for fun and conversation, and that includes the website’s own Wave Code, which shows four leanings on four dials, but they sort people into a handful of types when real people are spread along each dial. “They’re just difficult” often means “they’re different from me”, and it can hide stress or illness or a need nobody has asked about. And practising something new isn’t being fake. It feels unnatural at first, the way new shoes do, and becomes part of you over time.

If you want to help someone else with this

Drop the old labels. Notice if you still describe someone the way they were years ago, and look for who they are now. Ask instead of guessing: “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” will tell you more than any theory about their personality. Notice growth out loud, because “You handled that so calmly” can show someone a change they hadn’t spotted in themselves. And describe the action rather than the person. “You’re so uptight” or “You’re just lazy” closes doors that a plain description of what happened would leave open.

Words to try “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits any more.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”

When to get more help

Personality differences are normal and don’t need fixing. It may be worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or psychologist if:

  • a pattern in how you feel, think or relate to people keeps causing you or others real distress, across many parts of life;
  • you feel very unsure who you are, or your moods swing so much it’s hard to cope;
  • worry, low mood or fear of people is stopping you from living the life you want.

Please don’t diagnose yourself or anyone else with a “personality disorder” from what you read online. Those are clinical terms that only a qualified professional can use, after a proper assessment.

A personality trait never excuses controlling, frightening or hurtful behaviour. If you’re afraid of someone close to you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help.

Monday, 8:15am

You ask him for the third time to just put his shoes on. Your son is lying on the hall floor with his hands over his ears, because the new socks feel wrong, and you’re already late. To him the seam across his toes feels like sandpaper, and the alarm clock from the bedroom, which nobody has switched off, is still ringing inside his head.

This chapter is for neurodivergent people, diagnosed or not, and for the people who live and work with them. It explains how these minds work and then what helps. It can’t diagnose anyone, and only you get to choose the words you use for yourself. I should say that some of it is personal: I’m autistic, with ADHD in the mix too, and the About page on the website tells that story. I’ve tried to write it the way I’d want it written about me.

Two tools elsewhere rest on it. The “How to talk to me” card (Chapter 2, page ) works because different minds need different input, and saying things plainly helps almost everyone. One owner per job and the written job list (Book 4, Chapter 2, page 27) move memory out of anyone’s head and onto paper, which helps ADHD and autistic minds most and everybody a bit.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    Brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with other people. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what gets treated as typical. A different mind isn’t a broken one. Differences can still be disabling, though, especially in a world built for one kind of mind, and both of those things are true at once.

    A man who struggled all through school finds out at forty that he’s dyslexic. Mostly what he feels is relief, sitting in the car outside the assessment centre, because for thirty years he had half believed the teachers who called him lazy in red pen.

    Thoughts you could try “Maybe this isn’t a flaw. Maybe it’s a difference that needs a different setup.”

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally. Many have deep, focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely than others do, and find predictability calming. Autism doesn’t run along a single line from “mild” to “severe”. Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change from day to day, and misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    An autistic analyst tells a colleague that his slides have three typos, one on slide four and two on the summary. She means it as help; it’s what she would want to be told. He hears criticism. They are using different rules for what kindness sounds like, and neither rule is wrong.

    You could say “I like it when people say exactly what they mean. You can be direct with me.” “Do you want my honest view, or just support right now?”

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD often have plenty of attention. The hard part is pointing it where they choose. Boring tasks can feel impossible while interesting ones swallow whole evenings, time can feel slippery (there’s “now” and there’s “not now”), and feelings can arrive fast and big. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself, and it has nothing to do with effort or care.

    A woman with ADHD forgets her best friend’s birthday entirely, then spends six hours making her a card with pressed flowers from the garden, glued on with tweezers. The forgetting and the six hours come from the same brain. If something keeps falling out of your head, move it into the world: an alarm with a label, a note on the door, a basket by the exit.

    Words you could use “I care about this. My brain drops things. A reminder really helps, and it doesn’t annoy me.”

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise or a scratchy label. Others need more input, like movement, strong flavours or firm pressure. Overload is a body at its limit, which is a very different thing from being fussy.

    Two housemates fall out over the kitchen radio, because one finds silence eerie and the other finds Radio 2 at breakfast physically painful. They settle on the radio in the mornings until nine and headphones after that, and the arrangement has lasted two years. Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you and one that calms you, and change one small thing today.

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, and none of them measures how clever someone is. Plenty of people have a “spiky” profile, very strong in some areas and struggling in others. The right tools can level the ground a good deal: audiobooks, say, or extra time.

    A fourteen-year-old with dyspraxia is called clumsy on the football pitch every Wednesday afternoon, and in the same term he writes and directs the school play. Ask someone you live or work with how they find it easiest to take in information, and then use that.

    Words to try “I learn better when I can listen than when I read. Could you send a voice note instead?”

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences: copying others, forcing eye contact, holding back movements, rehearsing small talk in the car. This is called masking. It can get someone through a day. It also uses a great deal of energy, and over years it can wear a person down into exhaustion and burnout. I spent years masking, and for a long time the cost was invisible even to me, which is rather the point of a mask.

    A student seems completely fine all day at college, chatty in the canteen, helpful in class. She gets home, goes straight to her room, and can’t speak for an hour while she recovers from a day of performing. Notice one place where you can drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with, and spend a bit more time there.

    You could say “I need some quiet time after work. It’s how I recharge, and I’m not upset with you.”

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and it often lasts months. Rest helps, and so do fewer demands and less masking. Pushing harder usually makes it worse. If you think you might be there, list three demands you could drop or hand to someone else for a month. If it goes on, or you feel hopeless, talk to a doctor.

    Words you could use “I’m in burnout. What I need for a while is fewer demands.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much, and it can look like crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version: going still, losing speech, not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload. What helps is less of everything, so less noise, fewer words, fewer questions, and more space and safety. Talking can wait, sometimes until the next day. On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Ways to say it (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds, like rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing or fiddling with something. Most people stim a little. (Watch any meeting and count the jiggling feet.) For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping. It’s worth noticing what your own hands do when you’re stressed or focused or happy, because that’s yours.

    Words to try “Rocking helps me think. It doesn’t mean anything’s wrong.” “You don’t need to stop. It’s fine with me.”

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds, often autistic and ADHD ones, go deep into one thing at a time. That focus can be a real gift. It brings real expertise, and a lot of joy. It also makes switching hard. Being interrupted can feel like being pulled out of water, and a warning before a change helps enormously.

    A dad calls his daughter down to dinner three times and gets crosser each time; the fish fingers are going cold. The next evening he gives her a ten-minute warning and then a five, and she comes to the table on time, still talking about the castle she’s building and the problem with the drawbridge.

    You could say “Dinner is in ten minutes. I’ll come and get you at five to.” “Can you give me a warning before we switch?”

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by and engaged with their special interests reported higher wellbeing, though very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing. Ask someone about their favourite subject and let them talk for five minutes without steering. It costs you five minutes, and it’s one of the warmest things you can do.

    Words you could use “Can I tell you about this for five minutes? It’s how I unwind.” “Tell me more. What’s the best bit?”

  12. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, explicit words help everyone: say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. And changes made for one person (a quiet space, a written agenda, flexible hours) usually help a lot of other people too.

Within a month most of the team say the meetings have got shorter, and two people who never spoke in them have started to. Today, swap one “how I work best” fact with someone: tell them one thing that helps you, and ask for one that helps them.

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, and sometimes support and a community too. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is to ask, use the words the person chooses, and never try to diagnose somebody else, least of all in an argument.

    During a row about the washing-up, a man tells his partner, “You’re so autistic.” It lands as an insult and ends the conversation. The next morning, over toast, he apologises and asks what his partner actually needs from him, which is the conversation they should have had the night before. If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed and choose one small next step, like talking to someone or asking a doctor.

    Words to try “How do you like to describe yourself?” “I’m wondering if I might be neurodivergent. I’d like to find out more.”

    Dig deeper: diagnosis, identity and words

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they’re autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties, fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed the way they understood their own lives. Relief and grief and joy can all turn up together, in the same afternoon. If that’s you, look for one autistic-led group or online space, and give yourself time.

    You could say “I’ve just found out I’m autistic. I’m still working out what it means.” “That explains so much about my life.”

    Dig deeper: diagnosis later in life

  15. Getting things started and finished is a skill of its own.

    Planning, starting, switching, remembering what you were doing and keeping track of time are called executive function. Many autistic and ADHD people find some of these harder, and harder on some days than others. A review of 235 studies by Demetriou and colleagues (2018) found differences in executive function in autism on average, with a lot of variation between people. The brain’s self-management system is working differently. Laziness doesn’t come into it.

    A woman can write a brilliant forty-page report for her board but cannot, for three weeks, get herself to post one parcel. Once the parcel sits by the front door with a yellow sticky note on it and a phone alarm set for Tuesday at 8.10, it goes on Tuesday. Pick one task that keeps not happening and make the first step tiny and visible. See routines that help and Wired differently.

    Words you could use “Could you send me that as a list? Spoken steps fall out of my head.” “I’m not ignoring it. Starting is the hard bit for me.”

  16. When demands feel like a threat.

    Some people, often autistic, feel strong anxiety when asked or expected to do things, even things they want to do. Avoiding the demand can look like refusing, joking, changing the subject or a meltdown. The profile was first described as “pathological demand avoidance,” or PDA, by Newson and colleagues (2003).

    It isn’t in the main diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it. A review by Kildahl and colleagues (2021) found the evidence limited and mostly based on parent reports. Many people who identify with it prefer the phrase “persistent drive for autonomy.” Whatever it’s called, what helps tends to be the same: less pressure, and requests that feel like invitations. A boy who digs in at “Put your coat on now” puts it on quite happily when his dad says, “It’s cold out. Coat or jumper, your choice.”

    Ways to say it “I wonder if we could…” “No rush. Which would you rather?” (for yourself) “Being told makes it harder. Can I choose when?”

    Dig deeper: demand avoidance, carefully

  17. Not recognising faces isn’t rudeness.

    Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, means finding it hard to recognise faces, sometimes even close friends seen out of context. It can be something you’re born with, and it often runs in families (NHS). Many autistic people also find remembering faces harder, as a review by Weigelt and colleagues (2012) found. People who don’t recognise you may be relying on your voice, your hair, your coat or where they usually see you.

    A man walks straight past his neighbour in the big Sainsbury’s without a word, and she goes home feeling snubbed. When he explains later that he didn’t recognise her without her spaniel, she starts saying hello first, with her name, and they get on better than they did before.

    Words to try “I’m bad with faces, so please say who you are when you say hi.” (to someone else) “Hi, it’s Jo from next door.”

    Dig deeper: face blindness

  18. Not speaking isn’t the same as having nothing to say.

    Some autistic people speak rarely, sometimes or not at all, and some lose speech when stressed. Many communicate through AAC (augmentative and alternative communication), which can mean typing, letter boards, picture cards, apps or sign. Using AAC doesn’t stop speech developing: a review by Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) found it did not hold speech back, and may help it. The safest place to start is to presume competence, which means assuming the person understands and has something to say, and giving them the time and the tools to say it.

    At a family Sunday lunch, a non-speaking teenager types on her tablet. Her uncle used to talk about her across the table as if she weren’t there. Now he waits for her reply and answers her, a bit slower than he’d like, which is fine. Wait longer than feels natural. It will feel very long. It isn’t.

    You could say “Take your time. I’ll wait.” “Would you rather type or point?” (for yourself) “I can’t talk right now. I can type.”

    Dig deeper: non-speaking people and AAC

  19. Telling friends and dates is your choice.

    Whether to tell someone you’re autistic or ADHD, and when, is up to you. Telling can bring understanding. In a study by Sasson and Morrison (2019), people formed better first impressions of autistic adults when they knew they were autistic, especially if they knew more about autism.

    People don’t always respond well, though, and interviews by Au-Yeung and colleagues (2025) describe autistic adults weighing hope for support against fear of not being believed. Many share a little at a time, starting with what helps and leaving the label for later. On a second date, a woman says, “I’m autistic, so I’m pretty direct, and I’d rather walk than sit in a loud bar.” Her date admits he hates loud bars too, and they walk along the canal as far as the lock and back.

    Words you could use “I’m autistic. For me that means…” “I won’t always pick up hints, so please just tell me.” (if someone shares with you) “Thanks for telling me. What helps?”

    Dig deeper: disclosure in friendships and dating

  20. Finding your people.

    Lots of autistic people describe meeting other autistic people as a turning point, because at last they don’t have to translate or explain or mask. The research backs this up. Cooper, Smith and Russell (2017) found that autistic people who felt more connected to other autistic people had higher self-esteem, and through that, less anxiety and depression. Being autistic can be an identity and a community as well as a diagnosis.

    After a late diagnosis, a man joins an online group of autistic adults who love old maps, the Ordnance Survey kind with the hand-drawn hachures. For the first time in years a long conversation leaves him with energy to spare.

    Things to tell yourself “There are people who work like me.” “I can belong somewhere without masking.”

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing how your mind works helps you build a life that fits it, and spot burnout before it’s on top of you (see Get to know your wiring, spreadloveandacceptance.com/self-path). Partners can swap “how I work best” notes about plans, messages, touch and time alone. With children, behaviour often has a sensory or overload reason, so “What’s too much right now?” is worth asking before “Why are you doing this?” A friend who cancels at the last minute may just be out of energy, and offering something easier, like a walk, keeps the door open. At work, written agendas, clear deadlines and quiet spaces help far more people than the ones they were made for.

If you have ADHD and share a home, make the chore tools lighter. Log three days instead of seven, with a tally on the fridge. Own a few jobs completely, each with a labelled alarm, instead of helping a bit with everything. Forgetting is a setup problem to fix together. More in the full chapter.

Common mix-ups

“Everyone’s a bit autistic” is probably the commonest of these. Everyone shares some traits, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life, and the phrase makes real needs sound small. ADHD doesn’t just mean not sitting still; many people with it, girls and women especially, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside. “They seem fine to me” overlooks how much masking can hide, and seeming fine can cost someone their whole evening. Adjustments aren’t special treatment. They remove a barrier, the way glasses do. And the idea that autistic people lack empathy is simply out of date: many feel other people’s feelings strongly, and the differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

Believe them about what’s hard and what helps, even when it’s different for you. Be clear: say what you mean, give times, write down anything important. Ask what helps, because every neurodivergent person is different and their answer will beat any list, this one included. Protect their energy; quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places matter more than they look. And enjoy their interests. Listening to someone talk about what they love is about the warmest thing you can do for them.

Ways to say it “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you wonder whether a difference is part of ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, for you or someone you care for, a doctor, school support staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so start early and write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has free, private help.

Questions people ask

Some questions this book tends to raise, with short answers and where to read more.

Do I need to remember my childhood clearly for the first chapter to help?

Most people remember a handful of scenes and a general feeling, and that is plenty. The first chapter asks about the rules your home ran on, such as how feelings were handled or who did the worrying. Those rules tend to show up in how you react today more than in any one memory, so start with one rule you can name. Where your lens came from (Chapter 1, page ) walks you through it.

What if looking back brings up something painful?

Stop there, and come back another day if you want to. Nobody has to finish a page. If old memories keep pulling you under, or you find yourself avoiding more and more of everyday life, talk to a doctor or counsellor. Worry, low mood and trauma (Book 2, Chapter 3, page 49) describes the kinds of help there are, and the help page at the front of this book (page ) lists free places to start.

I think I might be autistic or have ADHD. Can this book tell me?

It can’t, and nothing in it is a test. Minds that work differently (Chapter 7, page ) describes how some minds work, so that you might recognise yourself or someone you love, and it says who can talk you through an assessment if you want one. Plenty of people find the descriptions useful whether or not they ever look for a diagnosis.

Can a grown-up really change their personality?

A little, slowly, and mostly by practising the thing you want to be more like. Personality, temperament and change (Chapter 6, page ) treats traits as dials that move a few notches over the years. Every setting has its gifts and its costs, so the aim is a better fit with your life more than a new you.

My partner isn’t interested in any of this. Is it still worth reading?

Yes. This book is about you, and it works if you are the only person in your home who ever opens it. Knowing your own wiring and weather makes it easier to say what you need, and the people around you often notice the change before they notice the book. If you later want to look at things together, Book 3 and Book 4 are written for that.

How do I know when a book isn’t enough?

Most chapters have a short part called “When to get more help”, with the signs to look out for. If you need someone now, the help page at the front of this book (page ) has numbers to call. Help beyond this book lists counsellors, helplines and support groups, and most of them are free.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Alexithymia
Finding it hard to notice, name or describe your own feelings. It is a trait on a scale, not an illness, and it is more common in some autistic people. Naming body signals first can help.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, how full your stress tank is: a fuller tank means a lower battery.
A bid
A bid is any small reach for attention or connection, like “look at this,” a sigh or a joke.
Calm-down plan
Your calm-down plan (once called the Calm-Down Kit) is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Carrier wave
The carrier wave is the feeling that travels underneath the words: the tone that arrives before the meaning does.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Double empathy
The idea, from the autistic researcher Damian Milton, that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways: each finds the other hard to read, so neither side is simply “missing” something.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
If-then plan
A small plan written as “If this happens, then I will do that”, which makes a new habit easier to start (Peter Gollwitzer).
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
Masking
Masking is hiding the way you’re wired to fit in, which can be very tiring over time.
Monotropism
A theory of autistic attention: attention gathers deeply on a few things at a time, rather than spreading thinly across many. Switching away can take real effort.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
The rulebook from home
The rulebook from home is the unwritten set of rules you grew up with, about love, upset, chores and rest, that you may still expect others to follow.
Second arrow
The extra pain we add on top of a hard moment, such as being angry at ourselves for being sad. The first arrow is the event; the second is what we tell ourselves about it (a Buddhist image).
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Wave Code
Four letters from the style quiz in How you connect, one for each of four preferences about how you take in and send words. Each letter shows where you lean on a dial today: a shorthand for preferences, not a personality type.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill in on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
