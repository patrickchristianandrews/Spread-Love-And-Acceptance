About this book, and how to use it

This book starts with you. It looks at where your way of seeing things came from, how you are wired today, how feelings work, why your worth was never a score, how personality works and how minds can work differently.

It is for anyone who wants to understand themselves a little better, at their own pace. You don’t need a partner, a problem or a diagnosis to read it, and nothing in it is a test.

Read the chapters in any order. Each is a short version of a chapter that is free to read online, where the full version has more research, more examples and more to try. The code at the end of each chapter opens it on your phone.

Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

Sunday, 10:15am

Your partner is still in bed and the kitchen is a mess. Before you have had a single thought, you feel it. Of course it’s always you. Nobody said that, but in the house you grew up in, whoever noticed the mess cleaned it, and that old rule has just made a decision on your behalf.

Across town, Ray’s house has been quiet since his wife died, and nobody will see the dishes. He still can’t sit down until every one of them is washed and put away, because a rule from the home he grew up in is still keeping watch.

  1. You’re not one thing.

    A reaction usually comes from three places at once. Part of it is how you are built, your wiring, which stays much the same. Part is what life taught you, patterns that can soften over time. And part is the kind of day you are having, which passes. This chapter is about the middle layer, the patterns life taught you, and Chapter 2 (page ) looks at the other two.

    A grandmother snaps when her grown-up son is late for Sunday lunch. Some of that is her wiring, because she has always liked things on time. Some is an old rule that lateness means you don’t care, and some is the bad night’s sleep she had. She could tell him, “Some of this is me being tired, and some of it is an old thing. Give me a minute.” The next time you react strongly, it is worth asking whether the reaction is mostly wiring, something you learned or simply today.

    Dig deeper: the three layers

  2. Your lens came from somewhere.

    Family, school, friends, culture, money at home and what you watched all taught you what to expect of yourself, of other people and of how a good partner, parent or friend behaves. By adulthood your lens no longer feels like a lens, because it feels like simply seeing. One friend grew up with big birthday parties and another with quiet ones, so a small dinner out feels hurtful to one of them and perfect to the other. Neither is being unreasonable, and “In my family this was a big deal. What was it like in yours?” usually clears the air.

    Pick one thing you think everyone does and ask someone how their family did it. Where your lens came from walks through the idea gently, and you can read one section and stop.

  3. Each stage of growing up asks its own question.

    A small child is asking whether the world is safe and whether they can do things for themselves. Later the questions become whether they are capable, where they belong and whether they can be close to someone without losing themselves. Later still, adults ask what they are building and whether their life has meant something. The ages are rough and people move at their own pace, but the answers you landed on can become understandings you keep using as an adult. Someone who moved schools four times as a teenager may still feel like the outsider at every new job, and may catch themselves thinking that part of them is still asking whether they belong.

    Read the questions again and notice which one still feels loud for you. Noticing is enough for now.

    Dig deeper: the stages

  4. When something happened matters.

    An experience that touches the question you were answering at the time tends to go deeper. A house move at four is mostly about new routines, while a move at fourteen, right in the middle of wondering where you belong, can leave a longer feeling of being the outsider. Two adult siblings remember their parents’ divorce quite differently. One was six and remembers a new bedroom and a new bus route, and the other was fifteen and remembers feeling torn in half. Both memories are true, and they go some way to explaining why the two of them still argue about that year (see Family rifts).

    Deeper isn’t permanent, though, because people keep learning all their lives. Think of one big change from growing up, and ask how old you were and what you were trying to work out at the time.

  5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts.

    Many people carry beliefs like “I’m only okay when I’m useful”, “my needs are a burden” or “mistakes mean I’m bad”. A child often can’t know what was going on for the grown-ups and only knows how it felt. As an adult you are allowed to look again with the full picture, and looking again doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine, because some things really were not okay. A foster carer who learned as a girl that her needs were a burden finds it hard to ask for respite, a planned break while someone else cares for the children, even when she is worn out. An old voice tells her not to ask, while the adult in her knows that asking is fair.

    Write down one old belief about yourself, and under it write what you would tell a friend who believed the same thing.

    Dig deeper: old answers and fairer readings

  6. An old rule can read other people for you.

    If silence at home meant anger, a partner’s quiet evening can feel like trouble on the way. An expectation that nobody ever said out loud can’t be agreed to either, so the other person can only miss it, whether it is “if you loved me, you’d just know” or “whoever notices, does it”. Each of these is somebody’s family rule. When a housemate leaves without saying goodbye, someone raised in a quiet, angry house may assume she is upset, when she may simply be late for a shift.

    This week, when you catch yourself guessing what someone’s silence means, try asking instead, perhaps with “When you went quiet earlier, I wasn’t sure what it meant. Is everything okay?”

  8. You get to choose which rules to keep.

    For each line of your rulebook, ask whether you still want that rule. When a reaction feels bigger than the moment, try a one-minute lens check once you have settled. Where did you learn this, how old does it feel, and is it true here, with this person, now? What would the adult you choose? A team leader notices that her old rule about mistakes makes her hide problems from her manager, so she writes a new one on a sticky note above her desk, saying that mistakes are information and the team fixes them together.

    Pick one rule from your list and write the version you would like to live by, in the form “I used to think ___. Now I’m choosing ___.” Later you can swap rulebooks with someone you trust and choose shared rules on purpose.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, and choosing together

  9. What you carry forward is yours.

    You couldn’t control the home you grew up in, what other people did or what you learned before you had all the facts. What is in your hands now is smaller and stronger. It includes which old rules you keep, how you treat yourself about them and where you spend your energy today, and that includes people. Give your time and care where they are met with care, and let one-sided effort shrink back to what feels fair, so that you still have something left to give.

    Someone living on their own always plans the meetups with one old friend, who never asks back. They stop chasing, send a warm “I’d love to see you. Let me know when works for you,” and find they see more of the friends who reply (see Always the one reaching out?). Is there a relationship where you give far more than comes back? You could give a little less there this month. (If you are afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has people to talk to.)

Tuesday morning

Two people get the same text, asking whether they can talk tonight. One is pleased, because she likes knowing ahead. The other feels his stomach drop, because in the home he grew up in, “we need to talk” meant trouble, and he spends the whole afternoon rehearsing answers to a conversation that turns out to be about a holiday.

You, in all your relationships

Your old rules go wherever you go, starting with how you treat yourself, since the voice you use after a mistake is often an old one. At home they decide who notices, who plans and what quiet means, and two housemates can find they are living by two rulebooks about mess, noise and guests. In families, old roles like the helper or the peacemaker switch back on at the front door. At work, someone who was praised mainly for being useful may say yes to everything and then quietly resent it, until seeing the old rule lets them pause before the next yes. After a breakup old rules can get louder, which is a good time to look at them gently (see On my own). Help by relationship lists pages for many situations.

Common mix-ups

  • “Looking back means blaming my family.” Looking back is about understanding. Many of the adults who raised us were working with their own lenses too, and if that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit.
  • “If it started in childhood, I’m stuck with it.” Many old patterns soften with awareness, safety and practice, and some leave a lasting sensitivity you learn to carry and explain.
  • “Every big reaction is an old wound.” Often it is just a full tank. Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 51) helps you tell leftover stress from this-moment stress.
  • “If they won’t look at their rules, there’s no point looking at mine.” Knowing your own rules changes how you react, even if nobody else ever reads a word of this.

A note on care: This part looks back at growing up. It is general education, not a diagnosis or therapy, and it can’t tell you what anyone has. Not every home felt calm or kind, so take only what is useful. If looking back feels heavy, you can stop and come back another day, or explore it with a qualified professional, if and when you want that. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared.

Sunday, 7:50pm

A friend asks whether you are okay, and you say you’re fine. The truth is that you can’t tell whether you are fine, tired or quietly bothered by something, and you couldn’t explain it to yourself, let alone to her.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part, made up of your temperament, your pace, your senses and how you take in words. You work with it by building a setup that suits you. Today’s conditions are the passing part, such as sleep, hunger, how much you are carrying, the room and the time of day, and you work around them by eating first, sleeping on it or talking tomorrow. Mixing the two up is where a lot of frustration comes from. (Chapter 1 (page ) covers the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    For example, a dad who works nights finds the school-run chatter unbearable at eight in the morning, and for a while he wonders whether he is cut out for parenting at all. When he swaps to the afternoon pick-up, the same chatter is fun again (see Different hours).

    You could say “It’s the hour, honestly. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

    Try this. Write down one thing that always bothers you, which is probably wiring, and one that only bothers you when you are tired.

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Every conversation runs through your own state first. Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 51) looks closely at the stress tank in a shared home, so this chapter only adds the self side, a one-minute reading on your own before you decide what a hard moment means. My weather today is the quick check, and How much are you carrying? is a fuller one on a short worksheet.

    For example, a long-distance couple notice that their difficult calls always fall at the end of one partner’s late shift, so they move serious talks to weekend mornings (see Long distance).

    Words you could use “I’m running on empty tonight. Can we pick this up tomorrow morning?”

    One state, many words. The chapters and tools use a few everyday pictures for one thing, how much room you have today. They describe the same state in different ways.

    • The three states (Book 2, Chapter 1 (page 9) calls them the ladder) are calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty.
    • My weather today uses sky words for the same three. Clear is calm and connected, gusty is revved up, and fogged in is running on empty. Its pressure (light, building or heavy) is how much you were already carrying.
    • The stress tank is the score from How much are you carrying?, from 0 to 1, where higher means fuller.
    • The battery is the other side of the tank, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery.

    “Gusty”, “revved up”, “a fairly full tank” and “a low battery” can all describe the same afternoon, so use whichever words suit you.

  4. Ask where this feeling is coming from.

    First name the feeling, perhaps as “tight, annoyed, a bit scared”. Then ask whether it is the size of the moment or feels older, which layer it belongs to, and what it needs right now, whether that is food, space, a heads-up, reassurance or a proper conversation later. It takes a minute and works best once the first wave has passed. When a coworker asks “Quick question?” and your heart races, you might name the feeling as jumpy, recognise an old boss in it as well as a busy day, and ask for five minutes before you answer.

    zqaxselfx2xnowxxhardxtoxnameqzWhen feelings are hard to name. For some people, “What are you feeling?” is a hard question most days, and on bad days it is almost impossible. They notice a racing heart or a heavy stomach but can’t tell whether it is worry, anger or hunger. Psychologists call this alexithymia, which means “no words for feelings”. It is a trait that runs from a little to a lot, it is common, especially among autistic people, and it is not an illness. The feelings are there and the words come slowly. It helps to start with the body (“tight chest, skipped lunch”), to choose between two words (“more tired or more annoyed?”), to use the three sky words in place of feeling words, and to give it time. “I’ll tell you tomorrow” is a real answer.

    One small step. On a calm day, write your one stepping-away sentence, including when you will be back.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear warns that something might be dangerous, anger that something seems unfair or in your way, and sadness that you have lost something that mattered. A signal is worth listening to, though it can be wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm can go off for burnt toast.

    For example, You feel jealous when a friend makes a new close friend. The jealousy proves nothing about you, and nothing about whether your friend is leaving. What it does show is how much the friendship matters to you.

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Many people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private, with sayings like “Don’t make a fuss”, “Big boys don’t cry” and “We don’t talk about that.” This is common for men and for older people, and in some cultures it is a sign of respect and strength.

    Keeping feelings private is no flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every day by doing, whether that is fixing the tap, driving someone to hospital or simply turning up. Caring by doing is real caring, and words can be added slowly, a few at a time.

    A retired builder whose wife has died has never talked about his feelings. On the touchline at his grandson’s football match, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder, and they watch the second half together.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    Something to try. Pick one sentence above and say it once this week, to one person or just out loud on your own. A text counts, and so does writing it down.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually comes with an urge to shout, run, hide, check, fix or text. You don’t choose the feeling, but you have more choice over what you do with the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to make it come back stronger, and acting on every urge can hurt people, so the middle path is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    For example, a father feels a hot rush of anger when his son breaks a window with a football. He says, “I’m too cross to talk right now. Let’s sort this out in ten minutes,” and goes to fetch the dustpan.

    Words to try “I’m feeling a lot right now. I’m going to take a minute before I answer.” “I want to reply when I’m calmer.”

    Try this. Pick one urge you often act on too fast, like sending a sharp text, and make a rule to wait ten minutes first.

Common mix-ups

  • “Some feelings are bad.” Some feelings are painful and some actions are harmful, but the feeling itself is information.
  • “If I feel it, it must be true.” Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid. A feeling is a signal to check.
  • “I should be able to name it straight away.” Many people find feelings hard to name, and some find it very hard. Body words, numbers and colours all count.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can feel like a label, while “You seem upset. Am I reading that right?” leaves room for them to correct you.
  • Make the feeling make sense. “Of course you’re upset, that was a lot” helps more than advice, and you can make sense of a feeling without agreeing with every action.
  • Offer choices. “Do you want ideas, or just someone to listen?” Many people only want the second.
  • Accept other ways of saying it. A number, a text, a drawing or “I’m at a seven” is a real answer.
  • Settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go to them, so they don’t have to look after your feelings too.

You sent the wrong file to the whole team. Someone noticed, fixed it and said there were no worries. Hours later you are still awake and replaying it, and the voice in your head has moved on from the file. It is telling you that you are useless, and that everyone knows it now.

This chapter looks at how self-worth and shame work and then at what you can do about them, whether you are on your own, at home, with friends or at work. Nothing here is a diagnosis, so take what helps and leave the rest.

  1. Your worth isn’t a score.

    Many of us carry a quiet scoreboard on which we are worth something only when we are useful, liked, slim, busy, top of the class or never a bother. Psychologists call these “conditions of worth”, and the trouble with them is that any score can drop. If your worth rises and falls with each day’s results, every bad day feels like a verdict on you. A retired teacher may feel lost for months after she stops working, even though nothing is wrong, because for forty years being needed was how she knew she mattered.

    It can help to finish the line “I feel okay about myself when I…” three times. The endings you write are your scores, and for now it is enough to notice them. On a bad day, you might remind yourself that you matter on days when you achieve nothing at all.

    Dig deeper: worth that doesn’t have to be earned

  2. Guilt says “I did a bad thing.” Shame says “I am bad.”

    Guilt is about something you did, so it points towards fixing it by saying sorry or making it right. Shame is about who you are, which feels impossible to fix, and so it tends to make people hide, freeze, blame someone else or lash out. Research on the two feelings, such as June Tangney and Ronda Dearing’s (2002), suggests guilt is more likely to lead to repair. When a teenager breaks his mum’s favourite mug, guilt tells him to own up and offer to help pay for a new one. Shame tells him he ruins everything, and he wraps the pieces in newspaper and hides them at the bottom of the bin.

    Next time you feel bad about something, ask whether it is about what you did or about who you are. If it has become about who you are, try to turn it back into one thing you did, and then ask what would make that thing right.

    Dig deeper: guilt, shame and repair

  3. Shame makes you want to hide.

    Shame shows up in the body first, as a hot face, lowered eyes, a sinking stomach and the wish for the floor to swallow you. It can also come out sideways, as sudden anger or a cold, closed face, which is how a small comment can start a big fight. In a meeting a coworker asks, “Didn’t we already cover this?”, and his colleague snaps back at him. Later she realises the snap had nothing to do with the question, because what she felt was stupid in front of everyone.

    Learning your own shame signs helps. Next time you suddenly want to disappear or to snap, you can name it quietly to yourself as shame, and sometimes say out loud, “I think I felt embarrassed just then, and it came out sharp. Can I try again?”

    Dig deeper: how shame works in the body

  4. The inner critic is trying to protect you.

    That harsh inner voice usually started as protection. If you criticise yourself first, perhaps nobody else can hurt you, and if you are hard on yourself, perhaps you will never fail again. It meant well. A voice that only threatens keeps your body on alert, though, and it is tiring to live with. A young dad calls himself an idiot every time he forgets something for the baby, the same word his own father used about him, so the voice he hears is an old one that he borrowed long ago.

    Write down one thing your inner critic says often, and ask whether you would say it to a friend you love, and what you would say instead. Some people find it helps to answer the critic directly, with something like “Thanks for trying to protect me. I can take it from here.”

  5. Self-compassion isn’t letting yourself off.

    Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you would treat a good friend who is struggling. One well-known model has three parts, which are kindness in place of attack, remembering that everyone struggles, and noticing the pain without drowning in it.   6. Comparing yourself pulls your worth outside you.

Feeds full of other people’s best moments make that measuring unfair, and self-esteem that depends on being better than others goes up and down a great deal.   8. Worth is learned between people, and we can give it.

    Children learn their worth from how they are treated, and adults go on learning it. How we talk to each other matters a great deal, because “you did a careless thing” leaves room to change and “you’re so careless” closes the door. Everyone laughs, and you smile along while something stings. You are forty-two and you stand in front of thirty children every weekday morning, so are you still the shy one, and were you ever?

We all describe people by their personality, as laid-back, a worrier or the organised one, and descriptions like these can harden into boxes. This chapter looks at how personality actually works, what stays the same and what changes.

  1. Traits are dials, not boxes.

    Personality is your usual pattern of thinking, feeling and acting. Many psychologists describe it with five broad traits, often called the “Big Five”. They are openness (curious about new ideas and experiences), conscientiousness (organised and careful), extraversion (drawn to people and busy places), agreeableness (warm and cooperative) and emotional stability (how steady your moods are).

    The same five turned up in work such as McCrae and Costa (1987), whether people rated themselves or were rated by people who knew them. Each one works like a dial, and most people sit somewhere near the middle of most dials.

    For example, two friends take an online quiz, and one is told she is an introvert while the other is told he is an extravert. When they look at their actual scores, they are only a few points apart, both close to the middle.

    Things to tell yourself “I’m more of this than that, some of the time.” “A label is only a summary.”

    Try this. For each of the five dials, mark where you think you sit from one to ten, and then ask yourself where a close friend would put you.

    Dig deeper: the five dials

  2. Temperament is the starting point.

    Temperament is the part of personality we can see from very early on. Some babies are very active and some calm, some settle easily into new routines while others take time, and some react strongly to noise or change. In their long-running study, Thomas and Chess (1977) described these early differences and the idea of “goodness of fit”, meaning how well a child’s temperament fits what the people and places around them expect.

    A toddler who is slow to warm up cries at every new playgroup. Her mum stops pushing her to join in and lets her watch from her lap for ten minutes first, and by the third week she climbs down and joins the sand table on her own.

    You could say “You like to watch first. That’s fine. Join in when you’re ready.” (to yourself) “I need time to warm up. That’s how I’m made.”

    This week. Think about what you were like as a small child, from what your family say or what you remember, and notice what seems the same now and what seems different.

    Dig deeper: temperament and fit

  3. Situations matter too.

    Personality describes a pattern. The same person can be loud with family and quiet at work, organised in their job and messy at home, patient with children and short with call centres. A trait tells you what someone tends to do across many situations, and much less about what they will do in this one.

    For example, a manager decides that a new colleague is cold. At the team’s charity football match, the same colleague turns out to be chatty, funny and warm, and the manager starts to wonder how much the open-plan office was shaping what she saw.

    Things to tell yourself “I’ve only seen them in one setting.” “Where am I most myself, and where am I least?”

    One small step. Before deciding what someone is “like,” try to see them in one different setting, or ask them about one.

People who are more extraverted often feel energised by company and busy places. Most of us are a mix, sometimes called an ambivert, and each kind of person simply needs different things to feel at their best.

  5. People change across life.

    Personality is fairly stable, but it can move. A large review by Roberts, Walton and Viechtbauer (2006) found that, on average, people become more organised, more emotionally steady and more confident with others as they move through adult life, with much of the change between 20 and 40. A later review by Bleidorn and colleagues (2022) found that emotional steadiness kept growing right across the lifespan. Big life events, roles and relationships all play a part.

    For example, a man who was a nervous, disorganised student becomes a calm, steady nurse in his forties, and his old school friends barely recognise him. When they ask, he says that life kept asking him to cope and he slowly learned how.

    Thoughts you could try “Who I was at fifteen isn’t who I have to be now.” “People I knew years ago may have changed too.”

    Try this. Write down one way you have changed in the last ten years, and what helped it happen.

  6. You can change on purpose, a little at a time.

    People who want to change a trait can, slowly, mostly by acting differently in small ways, again and again. In a study by Hudson and Fraley (2015), people who set goals to change one of their traits tended, over sixteen weeks, to change in the direction they wanted. A review by Roberts and colleagues (2017) found that therapy was often linked to lasting change too, especially towards more emotional steadiness.

    A woman who wants to be less shy at work makes one small plan, to say one thing in each team meeting, and after two months speaking up has started to feel ordinary.

    Things to tell yourself “I don’t have to become a different person. I can practise one different thing.”

    This week. Pick one small action that fits who you would like to be more of, such as replying to messages the same day or saying hello first, and do it three times this week. Book 2, Chapter 6 (page 107) has more on habits.

    Dig deeper: changing on purpose

  7. Every trait has gifts and costs.

    No personality is the good one. Each trait helps in some places and gets in the way in others. Very agreeable people are kind and easy to be with, and may find it hard to say no. Very conscientious people are reliable, and may struggle to rest. People high in openness love new things, and may get bored with routine. Knowing your mix helps you use the gifts and plan for the costs.

    For example, a highly organised woman is often irritated by her relaxed housemate. Then the boiler breaks on a freezing night, and he is the one who stays calm and finds a plumber at ten o’clock.

    Things to tell yourself “What is the gift in this trait, and what is the cost?” “What they do that annoys me might be the flip side of something I value.”

    One small step. Pick one trait of yours that people sometimes complain about. Write down one time it helped.

  8. Differences are about fit, not fault.

    When two people with different personalities share a home, a family or a job, friction is normal. A tidy person and a relaxed one, a planner and someone spontaneous, a talker and someone who needs quiet can each be right in their own way, and what helps is naming the difference and agreeing how to live with it. In everyday life

On your own

Knowing your own mix helps you plan a life that suits you, with the right amount of company, quiet, routine and novelty. “I’m just not a people person” might be true, or it might be an old label worth testing. With a partner, a shared plan such as one of you handling the bills and the other the holidays often works better than trying to change each other. A quiet friend who doesn’t text much may still care a great deal, so it is worth asking what kind of contact suits them. A child’s temperament says nothing about good or bad parenting, and adjusting to fit them is a skill. Family roles like “the clever one” or “the difficult one” can stick long after they stop fitting, and you are allowed to grow out of them.

Common mix-ups

  • “You’re either an introvert or an extravert.” Most people are somewhere in the middle, and it can shift with setting and mood.
  • “Personality is set by adulthood.” It keeps changing, slowly, across the whole of life.
  • “Online type quizzes tell you who you are.” Many popular quizzes sort people into a handful of types, while real people are spread along each dial. Quizzes are good for fun and conversation, and that includes the website’s own Wave Code, which shows four leanings on four dials.
  • “They’re just difficult.” “Difficult” often means “different from me”, and it can also hide stress, illness or a need nobody has asked about.
  • “Changing would mean being fake.” Practising something new feels unnatural at first and becomes part of you over time.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Drop the old labels. Notice if you still describe someone the way they were years ago, and look for who they are now.
  • Ask instead of guessing. “What helps you recharge?” or “How do you like to plan things?” tells you more than any guess from their personality.
  • Notice growth out loud. “You handled that so calmly” can help someone see a change they hadn’t noticed.
  • Describe the action. “You’re so uptight” or “You’re just lazy” closes doors, while a description of what happened leaves them open.

Words to try “I used to think of you as the shy one. I don’t think that fits any more.” “What do you need to be at your best this week?”

When to get more help

Personality differences are normal and don’t need fixing. It may be worth talking to a doctor, counsellor or psychologist if:

  • a pattern in how you feel, think or relate to people keeps causing you or others real distress, across many parts of life;
  • you feel very unsure who you are, or your moods swing so much it’s hard to cope;
  • worry, low mood or fear of people is stopping you from living the life you want.

Please don’t diagnose yourself or anyone else with a “personality disorder” from what you read online. Those are clinical terms that only a qualified professional can use, after a proper assessment.

A personality trait never excuses controlling, frightening or hurtful behaviour. If you’re afraid of someone close to you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help.

You ask him for the third time to just put his shoes on. Your son is lying on the hall floor with his hands over his ears, because the new socks feel wrong, and you are already late. To him the seam across his toes feels like sandpaper, and the alarm clock from the bedroom is still ringing in his head.

This chapter is for neurodivergent people, diagnosed or not, and for the people who live, work and learn with them. It explains how these minds work and then what helps. It can’t diagnose anyone, and only you get to choose the labels you use for yourself.

  1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    People’s brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with others. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what is treated as typical. A different mind is not a broken one, and at the same time differences can be disabling, especially when the world is built for one kind of mind.

    For example, a man who struggled all through school finds out at forty that he is dyslexic. Mostly he feels relief, because for thirty years he had half believed the teachers who called him lazy.

    Thoughts you could try “Maybe this isn’t a flaw. Maybe it’s a difference that needs a different setup.”

    Try this. Think of one thing that is hard for you and easy for others, and ask whether the difference is effort or a different kind of brain.

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally, have deep and focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely, and find predictability calming. Autism doesn’t run along a single line from “mild” to “severe”, because each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change from day to day. Misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    An autistic coworker mentions that her colleague’s slides have three typos. She means it as help, and he hears criticism, because they are using different rules for what kindness sounds like.

    You could say “I like it when people say exactly what they mean. You can be direct with me.” “Do you want my honest view, or just support right now?”

    This week. In one conversation today, say exactly what you mean without hints, and notice whether it makes things easier.

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD often have plenty of attention but find it hard to point it where they choose. Boring tasks can feel impossible while interesting ones swallow hours, time can feel slippery, divided into “now” and “not now”, and feelings can come fast and big. It is a real difference in how the brain manages itself, and it has nothing to do with effort or care.

    For example, a woman with ADHD forgets her friend’s birthday and then spends six hours making her a handmade card with pressed flowers, because the forgetting and the focus come from the same brain.

    Words you could use “I care about this. My brain drops things. A reminder really helps, and it doesn’t annoy me.”

    One small step. Move one thing you keep forgetting out of your head and into the world, with an alarm, a note on the door or a basket by the exit.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise or scratchy clothes. Others need more input, such as movement, strong flavours or pressure. Overload is a body at its limit, which is very different from being fussy.

    Two housemates argue about the kitchen radio, because one finds silence eerie and the other finds the radio painful. They settle on the radio in the mornings and headphones in the evenings.

    Ways to say it “The noise in here is too much for me right now. Can we move somewhere quieter?”

    Something to try. Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you and one that calms you, and make one small change today.

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, and none of them measures how clever someone is. Many people have a “spiky” profile, very strong in some areas and struggling in others, and the right tools, like audiobooks, spoken notes or extra time, can level the ground.

    For example, a teenager with dyspraxia is called clumsy on the football pitch, and in the same term he writes and directs the school play.

    Words to try “I learn better when I can listen than when I read. Could you send a voice note instead?”

    Try this. Ask someone you live or work with what the easiest way is for them to take in information, and then use it.

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences by copying others, forcing eye contact, holding back movements and rehearsing small talk. This is called masking. It can help someone get through a day, but it uses a lot of energy, and over years it can lead to exhaustion, anxiety and burnout.

    A student seems fine all day at college, then comes home and can’t speak for an hour while she recovers from a day of performing.

    You could say “I need some quiet time after work. It’s how I recharge, and I’m not upset with you.”

    This week. Notice one place where you can drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and often lasts months. Rest, fewer demands and less masking help. Pushing harder usually makes it worse.

    Words you could use “I’m in burnout. What I need for a while is fewer demands.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    One small step. If you think you might be in burnout, list three demands you could drop, delay or share for a month. If it goes on, or you feel hopeless, talk to a doctor.

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much, which can look like crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version, going still, losing speech and not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload. What helps is less of everything, so less noise, fewer words and fewer questions, with more space and safety. Talking can wait.

    Ways to say it (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Something to try. On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds, like rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing or fiddling with something. Most people stim a little (think of a jiggling foot). For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping.

    Words to try “Rocking helps me think. It doesn’t mean anything’s wrong.” “You don’t need to stop. It’s fine with me.”

    Try this. Notice what your own hands or body do when you’re stressed, focused or happy. That’s your stim.

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds, often autistic and ADHD ones, focus deeply on one thing at a time. That focus can be a gift, bringing real expertise, flow and joy. It also makes switching tasks hard, so being interrupted can feel like being pulled out of water, and warnings before a change help a lot.

    For example, a dad calls his daughter to dinner three times and gets more frustrated each time. The next evening he gives her a ten-minute warning and then a five-minute one, and she comes to the table still talking about the castle she has been building.

    You could say “Dinner is in ten minutes. I’ll come and get you at five to.” “Can you give me a warning before we switch?”

    This week. Before you interrupt someone who is focused, give them a heads-up and a time, and see whether it goes more smoothly.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by and engaged with their special interests reported higher wellbeing, though very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing.

    Words you could use “Can I tell you about this for five minutes? It’s how I unwind.” “Tell me more. What’s the best bit?”

    One small step. Ask someone about their favourite subject, and let them talk for five minutes without steering.

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, support and community. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is to ask and to use the words the person chooses, and never to try to diagnose someone else, especially in an argument.

    For example, during a row, a man tells his partner, “You’re so autistic.” It lands as an insult and ends the conversation. The next morning he apologises and asks what his partner actually needs from him.

    Words to try “How do you like to describe yourself?” “I’m wondering if I might be neurodivergent. I’d like to find out more.”

    Try this. If you have wondered about yourself, write down what you have noticed and decide one small next step, such as reading, talking to someone or asking a doctor.

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they are autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties, fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed how they understood their lives. Relief, grief and joy can all come together.

    You could say “I’ve just found out I’m autistic. I’m still working out what it means.” “That explains so much about my life.”

    This week. If you’ve found out later in life, look for one autistic-led group, book or online space, and give yourself time to make sense of it.

  15. Getting things started and finished is a skill of its own.

    Planning, starting, switching, remembering what you were doing and keeping track of time are called executive function. Many autistic and ADHD people find some of these harder, on some days more than others. A review of 235 studies by Demetriou and colleagues (2018) found differences in executive function in autism on average, with a lot of variation between people. The brain’s self-management system is working differently, and laziness has nothing to do with it, so it often helps to move the managing out of your head and into the world.

    A woman can write a brilliant report but can’t get herself to post a parcel. Once the parcel sits by the front door with a sticky note and a phone alarm, it goes on Tuesday.

    Words you could use “Could you send me that as a list? Spoken steps fall out of my head.” “I’m not ignoring it. Starting is the hard bit for me.”

    One small step. Pick one task that keeps not happening and make the first step tiny and visible, with a note, an alarm or the thing left where you will trip over it. See routines that help and Wired differently.

  16. When demands feel like a threat.

    Some people, often autistic, feel strong anxiety when asked or expected to do things, even things they want to do. Avoiding the demand can look like refusing, joking, distracting or a meltdown. The profile was first described as “pathological demand avoidance,” or PDA, by Newson and colleagues (2003).

    It is not in the main diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it. A review by Kildahl and colleagues (2021) found the evidence limited and mostly based on parent reports. Many people who identify with it prefer the phrase “persistent drive for autonomy.” Whatever it is called, what helps tends to be the same, which is less pressure, more choice and requests that feel like invitations.

    For example, a boy who refuses “Put your coat on now” happily puts it on when his dad says, “It’s cold out. Coat or jumper, your choice.”

    Ways to say it “I wonder if we could…” “No rush. Which would you rather?” (for yourself) “Being told makes it harder. Can I choose when?”

    Something to try. Turn one daily instruction into a choice or an invitation this week, and see what changes.

  17. Not recognising faces isn’t rudeness.

    Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, means finding it hard to recognise people’s faces, sometimes even close friends out of context. It can be something you’re born with, and it often runs in families (NHS). Many autistic people also find remembering faces harder, as a review by Weigelt and colleagues (2012) found. People who don’t recognise you may be relying on voices, hair, clothes or where they usually see you.

    A man walks past his neighbour in the supermarket without a word, and she feels snubbed. When he explains later that he didn’t recognise her without her dog, she starts saying hello first, with her name.

    Words to try “I’m bad with faces, so please say who you are when you say hi.” (to someone else) “Hi, it’s Jo from next door.”

    Try this. If faces are hard for you, tell one or two people you see often. If not, try saying your name when you greet someone out of context.

    Dig deeper: face blindness

  18. Not speaking isn’t the same as having nothing to say.

    Some autistic people speak rarely, sometimes, or not at all, and some lose speech when stressed. Many communicate through AAC (augmentative and alternative communication), which can mean typing, letter boards, picture cards, apps or sign. Using AAC doesn’t stop speech developing, and a review by Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) found it did not hold speech back, and may help it. The safest starting point is to presume competence, which means assuming the person understands and has something to say, and giving them the time and tools to say it.

    For example, at a family meal, a non-speaking teenager types on her tablet. Her uncle used to talk about her in front of her, and now he waits, reads her reply and answers her directly.

    You could say “Take your time. I’ll wait.” “Would you rather type or point?” (for yourself) “I can’t talk right now. I can type.”

    This week. Talk to the person directly, and wait longer than feels natural for an answer.

    Dig deeper: non-speaking people and AAC

  19. Telling friends and dates is your choice.

    Whether to tell someone you’re autistic or ADHD, and when, is up to you. Telling can bring understanding. In a study by Sasson and Morrison (2019), people formed better first impressions of autistic adults when they knew they were autistic, especially if they knew more about autism.

    But people don’t always respond well, and interviews by Au-Yeung and colleagues (2025) describe autistic adults weighing hope for support against fear of not being believed. Many people share a little at a time, starting with what helps before any label.

    On a second date, a woman says, “I’m autistic, so I’m pretty direct, and I’d rather walk than sit in a loud bar.” Her date admits that he hates loud bars too, and they walk along the canal instead.

    Words you could use “I’m autistic. For me that means…” “I won’t always pick up hints, so please just tell me.” (if someone shares with you) “Thanks for telling me. What helps?”

    One small step. Write one sentence about what helps you, without a label, and practise it. You can add the label later if and when you want to.

  20. Finding your people.

    Many autistic people describe finding other autistic people as a turning point, because at last they don’t have to translate, explain or mask. Research backs this up. Cooper, Smith and Russell (2017) found that autistic people who felt more connected to other autistic people had higher self-esteem, and through that, less anxiety and depression. Being autistic can be an identity and a community as well as a diagnosis.

    For example, after a late diagnosis, a man joins an online group for autistic adults who love old maps, and for the first time in years a long conversation leaves him with energy to spare.

    Things to tell yourself “There are people who work like me.” “I can belong somewhere without masking.”

    Something to try. Look for one autistic-led group, book, podcast or online space about something you love.

In everyday life

On your own

Knowing how your mind works helps you build a life that fits it and spot burnout early (see Get to know your wiring). Partners can swap “how I work best” notes about plans, messages, touch and time alone. With children, behaviour often has a sensory or overload reason, so “What’s too much right now?” is worth asking before “Why are you doing this?” A friend who cancels at the last minute may simply be out of energy, and offering an easier option, like a walk or a message, keeps the door open. At work, written agendas, clear deadlines and quiet spaces help many people beyond the neurodivergent ones.

Common mix-ups

  • “Everyone’s a bit autistic.” Everyone shares some traits, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life, and the phrase can make real needs sound small.
  • “ADHD just means you can’t sit still.” Many people with ADHD, especially girls and women, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside.
  • “They seem fine to me.” Masking can hide a lot, and seeming fine can cost someone the rest of their evening.
  • “Adjustments are special treatment.” They remove barriers so people can do what is expected, in the way glasses do.
  • “Autistic people lack empathy.” Many feel others’ feelings strongly, and differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Believe them about what’s hard, what hurts and what helps, even if it’s different for you.
  • Be clear. Say what you mean, give times, and write down anything important.
  • Ask what helps. Every neurodivergent person is different, and their answer will be better than any list.
  • Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places matter more than they look.
  • Enjoy their interests. Listening to someone talk about what they love is one of the warmest things you can do.

Ways to say it “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you wonder whether a difference is part of ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, for you or someone you care for, a doctor, school support staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so start early and write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has free, private help.

Questions people ask

Some questions this book tends to raise, with short answers and where to read more.

Do I need to remember my childhood clearly for the first chapter to help?

Most people remember a handful of scenes and a general feeling, and that is plenty. The first chapter asks about the rules your home ran on, such as how feelings were handled or who did the worrying. Those rules tend to show up in how you react today more than in any one memory, so start with one rule you can name. Where your lens came from (Chapter 1, page ) walks you through it.

What if looking back brings up something painful?

Stop there, and come back another day if you want to. Nobody has to finish a page. If old memories keep pulling you under, or you find yourself avoiding more and more of everyday life, talk to a doctor or counsellor. Worry, low mood and trauma (Book 2, Chapter 3, page 51) describes the kinds of help there are, and the help page at the front of this book (page ) lists free places to start.

I think I might be autistic or have ADHD. Can this book tell me?

It can’t, and nothing in it is a test. Minds that work differently (Chapter 7, page ) describes how some minds work, so that you might recognise yourself or someone you love, and it says who can talk you through an assessment if you want one. Plenty of people find the descriptions useful whether or not they ever look for a diagnosis.

Can a grown-up really change their personality?

A little, slowly, and mostly by practising the thing you want to be more like. Personality, temperament and change (Chapter 6, page ) treats traits as dials that move a few notches over the years. Every setting has its gifts and its costs, so the aim is a better fit with your life more than a new you.

My partner isn’t interested in any of this. Is it still worth reading?

Yes. This book is about you, and it works if you are the only person in your home who ever opens it. Knowing your own wiring and weather makes it easier to say what you need, and the people around you often notice the change before they notice the book. If you later want to look at things together, Book 3 and Book 4 are written for that.

How do I know when a book isn’t enough?

Most chapters have a short part called “When to get more help”, with the signs to look out for. If you need someone now, the help page at the front of this book (page ) has numbers to call. Help beyond this book lists counsellors, helplines and support groups, and most of them are free.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Alexithymia
Finding it hard to notice, name or describe your own feelings. It is a trait on a scale, not an illness, and it is more common in some autistic people. Naming body signals first can help.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, your load: a higher load score means a heavier load, and a lower battery.
A bid
A bid is any small reach for attention or connection, like “look at this,” a sigh or a joke.
Calm-Down Kit
The Calm-Down Kit is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Carrier wave
The carrier wave is the feeling that travels underneath the words: the tone that arrives before the meaning does.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Double empathy
The idea, from the autistic researcher Damian Milton, that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways: each finds the other hard to read, so neither side is simply “missing” something.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
If-then plan
A small plan written as “If this happens, then I will do that”, which makes a new habit easier to start (Peter Gollwitzer).
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
Masking
Masking is hiding the way you’re wired to fit in, which can be very tiring over time.
Monotropism
A theory of autistic attention: attention gathers deeply on a few things at a time, rather than spreading thinly across many. Switching away can take real effort.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
The rulebook from home
The rulebook from home is the unwritten set of rules you grew up with, about love, upset, chores and rest, that you may still expect others to follow.
Second arrow
The extra pain we add on top of a hard moment, such as being angry at ourselves for being sad. The first arrow is the event; the second is what we tell ourselves about it (a Buddhist image).
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 to 0.59 moderate, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Wave Code
Four letters from the style quiz in How you connect, one for each of four preferences about how you take in and send words. Each letter shows where you lean on a dial today: a shorthand for preferences, not a personality type.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill in on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
