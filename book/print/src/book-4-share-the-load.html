It is for anyone who shares a home, a family or a job, whether that is a couple, a family, housemates, co-parents or coworkers. Most of it also works if you are the only one reading.

Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

The work nobody sees: invisible labour at home

In short

  • Running a home takes unseen work, the noticing, remembering and planning that many people call the mental load.
  • The other person usually just hasn’t seen it. This work hides by nature, and missing it says little about how much someone cares.
  • Each of you writes only your own side of one ordinary week, and you read the two lists together.
  • The list is for seeing. Thank each other for the past, and plan next week.

Sunday, 9pm

The washing has been in the machine since Saturday morning and will need doing again, because it has that smell. The school trip form is due Tuesday. The birthday card for Auntie Jean is on the hall table, still in its cellophane, unsigned. Somebody in the house has noticed all three of these things, and it is probably you.

Somebody has also noticed that nobody else has noticed. From the hallway the house looks fine, which is exactly the trouble.

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment before it turns into a row. None of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you, and over months it piles up. This book calls the pile unbilled debt, because nobody ever sends a bill for it. (It’s a ledger word, and I make no apology for it. I spent my career as a government auditor, and work that only gets done because the same person quietly catches it every time has a name there: a control that was never there to begin with.) You can thank someone for what they carried before, and you leave the past unbilled.

    Marcus remembers every school form, every dentist appointment and every bit of lunch money for their two children. His husband, Theo, sees two children who always seem to have what they need, PE kit included, and genuinely has no idea how. When Marcus went to Glasgow for three days in March, Theo found out. Both children went to school on non-uniform day in uniform, and one of them went without a coat. Theo rang Marcus on the second night and said, “How do you know?” He isn’t careless, and never was; the work had simply been out of his sight for eleven years.

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check that it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, though nobody can see it. In a shared flat above a launderette, Yasmin noticed the broadband deal ended in three weeks. She compared six plans on a spreadsheet, picked one, set it up, and checked the next morning that it had gone through. Paying took two minutes, while the thinking took most of a Thursday evening, and as far as she knows nobody else in the flat has any idea it happened. When the internet kept working, one of them said, “Oh good, the broadband’s fine.”

    Pick one job at home and split it into its steps (noticing, planning, doing, checking), then ask who usually does each one.

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen, so the other person is rarely ignoring it. From where they stand, things look as though they take care of themselves. Edna minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own hospital appointments to fit, and her daughter thinks it all slots in easily. It doesn’t. Edna has cancelled her eye clinic twice. A gentle question often opens it up (“What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?”), followed by listening without explaining.

    Sometimes it only becomes visible when the person who carried it has gone. Over his first winter alone, a widower found out how many jobs his wife had quietly held, from the birthday cards and the boiler service to knowing who to ring when someone was ill. He didn’t see any of it until it stopped getting done, and the boiler broke in January. If that’s where you are, the section for people on their own, further on in this chapter, was written with you in mind.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time, so the talk starts from facts you share. This book calls that shared record a ledger, a plain list of who did what, kept for seeing.

    Two colleagues who job-share each listed a week of tasks. It turned out every single difficult client call had landed on Tamsin, every one of them, and nobody had decided that. It had just happened, the way these things do, because she was better at them. They left last year alone and agreed to take turns with Friday calls. A quick look at who does what is a gentle place to start. Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself honestly whether it’s to plan next week or to prove a point. If it’s the second, keep it in the drawer for now.

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did and then read the two lists together, which keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, a long week at work, children off school with colds, so you both understand what you read.

    Arran, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a rented house in Leith. Each wrote only their own list, in their own handwriting, which in Dani’s case needed some translating. Reading them together at the kitchen table, Sol learned for the first time that Arran had rung the landlord about the damp every single month since February. “Every month?” said Sol. “Every month,” said Arran. Dani said, “I thought it had just stopped.” It hadn’t; it had spread behind the wardrobe.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what is happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    Two brothers share their mum’s care, and one of them had been about to say “You never help” for about a year. What he said in the end was “There’s some work here neither of us has looked at properly.” His brother, braced for a row, found he didn’t need to have one. If words like “ledger” or “debt” feel heavy, use your own. “Our list” and “the stuff nobody sees” do the same job.

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool, and one week of notes is enough to start. Later come a 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying that day, and a monthly look-back that needs only a few lines a week. Precious is a nurse on nights and her partner works days, and they hardly see each other awake. They started with one week of notes stuck to the fridge with a magnet from Whitby, and changed nothing at all for a week. That was the whole plan. Your first step can be as small as picking a start day and setting one reminder.

    Dig deeper: how the book fits together

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, families, housemates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, and the ideas fit anyone who shares a load. In a foster home, the carer keeps track of family visits and social worker calls, while a fifteen-year-old in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week, usually pasta, and both kinds of work deserve counting.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

Your part, and only your part. You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back towards your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. Friday, after work

“You didn’t have to do all that,” they say, and they mean it kindly.

Someone did, though. The list only ever lived in your head, so to them it looked as if it took care of itself, the way a house seems to clean itself if you’re never in it on a Saturday morning. You don’t say this. You say “It’s fine,” which it isn’t, quite.

zqaxprefacexxmentalxloadqzThe mental load: what the research says

Many people call the thinking side of home life the mental load, meaning the noticing and planning that come before a job gets done and the checking after. It includes knowing the baby needs the next size of clothes, and remembering the form and the party present. It is real work, even when it doesn’t look like any.

Studies of couples find some patterns, on average. In couples of a man and a woman, women tend to do more of the noticing and checking (Allison Daminger, 2019). After a first baby a gap often opens, and in one study of working couples, mothers took on most of the new childcare while keeping their paid hours (Jill Yavorsky and colleagues, 2015). Arlie Hochschild called the home work that starts after paid work ends “the second shift.”

There is a fair point on the other side too. Sometimes the parent who knows how things are done holds on to that, without meaning to, which researchers call gatekeeping. When a parent openly invites the other in and lets them do it their own way, the other parent tends to do more of the care (Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins, 1999; Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, 2008).

These are averages across many homes, and they say nothing certain about yours. In your home it may be the other way round, or already even, or shared between two men or two women, which is why the book asks you to look at your own week on paper instead of guessing.

Hollie and Gareth have a toddler and a baby, and Gareth works shifts. Their week’s minutes come out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked anyone to do. Their week is in Chapter 2 (page ).

You could say “You do lots of the doing. It’s the remembering I’d love you to take some of, whole jobs, start to finish.” Or the other way round, “Show me once how you like it done, then let me do it my way.”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing it. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember (the appointments, the smoothing-over) and write them down for yourself. Count it without charging for it. The point is to see your real load so you can plan your energy. Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which just landed on you, and pick one you could hand back or do less often.

Nicola has the children every other week. Four years after the divorce, she sat down with a pen and found she was still buying the birthday presents for both sides of the family. His mum, his sister’s twins and his godson in Cardiff were all still on her list. She handed his side back in one friendly message, and braced herself. He replied with a thumbs-up and, to her surprise, “fair enough”. More for people living alone or after a breakup is on On my own.

Common mix-ups

“If it mattered, they’d have asked for help” misses how hidden this work is, often from the person doing it, until it’s written down. Nobody needs exactly 50/50, either. What you’re after is a split you can both see and both agree to. Please don’t start by going over the last ten years. Start with one ordinary week, and thank each other for the past without trying to settle it. And the one doing less usually isn’t the one who cares less. Usually they just can’t see it yet, and once you’ve both seen it, what happens next is a choice you make together.

To start, each of you can list the things you did this week that nobody saw, then swap lists and read the other one slowly. Say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed. Pick one item and thank them for it in a specific way. If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a coffee with the same questions works too. Fixing comes later. These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about, like the bin rota and the fish food, the reply to the school, the dentist reminder, and the bit at the bottom of the shopping list that just says “bulbs?”.

When the other one says it feels pretty even, they mean it. From inside their own week, it does.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    Ask whether the split is working and the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently, which is usually last night’s pans. This chapter asks a calmer question, using what you both wrote down. Ask it again every few months, even when things seem fine, because a split that worked once can quietly go stale.

    Two housemates had one truly terrible week of dishes in May, the kind where you wash a fork in order to eat with it. They were all set for a big row. Then they looked at the whole month, written down, and it was three ordinary weeks and one exam week. The row didn’t happen, which was almost a disappointment after all that build-up. They bought paper plates for exam week and left it at that.

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    From your week-long logs, work out each person’s share of the hours. An even split scores 1 and one person doing everything scores 0, whichever person it is. If you’ve agreed an uneven split that suits you, perhaps because one of you works longer hours outside the home, compare against that split instead. With three or more of you, compare each person’s share with an even share (100 divided by the number of people) or with the split you agreed. Three partners who log 30 hours of home jobs between them, at 15, 9 and 6, would each have 10 as an even share. The “Who does what?” page, where each person lists their hours, does the arithmetic for you.

    Log one week each, and put in the small jobs that take under ten minutes. Those are the ones that go missing. They are often the ones that start the rows, too.

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Each job gets two names. The owner does the job from start to finish. The backstop notices if it slipped and checks in gently, which is very different from supervising. “Did you remember to ring the dentist?”

  1. A full tank overflows.

    If you’re already tired or stretched from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction, which is simply human. Anything decided in that moment is worth coming back to once you’ve both calmed down.

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing in the first place. You are both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate, usually on a Sunday evening, and usually, for reasons nobody can explain, about the oven.

    There are two kinds of fair.

    One kind asks whether everything got done. The other asks whether each person did what they said they would. Both are reasonable, and friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realising.

    Nisha and Kezia share a room in halls. Nisha thinks a week was fair if the room is tidy by Friday, whoever tidied it. Kezia thinks it was fair if each of them kept her own half clean. For most of the first term each thought the other was being unreasonable, and said so to other people, and neither of them was. It came out at two in the morning over a shared packet of noodles, and they both laughed until the girl next door banged on the wall.

    You could say “When you say fair, do you mean it all gets done, or that we each do our bit?”

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind of fair judges by outcomes, by whether the right things got done by whoever had the time or energy. It’s flexible and easy-going, which is lovely when it works. Done carefully it counts the cost to whoever covered. Done carelessly it hides who is always stepping in.

    Marjorie is raising her grandchildren. Her grown-up son helps with whatever’s needed when he’s home, cheerfully and well. The house runs, and she is always, always the one who notices what’s needed. She didn’t see it herself until she marked, for one week, every job she picked up that wasn’t planned. It came to forty-one, and she showed her son. He said, “Forty-one?” and then, after a bit, “Right.”

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, and it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way. Two business partners run a café.   4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job from start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop, who notices if it slipped and checks in gently without supervising, and it can be the same person. zqaxchapterx4xxmoneyqzMoney between you

Money is where the two kinds of fair meet most often. Some couples put everything into one pot. Some keep their money separate and pay agreed bills, often half each, which is fair by promises, the same rule for both. This isn’t where you settle who owes what. Past hours are never billed to anyone, though you can still say thank you for them. You only fix the setup going forward. I wrote them so you could start anywhere, stop anywhere, and pick them up again a year later with a cup of tea and no guilt.

Four books, one idea

Know Yourself Kindly came first because everything else leans on it. It looked at the lens you see through, at where your lens came from (Book 1, Chapter 1, page 9) and how you are wired today (Book 1, Chapter 2, page 25), and at how feelings, worth, personality and different minds work. It’s hard to share a life well with no idea how you work yourself.

Heal and Steady was for the heavy days. It explained what stress does to the body (Book 2, Chapter 1, page 9), what anger is for, how worry and low mood work and where to find help, and it walked through grief, repair, change and what matters to you.

Understanding Each Other turned outwards, to why two kind people fall out of tune, how a message travels from one mind to another, why two people see the same moment so differently (Book 3, Chapter 3, page 45), and how belonging, love, groups and helping work.

Share the Load, this one, looked at the quiet work of running a home, a family or a team. How to see it. How to share it fairly. How to talk about it without anyone ending up in the dock.

Underneath all four there’s one idea, and if you forget everything else I’d keep this. Most of what goes wrong between people can be understood, and a good deal of it can be put right, mostly by seeing a bit more clearly and acting a bit more kindly, slightly more often than before. That’s it. It took four books to say because the slightly-more-often is the hard part.

Six threads through the series

The five big ideas at the front of each book (see the whole load, fix the setup, not the person, read your state first, tune how you send and receive and notice the quiet incentives) come down, in daily life, to a handful of habits, with one rule round all of them.

  1. Notice before you judge.

    A sharp word, a forgotten job, a quiet evening. There’s nearly always a reason. Tiredness, a full stress tank, a different family rule, a different kind of mind. Ask what might be going on before you decide what’s wrong with someone. How we see each other (Book 3, Chapter 3, page 45) goes into this.

  2. Settle before you speak.

    Nobody listens well with a flooded body. Breathe out slowly, step away with a time to come back, and talk after. Stress and the nervous system (Book 2, Chapter 1, page 9) and Anger (Book 2, Chapter 2, page 29) show how.

  3. Say it plainly and kindly.

    What happened, how it felt, what you’re asking for. How communication works (Book 3, Chapter 2, page 25) has the details, and the grumpy-voice test, which I still think is the best four seconds in the series.

  4. Small steps beat big pushes.

    One job with one owner. One walk. One message on a Sunday. Most change that lasts starts like that, small and repeated until it’s boring. Habits and change (Book 2, Chapter 6, page 103) and Is the split working? (Chapter 2, page ) both rest on it.

  5. Repair is normal.

    Everyone gets it wrong, often, me included. What keeps relationships going is coming back, with a short, real apology and a plan for next time, as Healing after hurt (Book 2, Chapter 5, page 85) describes.

  6. Your part, and only your part. Safety first.

    You can change what you do. You can’t make anyone else change, and you never have to accept being frightened, controlled or hurt. If that’s happening, getting safe comes before working it out. See Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety)

How the numbers work

Every number in this book is arithmetic you can do with a pencil. None of them is a test, a diagnosis or a verdict on anyone: they describe a week, a load or a way of sharing work. The weights and cut-offs are this book’s own rules of thumb, not research findings, and you are free to disagree with them. The worked examples are the book’s own, so you can check them against the chapters.

Two habits run through all of them. Scores are rounded to two decimal places before their band is read, so the number you see and the words you see always agree. And nobody’s score is ever worked out for them: if someone’s own answer is missing, the total waits.

The stress tank: How much are you carrying?

You score five things from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very true), about the last day or two: sleep debt, workload elsewhere, unresolved conflict, your physical state, and time pressure today.

tank = (sleep + workload + conflict + body + time) ÷ 20

The answer runs from 0 to 1, and higher means fuller. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 medium, and 0.60 or more high. (The worksheet splits each band in two for its words: very light under 0.15, light, medium on the lighter side under 0.45, medium on the heavier side, high under 0.80, and very high from 0.80.) Your battery is the other side of the same thing, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery. The battery is a picture in words, not a second score.

Worked example Rosa, from Chapter 3, page , scores sleep 3, workload 4, conflict 1, body 2 and time 3.

3 + 4 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 13; 13 ÷ 20 = 0.65, which is in the high band.

Two or more of you: each person scores only themselves, and the shared figure is the plain average of everyone’s own scores. If any one person is at 0.60 or more, treat that as the headline, whatever the average says.

Coming back after a pause (the Calm-Down Kit): if you like numbers, take the score again after a calming round. Under 0.50, go back at the time you named. From 0.50 to 0.59, take another round. At 0.60 or more after two rounds, put it off to a named time. The kit’s line sits at 0.50 on purpose: you don’t need an empty tank to come back, just one less than half full.

Under 45 reads as lots of room today, 45 to 64 as some room, and 65 or more as very little room.

Worked example Joel, from Book 1, Chapter 2, page 25, picks gusty, building pressure, a short night, a lot of people time, one ground condition (hungry) and one thing blowing in (work).

26 + 10 + 10 + 9 + 4 + 3 = 62: some room today, with less than usual, so the forecast says to go gently and settle first.

Workload balance: how evenly the hours are shared

Two people. Work out each person’s share of the logged hours as a percentage.

balance = 1 − (the difference between the two percentages ÷ 100)

50/50 scores 1.00; one person doing everything scores 0.00. It doesn’t matter which of you does more. Balance ignores the total, which is one reason the stress tank sits beside it.

Worked example Rosemary logs 14 hours and Ken 10 (Chapter 2, page ). Out of 24 hours, that is 58.3% and 41.7%.

1 − (58.3 − 41.7) ÷ 100 = 1 − 0.167 = 0.83

Three or more people, or an agreed split. The same idea, said more generally: how much of the week would have to change hands to reach the target, compared with the most that ever could.

each share = that person’s hours ÷ everyone’s hours target = an even share (1 ÷ number of people), or the split you agreed moved = ½ × the sum of every |share − target| most = 1 − the smallest target share balance = 1 − moved ÷ most

Worked example Three partners log 15, 9 and 6 hours (Chapter 2, page ): shares 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20, against a third each.

moved = ½ × (0.167 + 0.033 + 0.133) = 0.167; most = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667; balance = 1 − 0.167 ÷ 0.667 = 0.75. In hours, 0.167 × 30 = 5 hours a week would change hands for an even split.

With an agreed split, use it as the target: co-parents who agreed 60/40 and log 12 and 8 hours score 1.00.

Ownership: does every job have a name?

ownership = jobs with a named owner ÷ all the jobs on the list

The calculator counts a job as owned when it has one named owner. A backstop, the person who notices if it slipped, is a good habit and has its own column, but the score only needs the owner. The starter jobs on the worksheet don’t count until you give them an owner or change them. Never raise the score by deleting the jobs nobody owns: they are exactly what the score is there to show.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken list 12 regular jobs, and 9 have an owner: 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75.

Who carries more of the list. A score of 1.00 can still mean one person owns everything, so One owner per job also shows each person’s share of the list, with each job weighted by how often it comes round and how big it is:

weight of a job = how often × size how often: daily 5, weekly 1, ongoing 1, monthly 0.25, as needed 0.1 size: small 0.5, medium 1 (the default), big 2 (or, where you gave the hours each time: hours ÷ 0.5) share = the weights of that person’s jobs ÷ the weights of all the jobs

A job you decide together counts the same for each of you; on a list for two homes it shows as “Together” instead.

Worked example One person owns the cooking (daily, medium: 5) and the bills (monthly, medium: 0.25). The other owns the laundry (weekly, big: 2) and the car (as needed, medium: 0.1). Each owns two jobs, but 5.25 ÷ 7.35 = 71% of the list’s weight sits with the first person, and 29% with the second.

Who’s carrying more right now. Who did what flags the busiest person when their share of the logged minutes (or of the work done without being asked) is at least half, and at least 20 points over an even share: 70% or more for two people, 53% or more for three. In the example in Chapter 1, page , one partner did 105 of the 115 minutes that were noticed and handled without anyone asking: 91%, so the sheet says so.

The setup score: can the way you share the work last?

setup score = balance × 0.40 + ownership × 0.35 + (1 − the average tank score) × 0.25

The weights add up to 1, so the score runs from 0 to 1, and here higher means the setup is working better, the opposite of the tank score. The tank is the only part that counts against the score, which is why it enters as 1 − tank. 0.70 or more reads as working well (the “Who does what?” page says “holding”); 0.40 up to 0.70 as needs a look (“drifting”); below 0.40 as needs a rethink, together. It is a snapshot of the setup, never a probability and never a verdict on anyone.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken (Chapter 2, page ): balance 0.83, ownership 0.75, and their own tank scores are 0.45 and 0.30, an average of 0.375.

0.83 × 0.40 + 0.75 × 0.35 + (1 − 0.375) × 0.25 = 0.333 + 0.263 + 0.156 = 0.75: working well.

The shortfall is what each part could have earned minus what it did earn: balance 0.40 − 0.333 = 0.067; ownership 0.35 − 0.263 = 0.088; tank 0.25 − 0.156 = 0.094. The biggest shortfall is the tank, which usually eases outside the chore list (more sleep, a lighter week elsewhere). The calculator’s “what would help most” is the single change with the biggest gain: handing about 2 hours a week from Rosemary to Ken would take balance from 0.83 to 1.00, worth 0.067. Naming one more job’s owner would be worth 0.029, and a tank score lower by 0.10 on average 0.025.

The 30-day look-back: what shifted this month?

The look-back splits the last 30 days into four weeks (the first takes the first ten days). For each part you filled in (balance, ownership, the stress tank, and last-minute rescues), it compares the start of the month with the end:

start = the average of weeks 1 and 2 end = the average of weeks 3 and 4 it shifted if end − start is 0.10 or more (balance, ownership, tank), or 1 a week or more (rescues); anything smaller didn’t shift

One next step: a part that moved the wrong way comes first (the biggest move for its size). Otherwise, the first of these that is still true at the end: ownership under 0.70, balance under 0.70, 2 or more rescues a week, or anyone’s tank at 0.60 or more. If none is, keep the same rhythm and look again in 30 days.

Hours to change hands over the month: for each person, the hours they did minus their share of everyone’s hours, added up week by week. Half the sum of those gaps (ignoring the minus signs) is how many hours would change hands to make the month even.

On your own: is your week sustainable?

The “Who does what?” page’s “just me” view adds up the hours your week is spoken for (jobs, the thinking work, care and so on) and compares them with a waking week of about 112 hours (16 waking hours, 7 days).

share = the hours spoken for ÷ 112

Under 50% reads as sustainable, 50% up to 70% as full but workable, and 70% or more as stretched. For example, 50 hours spoken for is 45%, which leaves real room; 80 hours is 71%, a lot to keep up week after week.

All of these are this book’s own ways of putting things into numbers. They have not been tested in research studies, and they shouldn’t be quoted as findings. The free calculators on the website do the arithmetic for you, and each one has a “How is this worked out?” note.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Backstop
The second name on a job: someone who notices if it slipped and checks in kindly. Not a supervisor. A job counts as owned once it has an owner; the backstop is a good habit on top.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, how full your stress tank is: a fuller tank means a lower battery.
Calm-down plan
Your calm-down plan (once called the Calm-Down Kit) is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
Gatekeeping
Holding on to how a home or child-care job must be done, so the other person steps back. Researchers first described it in mothers (“maternal gatekeeping”), but anyone can do it.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
Ledger
A notebook where you write down what happened, so you can look at it calmly later. The book works the same way: see what is really going on, without blame, then change one small thing.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
Mental load
The noticing, planning and remembering behind running a shared life: knowing the shoes are too small, booking the appointment, keeping the list. It is real work, even though nobody sees it.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Parallel parenting
Co-parenting with as little direct contact as possible: each home runs its own way, and messages stay short, polite and about the children.
Person A and Person B
Person A and Person B are simply the two people on a two-person worksheet or tool. They are labels, not a ranking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
The rulebook from home
The rulebook from home is the unwritten set of rules you grew up with, about love, upset, chores and rest, that you may still expect others to follow.
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Unbilled debt
Unbilled debt is the work of a shared life that got done but was never noticed or thanked.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill in on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
