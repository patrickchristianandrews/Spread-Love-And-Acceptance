The work nobody sees: invisible labour at home

Part Two of the book is about the people you share a life, a home or a job with, and it opens here. Running a life takes a lot of work that nobody sees. This preface helps you make that work visible, to yourself and to anyone you share it with, so you can talk about it fairly and kindly.

In short:

  • Running a home takes unseen work: the noticing, remembering and planning that many people call the mental load.
  • The other person usually just hasn’t seen it. This work hides by nature. It isn’t a sign that they don’t care.
  • Each of you writes only your own side of one ordinary week, and you read the two lists together.
  • The list is for seeing, not for billing. Thank each other for the past, and plan next week.

The big ideas: This preface mostly teaches “See the whole load”, with help from “Notice the quiet incentives”. What are the five big ideas?

Sunday, 9pm

Someone notices the laundry is still in the washer, the school forms are due on Tuesday, and the birthday card never got posted. They also notice that nobody else has noticed. The house looks fine. That’s the problem.

Or, living alone: A widower says, “Since my wife died, I notice how many jobs she quietly carried: the birthday cards, the boiler service, knowing who to ring when someone was ill. I never saw them until they stopped getting done.”

  1. Unseen work quietly adds up.

    Remembering appointments, noticing the milk is low, calming a tense moment: none of it shows up on a calendar, so it rarely gets a thank-you. Over months, it piles up. The book calls this pile unbilled debt. Nobody sends a bill for it. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past.

    For example: Marcus remembers every school form, dentist visit and bit of lunch money for their two kids. His husband, Theo, sees two kids who always seem to have what they need. The work is just out of sight.

    Words you could use: “Can I show you some of the things I keep track of? I’m not asking you to pay anything back. I just want you to see them.”

    Try this: Tonight, write down three things you did today that nobody saw. Don’t do anything with the list yet. Just notice.

  2. A lot of it is thinking, not doing.

    Before a job gets done, someone has to notice it, remember it, plan it and check it happened. That thinking takes real time and energy, even though nobody can see it.

    For example: In a shared flat, Yasmin notices the internet deal is ending, compares plans, picks one and checks it went through. Paying took two minutes. The thinking took an evening.

    Words you could use: “Doing it is the quick part. Remembering it and planning it is the part I’d love some help with.”

    Try this: Pick one job at home and split it into four steps: notice, plan, do, check. Who usually does each step?

    Dig deeper: the kinds of unseen work

  3. The other person usually just hasn’t seen it.

    When this work is done well, nothing seems to happen. So the other person isn’t ignoring it. From where they stand, it looks like things take care of themselves.

    For example: Edna minds her grandchildren three afternoons a week and quietly moves her own doctor visits to fit. Her daughter thinks it all “fits in easily.”

    Words you could use: “I don’t think you’ve been ignoring it. I think it’s been hard to see from where you are.”

    Try this: Ask one person, “What’s something you do that you think I don’t see?” Then just listen, without explaining.

    Dig deeper: why it stays hidden

  4. Writing it down isn’t keeping score.

    The point is for both of you to see the same picture, in the same words, at the same time. Then the talk starts from facts you share, not from two different memories.

    The book calls this shared record a ledger: a plain list of who did what. It’s a way to see, not a bill.

    For example: Two coworkers sharing one job each list a week of tasks. Every client call landed on one person. They don’t argue about last year; they swap Friday calls.

    Words you could use: “I’m not trying to win anything. I’d like us both to be looking at the same list.”

    Try this: Before you show anyone a list, ask yourself: is this to plan next week, or to prove a point? Share it only if the answer is “plan.”

    Dig deeper: why a ledger isn’t a grudge

  5. Each of you writes only your own side.

    Nobody fills in anything for the other person. You each note what you did, then read the two lists together. That keeps it fair and keeps it kind. Each of you also adds the context only you have, like what the week was like, so you both understand rather than judge.

    For example: Arran, Dani and Sol are three partners sharing a home. Each writes only their own list. Reading them together, Sol learns Arran has been calling the landlord every month.

    Words you could use: “I’ve written my side. Would you like to write yours, in your own words, when you’re ready?”

    Try this: Add one line of context to your own list, like “long week at work” or “the kids were ill.” It helps the other person understand, not judge.

    Dig deeper: your side, their side

  6. The borrowed words are there to take the blame away.

    The book uses a few terms from accounting and science because they describe what’s happening without saying whose fault it is. Each one is explained in plain words, and you never need them to use anything here.

    For example: Two brothers share their mum’s care. Instead of “you never help,” one says, “There’s some unseen work here we haven’t looked at.” That’s much easier to hear.

    New together? If words like “ledger” or “debt” feel heavy a few months in, use your own. “Our list” and “the stuff nobody sees” work just as well. The idea stays the same: two people looking at one honest picture, kindly, so nobody has to guess.

    Words you could use: “Let’s look at how it’s set up, not at each other.”

    Try this: Swap one blaming phrase you use, like “you never,” for a plain one, like “this job keeps landing on me.”

    Dig deeper: why the book borrows these words

  7. One small step at a time.

    Each chapter adds one idea and one tool. One week of notes is enough to start. Later come a 90-second daily check-in about how much you were carrying today, a weekly catch-up and a monthly look-back. If you use the monthly look-back later, a few lines a week is all it needs.

    For example: A nurse on nights and a partner on days hardly see each other awake. They start with one week of notes on the fridge. No changes yet.

    Words you could use: “Could we just try one week of notes and see what we notice? We don’t have to change anything yet.”

    Try this: Pick a start day this week and set one reminder on your phone. That’s the whole first step.

    Dig deeper: how the book fits together

    Tidbit

    Tidbit wants you to know: counting jobs isn’t keeping score. It’s more like a treasure hunt for the work nobody noticed! The quick split check is a good first sniff.

    Tool: Who does what: chore split check spreadloveandacceptance.com/lemonade-stand

  8. It can help in many kinds of shared life.

    Partners, family, roommates, co-parents and coworkers all share unseen work. The examples often use a couple at home, but the ideas fit anyone who shares a load.

    For example: A foster carer tracks family visits, social worker calls and school meetings. A teenager in the home quietly cooks for the younger ones twice a week. Both count.

    Words you could use: “Whatever we are to each other, we share some work. Could we look at it together?”

    Try this: Name one shared load in your life that isn’t a couple: a family group chat, a shared flat, a team at work.

    Dig deeper: who this book is for

Your part, and only your part. You can’t make anyone see the unseen work, and you can’t write their side of the ledger. You can write your own side honestly, say it once, kindly, and then match what you take on to what comes back. If you keep carrying more than your share with nothing in return, stepping back towards your share isn’t a punishment. It protects the energy you need to stay kind. zqaxprefacexxmentalxloadqzThe mental load: what the research says

Many people call the thinking side of home life the mental load: the noticing and planning that come before a job gets done, and the checking after. Knowing the baby needs the next size of clothes. Remembering the form, the jab, the party present. It’s real work, even when it doesn’t look like any.

Studies of couples find some patterns, on average. In couples of a man and a woman, women tend to do more of the noticing and checking (Allison Daminger, 2019). After a first baby, a gap often opens: in one study of working couples, mothers took on most of the new childcare while keeping their paid hours (Jill Yavorsky and colleagues, 2015). Arlie Hochschild called the home work that starts after paid work ends “the second shift.”

There is a fair point on the other side too. Sometimes the parent who knows how things are done holds on to that, without meaning to. Researchers call it gatekeeping. When a parent openly invites the other in, and lets them do it their own way, the other parent tends to do more of the care (Sarah Allen and Alan Hawkins, 1999; Sarah Schoppe-Sullivan and colleagues, 2008).

These are averages across many homes, not a verdict on yours. In your home it may be the other way round, or already even, or shared between two men or two women. That’s why the book asks you to look at your own week on paper, instead of guessing.

For example (made up): Hollie and Gareth have a toddler and a baby, and Gareth works shifts. Their week’s minutes come out nearly even. But Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked anyone to do. Their week is in Chapter II (page ).

Words you could use: “You do lots of the doing. It’s the remembering I’d love you to take some of: whole jobs, start to finish.” Or, the other way round: “Show me once how you like it done, then let me do it my way.”

Dig deeper: the mental load, fairly

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Notice everything you carry, including the thinking and feeling work you have never counted. Notice, too, the work others do that you may have missed.
  • Between you and others: Put both lists on one shared, fair page, so nobody has to argue about whose work “counts.” Jobs nobody claims tend to drift to whoever notices first, and a specific thank-you helps keep things steady.

Words you could use: “I want to see what you carry, too, not just what I carry.” “Could we put both our weeks on one page?”

On your own: your own unseen work

You don’t need anyone else to start seeing the unseen. For one week, notice the jobs only you remember: the appointments, the restocking, the smoothing-over. Write them down for yourself, not as a bill.

  • Count it, don’t charge it. The point is to see your real load, so you can plan your energy.
  • Notice which jobs are yours by choice and which just landed on you.
  • Pick one job you could hand back, drop or do less often, and say so kindly.

For example: Nicola, divorced, has the children every other week. On paper, she sees she still buys presents for both sides of the family. She hands his side back, kindly, in one message.

Words you could use: “I’ve noticed I’ve been doing this for both of us. From next month, could you take it on?” Or, to yourself: “That was real work, even if nobody saw it.”

Try this: Circle one job on your list that is yours by choice. Enjoy it. Then circle one that just landed on you, and decide what you’ll do about it. More for people living alone or after a breakup: On my own (spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own).

Common mix-ups

  • “If it mattered, they would have asked for help.” Unseen work is often hidden from the person doing it, too, until it’s written down.
  • “Writing it down means I’m angry.” It means you want you both to see the same picture.
  • “It has to be exactly 50/50.” The goal is a split you can both see and both agree to, not a perfect half.
  • “Mental work doesn’t count, only chores do.” Remembering, planning and checking take real time and energy. Put them on the list.
  • “We should start by going over the last ten years.” Start with one ordinary week. You can thank someone for the past without trying to settle it.
  • “The one doing less doesn’t care.” Usually they just can’t see it yet. Nobody here is the villain. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

Words you could use: “I don’t think either of us is the bad guy here. We just haven’t seen the same picture yet.”

Next, read the other list slowly and say one thing on it you hadn’t noticed before.

Then pick one item from their list and say thank you for it, in a specific way.

If writing things down isn’t your style, a ten-minute talk over a cup of coffee with the same questions works too.

Limits and cautions

These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private hotlines.

A list should never be demanded, checked over your shoulder, or used to keep track of where you go or what you spend. If that is happening, it isn’t a ledger. It’s control, and it’s not your fault. Signs to look out for. If you are a counsellor or group leader using this book with others, see For counsellors (spreadloveandacceptance.com/for-counselors).

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about. When the other one says it feels even, they mean it. From inside each person’s own week, it does.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    When people ask “Is this working?”, the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently. This chapter asks a calmer question, using what you both wrote down. Re-ask it every few months, even when things seem fine, because a split that worked once can quietly go stale.

    For example: Two housemates had one awful week of dishes. The whole month showed three fine weeks and one exam week.

    Words you could use: “Can we look at the whole month before we decide anything?”

    Try this: Put a short check in your calendar for about a month from now.

    Words you could use: “Let’s each bring our own numbers, so neither of us has to guess.”

    Try this: Open the three worksheets once, just to see what they ask.

    Dig deeper: where each number comes from

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    From your week-long logs, work out each person’s share of the hours. An even split scores 1. One person doing everything scores 0. It doesn’t matter which person is doing more. If you agreed an uneven split that suits you (say, one of you works longer hours outside the home), compare against that split instead.

    For example: Three partners log 30 hours of home jobs: 15, 9 and 6. An even share is 10 each.

    Words you could use: “I’m not keeping score of you. I just want us both to see the hours.”

    Try this: Log one week each, and include the small jobs that take under ten minutes.

    Three or more of you? Compare each person’s share with an even share (100 ÷ number of people), or with a split you agreed. The Lemonade Stand (spreadloveandacceptance.com/lemonade-stand), a free page where each person lists their hours, does the arithmetic.

    Dig deeper: the balance score

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Each job gets two names: the owner, who does the job start to finish, and a backstop, who notices if it slipped and gently checks in (never a supervisor). It can be the same person, though a second name gives the job a gentle check. Count the jobs that have an owner, then divide by all the jobs on the list. (The backstop is a good habit on top, but the count only needs the owner.) Leave jobs nobody owns on the list. They are the ones that matter most.

    A decision you make together, like which school, can be marked “Both (decide together)”. It counts as owned, because a decision needs both voices. A job that needs doing gets one name.

    For example: Two sisters care for their dad. Nobody owned his prescription, so each thought the other had ordered it.

    Words you could use: “Can we put a name next to this one, so it doesn’t fall between us?”

    Try this: Find one job with no name, and give it an owner and a backstop today.

  5. Stress: how full each stress tank is.

    Each of you rates how full your own stress tank is with How much are you carrying? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-02-how-much-are-you-carrying). A higher score means more stress. Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 up to 0.60 is moderate, and 0.60 or more is high. The score uses the average of your scores. But if either of you is at 0.60 or more, talk about that first, even when the score looks fine. A setup that only works when you’re both well rested isn’t one that can last, so more stress lowers the result.

    For example: A grandmother raising two grandchildren scores 0.70. Her son, who helps at weekends, scores 0.35. Her 0.70 is the headline.

    Words you could use: “My tank is high this week. Can we plan around that, not around the average?”

    Try this: Each of you scores your own tank on the same day, before looking at the other’s.

    Dig deeper: the stress score

  6. One number comes out, from 0 to 1.

    0.70 or above: working well, the setup is holding. From 0.40 up to 0.70: needs a look, something is drifting, often who owns what. Below 0.40: needs a rethink, together. (The result is called the setup score. It looks like a probability, but it isn’t one. It’s a simple weighted sum.)

    A weighted sum means each part counts for a set share: the hours count most, then the owners, then stress.

    For example: Two friends who run a café score 0.55. Nothing is broken, but something is drifting.

    Words you could use: “This number is about our setup, not about us.”

    Try this: Write the date next to your number, so you can compare next month.

    Dig deeper: the formula and what each range means

  7. Start with the biggest shortfall.

    The single number matters less than which of the three parts pulled it down most. In the middle band that is often ownership, which is why the calculator suggests One owner per job first. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

    The shortfall is how far one part fell below the most it could add.

    For example: Separated parents score 0.62. Their biggest gap: nobody owns school forms. They fix only that.

    Words you could use: “Which part pulled the number down most? Let’s start there.”

    Try this: Choose one small change, not five.

    Dig deeper: reading your result

  8. It judges the setup, never a person.

    The number isn’t a verdict on who’s better or whether the relationship works. It’s where a conversation starts, never how one ends. These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private hotlines.

    For example: One partner sees 0.35: “So I’m the problem.” The other: “No. The bathroom has no owner, and you work nights.”

    Words you could use: “Whatever this shows, I’m on your side.”

    Try this: Before you start, agree that either of you can say “pause” and come back later.

    Dig deeper: what the number is not

Your part, and only your part. The score describes the setup you share, but your levers are your own: your jobs, your hours and what you agree to take on. You can propose a fix; you can’t make the other person accept it. If the balance stays one-sided, step your load back towards your share on purpose, and say so kindly. Common mix-ups

  • “A low number means the relationship is failing.” It only means the current setup needs changing. Setups change all the time.
  • “A high number means nobody can complain.” A good score never silences anyone. If something feels wrong, say so.
  • “We can just guess the numbers.” A guessed number is really just a feeling with a decimal point. Use your written logs.
  • “Taking unowned jobs off the list will help.” It raises the number and changes nothing at home.
  • “One of us can fill it in for both.” Each person writes their own log and scores their own stress tank.
  • “Fair means exactly 50/50.” Fair is what you both agree to, with eyes open. An uneven split you chose together can be fair. Chapter IV covers the kinds of fair.
  • “The backstop checks up on the owner.” No. The backstop only notices if something slipped, and asks kindly. It is help, not a check on someone.

Thursday, 5:15pm

They ask, perfectly pleasantly, whether you remembered to call the dentist. Your reaction is much bigger than the question deserved, and you can hear it happening while it happens.

  1. A full tank overflows.

    If you’re already tired, stretched or stressed from somewhere else, a small question can get a big reaction. That’s human. Anything decided in that moment is worth coming back to once you’ve both calmed down.

    For example: A nurse gets home after a night shift. Her housemate asks, “Did you see my message?” She snaps. It wasn’t the message. It was twelve hours on her feet.

  3. Check your stress tank in a minute.

    How much are you carrying? measures how full your stress tank is. A higher score means more stress in the tank. Score five things from 0 to 4: sleep, work elsewhere, any unsettled argument, how your body feels, and time pressure today. Add them up and divide by 20. Under 0.30 is low. From 0.30 up to 0.60 is moderate. 0.60 or more is high. If it has been high for a long time, it can start to feel normal, so a quick reading each week is worth it.

    For example: A man caring for his wife after surgery scores 0.65 three days in a row. That tells him he needs help, not more effort.

    Words you could use: “I checked my tank. I’m in the high band today.”

    Try this: Score yourself tomorrow morning, before you look at your phone.

  4. Three everyday states.

    Calm and connected. Revved up. Running on empty. Knowing which one you’re in tells you whether now is a good time to talk.   5. There are seven angles on the same moment.

    For example: Two adult brothers argue about their late mother’s house. One sees memories. The other sees bills. Both are real.

    Words you could use: “You’re right about what you see. Can I tell you what I see?”

    Try this: Say the other person’s view back to them before you give yours.

    Before a hard conversation, each of you says how full your stress tank is and which angle you’re seeing things from. It works best before you start, not in the middle.

  8. A full tank means “later,” not “never.”

    A high score is a reason to pause, never a way out of the talk. Say when you’ll come back, and the Calm-Down Kit (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wp-11) can help in the meantime.     Try this: Whenever you pause, name the time you’ll come back.

Protect yours: rest, say “later,” and give energy where it’s met. When both tanks are full, the kindest thing you control is the timing. Common mix-ups

  • “A big reaction means it was a big deal.” Sometimes. Often it means the tank was already full.
  • “A high score is an excuse.” It’s a note to come back later, not a pass to skip the talk.
  • “I can tell how full their tank is.” Only they can say. Each person scores only themselves.
  • “Pausing is the same as the silent treatment.” A pause has a time to come back. The silent treatment doesn’t. Always say when.
  • “If we see it differently, one of us is wrong.” You may just be standing at different angles.
  • “Naming my angle means giving up my point.” It just helps the other person see where your point is coming from.

Words you could use: “I don’t think you’re wrong. I think we’re standing in different places. Can we swap for a minute?”

Children carry two homes, and changeover day fills their tank. Being quiet, clingy or cross for a few hours after a handover is common. It usually isn’t a sign that something is wrong at the other home.

  • The child is never the messenger. Adult news goes adult to adult.
  • Money: follow any formal arrangement. Split the extras by a ratio you agree, keep one list, and settle up once a month.

  • Once a job has an owner, look to the owner. Covering for someone is kind when you say it out loud.

The big ideas: This chapter mostly teaches “Fix the setup, not the person” and “Notice the quiet incentives”, with help from “Read your state first”. What are the five big ideas?

The same argument, twice a month

One of you counts hours. The other counts who noticed the job needed doing. You’re both counting honestly, in two different currencies, and then arguing about the exchange rate.

  1. There are two kinds of fair.

    “Did everything get done?” and “Did each of us do what we said we’d do?” Both are reasonable. Friction starts when each of you is using a different one without realising.

    Dig deeper: the two kinds of fair

  2. Fair by results.

    This kind judges by outcomes: did the right things get done, by whoever had the time or energy? It’s flexible and easy-going. Done carefully, it counts the cost to whoever covered, but done carelessly, it can hide who is always stepping in.

    Dig deeper: judging by results

  3. Fair by promises.

    This kind asks whether each person did the jobs they agreed to own. It’s clear and steady, but it can feel rigid in a week when life gets in the way.

    Dig deeper: judging by promises

  4. Once a job has an owner, look to the owner.

    The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. A second name on the list is a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in, never a supervisor. (It can be the same person.) If it’s your job, the question is whether you did it, not whether someone else quietly covered for you. That protects the person who keeps filling the gaps.

    Dig deeper: agreeing which kind you’ll use

  5. Covering is kind. Say it out loud.

    Helping out when the owner can’t is lovely. Just mention it, so the help is seen: “I put the bins out this week, since you were away.” Help that stays silent turns into unseen work, and unseen help slowly becomes expected (see Complacency (spreadloveandacceptance.com/complacency)).

    Dig deeper: covering without resentment

    Sugarfoot

    Sugarfoot’s tip: covering for someone is kind. Say it out loud, gently, and ask for what you’d like next time, so it doesn’t turn into quiet resentment.

    Tool: Turn a complaint into a request spreadloveandacceptance.com/small-wins

  6. Let a comment land before you react.

    When a remark hits harder than it should, say “Give me a second.” The idea is borrowed loosely from brain science, where the brain turns down sounds and sensations automatically, in a fraction of a second. This book’s pause is different: a few seconds you choose to take on purpose. Let the spike pass, then decide whether it needs an answer now, later or not at all.

    Dig deeper: giving a comment time to land

  7. Notice, name, decide.

    Notice it landed hard. Name it kindly: “That hit harder than you probably meant.” Then, once you’ve settled, decide how to reply. Say it so it lands (spreadloveandacceptance.com/workpapers/wp-09-say-it-so-it-lands) helps with the words.

    Dig deeper: the three steps

  8. Keep the two questions apart.

    “Was the job done?” is a question about a fact. “Did that comment hurt?” is a question about a feeling. Both matter. Answer them one at a time, not in the same argument.

Common mix-ups

  • “If it got done, there’s no problem.” Someone may be paying for that in time and energy.
  • “Holding the owner to it is being strict.” It’s just keeping the agreement you both made. That’s kinder than quiet resentment.

zqaxchapterx4xxmoneyqzMoney between you

Money is where the two kinds of fair meet most often. Three common ways to share it:

  • All shared: both incomes go into one pot.
  • Separate: each keeps their own money and pays agreed bills, often half each. That’s fair by promises: the same rule for both.

Many couples mix them: a joint account for bills and food, and some money each that nobody has to explain. The right way is the one you both chose with your eyes open.     Dig deeper: gathering the month’s notes

  4. A problem that keeps coming back usually has no real owner.

    It gets patched every week and never fixed. The owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The fix is to give the job one owner whose name sticks, or to talk honestly about whether anyone has the time and energy for it.

    Dig deeper: spotting the gaps that keep reopening

  5. Sort each repeat into one of three kinds.

    No clear owner: update the job list. An owner who can’t keep up: have a kind, honest talk. A short-term cause, like illness or travel: note it and move on.

    Dig deeper: sorting each gap

  6. Count the gaps each month.

    Write down how many gaps of each kind you found. If the number of jobs with no clear owner falls over several months, the setup is working; one month can be a normal swing. If it keeps rising, something bigger has changed, and it’s worth a talk.

  7. Look back even when nothing is slipping.

    A month with no gaps is good news, and it’s also the month most likely to go unchecked. When something has worked for a while, people stop looking, and nothing breaks on the day that happens. Keep the look-back going on quiet months, and add one question: “What’s something we’ve stopped noticing or mentioning?” That is how a look-back guards against complacency (spreadloveandacceptance.com/complacency).

  8. It’s not a review of either person.

    You don’t add up old hours or punish old mistakes. You can thank someone for what they carried before; you just don’t bill for the past. The only question is where the setup isn’t working yet, and what you’ll try next month.

The last Sunday of the month

Two mugs of tea, four weeks of notes and twenty minutes. You go down the list. Most weeks went fine. Two jobs keep coming up. That’s the whole meeting, and it’s enough.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Look at your own routines as a setup you can adjust. Spot the easy defaults, like “I’ll just do it,” that let a gap quietly reopen.
  • Between you and others: Look at the month together, give repeating gaps a clear owner, and keep count so jobs don’t drift back to whoever notices first.

In real life

  • Partners: Sam and Alex’s bins slipped three weeks in a row, because Alex works late on Tuesdays. After a trial swap, they make it official: Sam owns the bins, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, so the hours stay even.
  • Family: Three siblings keep forgetting to call their dad’s landlord about repairs. It turns out nobody ever agreed who would.
  • Roommates: The recycling overflows every other week. Everyone thought someone else was taking it down.
  • Coworkers: Meeting notes go missing twice a month. Giving note-taking a named owner fixes it by the next month.
  • Long distance: A couple in two cities do the look-back by video. Their repeat is “who calls first,” so they give it an owner per weekday. See Long distance (spreadloveandacceptance.com/long-distance).

Common mix-ups

  • “This is where we settle who owes what.” Past hours are never billed to anyone, though you can still say thank you for them. You only fix the setup going forward.
  • “If a job slipped, the owner failed.” The gap might be in the list, in the owner’s capacity, or just a one-off week.
  • “One bad week means we should change everything.” Don’t make a lasting change because of a short-term cause.
  • “We can skip the weekly checks and just do this.” The monthly look-back only works if the weekly notes feed it.
  • “More gaps this month means we’re failing.” One month can be a normal swing, or it can mean life changed. That’s a reason to talk, not to worry.
  • “The look-back is for the person who keeps the notes.” It works best when both bring their notes, and both talk.

A note on safety: These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If looking back together ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private hotlines.

A look-back is something you both choose. If one person demands logs of the other’s every hour, checks their phone without agreement, or uses the ✗ marks to punish, that is control, not a look-back, and it is not your fault. Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend is telling you about her awful week. You have five good ideas before she’s finished her first sentence. You share them all. She goes quiet, then says, “Yeah, maybe.” You both hang up feeling a bit worse, and you’re not sure why.

Most of us want to help the people around us. But helping well is a skill, and good intentions don’t always land. This chapter explains how support works, why “fixing” can backfire, and how to care for others in a way you can keep up. It’s for friends, partners, parents, coworkers, carers, neighbours, and anyone who has ever wanted to help and didn’t know how.

  1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in different kinds. Some people want ideas. Some want someone to listen. Some want a practical hand, like a meal or a lift. Some just want company. Giving the wrong kind, even kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard. The simplest fix is to ask.

    For example (a friend): In the scene above, a better start would have been: “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to vent?” She would have said “vent,” and the call would have gone very differently.

    Words you could use: “Do you want ideas, a listening ear, or a hand with something?” “What would help most right now?”

    Try this: Next time someone tells you about a problem, ask what kind of help they want before you give any.

    Dig deeper: kinds of support

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being truly heard is powerful. It helps people calm down, think more clearly, and often find their own answers. Good listening means paying attention, not interrupting, checking you understood, and showing that their feelings make sense, even if you’d feel differently.

    For example (partners): Ravi comes home upset about his boss. Instead of “Just quit,” his partner says: “So he criticised you in front of everyone, after you stayed late all week. No wonder you’re furious.” Ravi’s shoulders drop. Ten minutes later, he has his own plan.

    Words you could use: “So what happened was… Have I got that right?” “That makes sense. Anyone would feel that way.”

    Try this: In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice. Then sum up what you heard.

    Dig deeper: how listening helps

    Sugarfoot

    Sugarfoot’s tip: sometimes the best help is sitting close and listening, no fixing needed. Listening takes energy too, so check your own tank now and then.

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone, we want to make the problem go away. But doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable. Helping well often means helping someone do it, not doing it for them. It respects that it’s their life and their choice.

    For example (a parent and a grown-up child): Rosalind’s 24-year-old son is struggling with a job application. She wants to rewrite it. Instead she asks, “Want me to read it and tell you what stands out?” He makes the changes himself, and feels proud when he gets the interview.

    Thoughts you could try: “Is this mine to fix, or theirs to solve with my support?” “What do they want to do here?”

    Try this: Notice the urge to fix. Before acting, ask: “Would you like me to do that, or help you do it?”

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were your own can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion: warm concern from a steadier place. Compassion still cares deeply. It just doesn’t require you to sink with the other person. A steady helper is more useful than a second person drowning.

    For example (housemates): When Abi’s housemate went through a breakup, Abi cried every night with her and couldn’t sleep. After a week she was exhausted. She changed approach: kind and present, but also taking breaks, and reminding herself, “This is her pain. I can care without carrying all of it.”

    Thoughts you could try: “I can be with them in this without making it mine.” “I care, and I’m steady.”

    Try this: After a heavy conversation, do something that brings you back to yourself: a walk, a shower, five slow breaths. It isn’t abandoning them. It’s refilling.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything isn’t kindness in the long run. It leads to resentment, exhaustion and, eventually, not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can give, so your yes means something. A kind no, with a suggestion of what you can do, is still help.

    For example (siblings): Kojo’s sister keeps asking to borrow money. He loves her and he’s running out. He says: “I can’t lend money any more. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you’d like.”

    Words you could use: “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    Try this: Write down one thing you’re giving that you can’t keep giving. Plan a kinder, smaller version you could offer instead.

    Dig deeper: boundaries in helping

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot, like carers, parents, nurses, teachers and the friend everyone calls, can burn out. Signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, feeling numb or cynical about the people you help, getting ill more often, or dreading the next call. It doesn’t mean you’re a bad helper. It means you need help too.

    For example (a carer): Helen has looked after her husband since his stroke. She snaps at him, then feels terrible. Her doctor says it sounds like carer strain. A local carers’ group and a few hours of respite each week give her back a little of herself, and she’s gentler at home.

    Thoughts you could try: “Looking after myself is part of looking after them.”

    Try this: Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love (spreadloveandacceptance.com/caregivers).

    Dig deeper: burnout and refilling

  7. In a crisis, stay with them and bring in help.

    If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s okay to ask them directly. Research, such as a review led by Tim Dazzi (2014), suggests asking about suicide doesn’t put the idea in someone’s head; it can bring relief. You don’t have to be their therapist. Your job is to listen, stay with them, and help them reach trained support.

    For example (a coworker): A colleague says, “Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me.” Kirsty says gently: “I’m really glad you told me. Are you thinking about ending your life?” She says she’s had thoughts. Kirsty stays with her and they call a crisis line together.

    Words you could use: “Are you thinking about suicide?” “I’m glad you told me. You don’t have to go through this alone. Let’s call someone together.”

    Try this: Save a crisis number in your phone now, so you have it if you ever need it. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

    Dig deeper: helping in a crisis

  8. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something go wrong, each person can assume someone else will act. Psychologists call this the bystander effect. Knowing about it helps you break it. You don’t have to be a hero. Small, safe actions count: asking “Are you okay?”, getting staff, or calling for help.

    For example (a stranger): On a train, a man is making a young woman uncomfortable. Yvonne doesn’t confront him. She sits next to the woman and asks loudly, “Hi! Is this the train to Leeds?” The woman joins in the chat, and the man moves away.

    Words you could use: “Are you okay? Do you need anything?” To someone else nearby: “Can you get the driver? I’ll stay here.”

    Try this: Think of one safe thing you could do if you saw someone in trouble in a public place: distract, get help, or check in afterwards. Having a plan makes it easier to act.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  9. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways. People who are good at giving help are often bad at receiving it. But letting others help is a gift to them, too: most people like being useful to someone they care about. Relationships where only one person ever helps tend to feel uneven over time.

    For example (on your own, as a new parent): Nkechi kept saying, “We’re fine, thanks,” to every offer after the baby came. When she finally said yes to a neighbour’s offer of a meal, the neighbour was delighted, and they became friends.

    Words you could use: “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

    Try this: Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage without it.

    Dig deeper: receiving help

Your part, and only your part. You can offer help, listen well, and be honest about your limits. You can’t make someone accept help, take your advice or get better on your timetable. Their life and their choices are theirs. If someone is in danger, your part is to bring in help, not to carry it alone.

  • On your own: Helping yourself counts too. Ask yourself the same question: “Do I need ideas right now, or just some rest?”
  • With a partner: “Do you want me to help, or just to hear it?” works at home as well as anywhere.
  • Family: Caring for a parent or a child with extra needs is long-haul help. Share the load early. See Caring for someone you love.
  • Friends: The friend everyone calls needs someone to call too. Notice who that might be.
  • At work: Covering for a struggling coworker is kind for a week. Longer than that, it needs a real conversation with the team. See At work (spreadloveandacceptance.com/work).
  • With strangers: In an emergency, point to one person: “You in the blue coat, please call for help.” A crowd often waits for someone else.
  • If you have ADHD: Helping is interesting and urgent, so a fast “yes” is easy. Try “I’d like to help. Let me check my week and come back to you tonight.” And ask for the practical help you need too. More in the full chapter (spreadloveandacceptance.com/book/understanding-12-helping-in-depth).

Common mix-ups

  • “Helping means solving the problem.” Often, helping means listening, being there, or helping someone find their own answer.
  • “If I really cared, I’d feel everything they feel.” Caring from a steady place helps more and lasts longer.
  • “Good helpers never say no.” Good helpers last because they know their limits.
  • “Asking about suicide might give them the idea.” Research suggests it doesn’t, and asking can help someone open up.
  • “I’ll make it worse if I step in.” There are small, safe ways to help. Doing nothing often feels worse for the person in trouble.
  • “I should be able to manage without help.” Everyone needs help sometimes. Receiving it is part of a healthy relationship.

If you want to help someone else with this

This whole chapter is about helping others, so here’s help for the helpers around you:

  • Notice the helpers. The carer, the friend everyone calls, the coworker who always covers. Ask them how they are, and mean it.
  • Offer specific help. “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.”
  • Give them permission to rest. “You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this.”
  • If a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love or suggest they talk to a doctor.

Words you could use: “You look after everyone. Who looks after you?” “I’d love to do something for you this week. What would help?”

When to get more help

  • If someone is in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • If someone talks about suicide, stay with them and contact a crisis line together. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.
  • If someone tells you they’re being hurt or controlled at home, believe them, don’t confront the other person yourself, and share Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety)
  • If you’re burned out as a helper or carer, talk to your doctor, a carers’ organisation or a counsellor. Respite and support are not luxuries.
  • If helping someone is becoming unsafe for you, step back and bring in professionals. You can still care from a safer distance.

How the numbers work

Every number in this book is arithmetic you can do with a pencil. None of them is a test, a diagnosis or a verdict on anyone: they describe a week, a load or a way of sharing work. The weights and cut-offs are this book’s own rules of thumb, not research findings, and you are free to disagree with them. The worked examples are the book’s own, so you can check them against the chapters.

Two habits run through all of them. Scores are rounded to two decimal places before their band is read, so the number you see and the words you see always agree. And nobody’s score is ever worked out for them: if someone’s own answer is missing, the total waits.

The load: How much are you carrying?

You score five things from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very true), about the last day or two: sleep debt, workload elsewhere, unresolved conflict, your physical state, and time pressure today.

load = (sleep + workload + conflict + body + time) ÷ 20

The answer runs from 0 to 1, and higher means heavier. Under 0.30 is a light load, 0.30 up to 0.60 a medium load, and 0.60 or more a high load. (The worksheet splits each band in two for its words: very light under 0.15, light, medium on the lighter side under 0.45, medium on the heavier side, high under 0.80, and very high from 0.80.) Your battery is the other side of the same thing, the energy you have left: a high load means a low battery. The battery is a picture in words, not a second score.

Worked example Rosa, from Part Two, Chapter III, page , scores sleep 3, workload 4, conflict 1, body 2 and time 3.

3 + 4 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 13; 13 ÷ 20 = 0.65, which is a high load.

Two or more of you: each person scores only themselves, and the shared figure is the plain average of everyone’s own scores. If any one person is at 0.60 or more, treat that as the headline, whatever the average says.

Coming back after a pause (the Calm-Down Kit): if you like numbers, take the score again after a calming round. Under 0.50, go back at the time you named. From 0.50 to 0.59, take another round. At 0.60 or more after two rounds, put it off to a named time. The kit’s line sits at 0.50 on purpose: you don’t need a light load to come back, just less than half.

Today’s Weather: the room in your day

Today’s Weather shows words, not numbers: a sky (clear, gusty or fogged in) and how much room you have today. Behind the words sits a rough tally from 0 to 100, where higher means a heavier day. You only see it if you tick “Show the numbers behind this”.

Worked example Joel, from Part One, Now (online), picks gusty, building pressure, a short night, a lot of people time, one ground condition (hungry) and one thing blowing in (work).

26 + 10 + 10 + 9 + 4 + 3 = 62: some room today, with less than usual, so the forecast says to go gently and settle first.

Workload balance: how evenly the hours are shared

Two people. Work out each person’s share of the logged hours as a percentage.

balance = 1 − (the difference between the two percentages ÷ 100)

50/50 scores 1.00; one person doing everything scores 0.00. It doesn’t matter which of you does more. Balance ignores the total, which is one reason the load sits beside it.

Worked example Rosemary logs 14 hours and Ken 10 (Part Two, Chapter II, page ). Out of 24 hours, that is 58.3% and 41.7%.

1 − (58.3 − 41.7) ÷ 100 = 1 − 0.167 = 0.83

Three or more people, or an agreed split. The same idea, said more generally: how much of the week would have to change hands to reach the target, compared with the most that ever could.

each share = that person’s hours ÷ everyone’s hours target = an even share (1 ÷ number of people), or the split you agreed moved = ½ × the sum of every |share − target| most = 1 − the smallest target share balance = 1 − moved ÷ most

Worked example Three partners log 15, 9 and 6 hours (Chapter II, page ): shares 0.50, 0.30 and 0.20, against a third each.

moved = ½ × (0.167 + 0.033 + 0.133) = 0.167; most = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667; balance = 1 − 0.167 ÷ 0.667 = 0.75. In hours, 0.167 × 30 = 5 hours a week would change hands for an even split.

With an agreed split, use it as the target: co-parents who agreed 60/40 and log 12 and 8 hours score 1.00.

Ownership: does every job have a name?

ownership = jobs with a named owner ÷ all the jobs on the list

The calculator counts a job as owned when it has one named owner. A backstop, the person who notices if it slipped, is a good habit and has its own column, but the score only needs the owner. The starter jobs on the worksheet don’t count until you give them an owner or change them. Never raise the score by deleting the jobs nobody owns: they are exactly what the score is there to show.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken list 12 regular jobs, and 9 have an owner: 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75.

Who carries more of the list. A score of 1.00 can still mean one person owns everything, so One owner per job also shows each person’s share of the list, with each job weighted by how often it comes round and how big it is:

weight of a job = how often × size how often: daily 5, weekly 1, ongoing 1, monthly 0.25, as needed 0.1 size: small 0.5, medium 1 (the default), big 2 (or, where you gave the hours each time: hours ÷ 0.5) share = the weights of that person’s jobs ÷ the weights of all the jobs

A job you decide together counts the same for each of you; on a list for two homes it shows as “Together” instead.

Worked example One person owns the cooking (daily, medium: 5) and the bills (monthly, medium: 0.25). The other owns the laundry (weekly, big: 2) and the car (as needed, medium: 0.1). Each owns two jobs, but 5.25 ÷ 7.35 = 71% of the list’s weight sits with the first person, and 29% with the second.

Who’s carrying more right now. Who did what flags the busiest person when their share of the logged minutes (or of the work done without being asked) is at least half, and at least 20 points over an even share: 70% or more for two people, 53% or more for three. In the Preface, page ’s example, one partner did 105 of the 115 minutes that were noticed and handled without anyone asking: 91%, so the sheet says so.

The setup score: can the way you share the work last?

setup score = balance × 0.40 + ownership × 0.35 + (1 − the average load) × 0.25

The weights add up to 1, so the score runs from 0 to 1, and here higher means the setup is working better, the opposite of the load. The load is the only part that counts against the score, which is why it enters as 1 − load. 0.70 or more reads as working well (the Lemonade Stand says “holding”); 0.40 up to 0.70 as needs a look (“drifting”); below 0.40 as needs a rethink, together. It is a snapshot of the setup, never a probability and never a verdict on anyone.

Worked example Rosemary and Ken (Chapter II, page ): balance 0.83, ownership 0.75, and their own load scores are 0.45 and 0.30, an average of 0.375.

0.83 × 0.40 + 0.75 × 0.35 + (1 − 0.375) × 0.25 = 0.333 + 0.263 + 0.156 = 0.75: working well.

The shortfall is what each part could have earned minus what it did earn: balance 0.40 − 0.333 = 0.067; ownership 0.35 − 0.263 = 0.088; load 0.25 − 0.156 = 0.094. The biggest shortfall is the load, which usually eases outside the chore list (more sleep, a lighter week elsewhere). The calculator’s “what would help most” is the single change with the biggest gain: handing about 2 hours a week from Rosemary to Ken would take balance from 0.83 to 1.00, worth 0.067. Naming one more job’s owner would be worth 0.029, and a load lower by 0.10 on average 0.025.

The overall score adds a fourth part, retuning: of the moments that landed badly this week, how many you retuned before answering.

overall = balance × 0.35 + ownership × 0.30 + (1 − load) × 0.20 + retuning × 0.15 (no rough moments this week: leave retuning out, and divide the first three parts by 0.85)

In Chapter II’s first worked example (balance 0.60, ownership 0.80, an average load of 0.40, and 3 of 5 rough moments retuned): 0.21 + 0.24 + 0.12 + 0.09 = 0.66.

The 30-day look-back: what shifted this month?

The look-back splits the last 30 days into four weeks (the first takes the first ten days). For each part you filled in (balance, ownership, the load, and last-minute rescues), it compares the start of the month with the end:

start = the average of weeks 1 and 2 end = the average of weeks 3 and 4 it shifted if end − start is 0.10 or more (balance, ownership, load), or 1 a week or more (rescues); anything smaller didn’t shift

One next step: a part that moved the wrong way comes first (the biggest move for its size). Otherwise, the first of these that is still true at the end: ownership under 0.70, balance under 0.70, 2 or more rescues a week, or anyone’s load at 0.60 or more. If none is, keep the same rhythm and look again in 30 days.

Hours to change hands over the month: for each person, the hours they did minus their share of everyone’s hours, added up week by week. Half the sum of those gaps (ignoring the minus signs) is how many hours would change hands to make the month even.

On your own: is your week sustainable?

The Lemonade Stand’s “just me” view adds up the hours your week is spoken for (jobs, the thinking work, care and so on) and compares them with a waking week of about 112 hours (16 waking hours, 7 days).

share = the hours spoken for ÷ 112

Under 50% reads as sustainable, 50% up to 70% as full but workable, and 70% or more as stretched. A made-up example: 50 hours spoken for is 45%, which leaves real room; 80 hours is 71%, a lot to keep up week after week.

All of these are this book’s own ways of putting things into numbers. They have not been tested in research studies, and they shouldn’t be quoted as findings. The free calculators on the website do the arithmetic for you, and each one has a “How is this worked out?” note.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Backstop
The second name on a job: someone who notices if it slipped and checks in kindly. Not a supervisor. A job counts as owned once it has an owner; the backstop is a good habit on top.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, your load: a higher load score means a heavier load, and a lower battery.
Calm-Down Kit
The Calm-Down Kit is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
Gatekeeping
Holding on to how a home or child-care job must be done, so the other person steps back. Researchers first described it in mothers (“maternal gatekeeping”), but anyone can do it.
A kind no
A kind no says what you can and can’t do right now, without blaming anyone.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. Today’s Weather calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
Ledger
A notebook where you write down what happened, so you can look at it calmly later. The book works the same way: see what is really going on, without blame, then change one small thing.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
Mental load
The noticing, planning and remembering behind running a shared life: knowing the shoes are too small, booking the appointment, keeping the list. It is real work, even though nobody sees it.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Parallel parenting
Co-parenting with as little direct contact as possible: each home runs its own way, and messages stay short, polite and about the children.
Pressure
Pressure, in Today’s Weather, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Second arrow
The extra pain we add on top of a hard moment, such as being angry at ourselves for being sad. The first arrow is the event; the second is what we tell ourselves about it (a Buddhist image).
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?: a load score from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 to 0.59 moderate, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Today’s Weather
Today’s Weather is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Unbilled debt
Unbilled debt is the work of a shared life that got done but was never noticed or thanked.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A workpaper is a short worksheet. Each person fills in their own, then you read them together.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
