How to use this guide

Thirty-eight everyday reactions, one to a page. Each page walks through the same six parts, so after a few you’ll start to see the logic in reactions this guide never mentions.

  • Dip in anywhere. Each page stands on its own. Use the contents, the section pages or “Find it fast” at the back to go straight to the situation in front of you.
  • Read it with the other person. A page can say what is hard to say in the moment. Hand it over, or read it together, and talk about the last two parts, the other side and the small step.
  • Use it both ways. Some pages may explain you, and some may explain the people around you. Either way, the aim is a fair view of both sides.
  • Scan for more. Each page has a code to the same situation on the website, free, with more pictures to choose from. The website has more than seventy situations and a box where you can type your own.
  • Build your own. The blank cards at the back walk you through the six parts for a reaction this guide doesn’t cover.
zqlensxwhyqz

The six-part lens

Why a reaction can look odd

A friend leaves the party without a goodbye. A colleague’s feedback lands like a slap. Someone says “I’m fine” when they plainly aren’t. Seen from outside, each of these can look odd, rude or over the top.

Most of them have a logic behind them. It makes sense once you can see it, and it is usually a sensible answer to how someone is built, what they learned and what kind of day they’ve had. The trouble is that the logic is hidden, so other people fill the gap with a guess, and the guess is often unkind.

This guide puts the logic into words, one step at a time, so the reaction can be understood. It also gives the other person a fair way to respond, because every page includes their side too.

No page is a diagnosis or a label. People differ, and no group always does anything. The pages say “many people” or “some people” on purpose.

zqlensxpartsqz

The six-part lens

The six parts

Take one reaction, yours or someone else’s, and walk through six parts.

  1. What it looks like. One line in camera words, what a camera would see or hear. There is no “rude” and no “weird”, because a verdict closes the question before the logic can answer it.
  2. The logic. Three or four short steps, each following from the one before. The chain starts with a plain fact about how things work, such as “Energy works like a budget”.
  3. It’s like. An everyday picture that works the same way inside. A phone on 3% shuts down the extras to protect the call, which is why it suits low energy so well.
  4. Why it fits. What makes the reaction reasonable, said fairly and without stretching it. Where research supports a page, the research notes at the back list it.
  5. The other side. What the other person may have felt or needed. This part keeps the lens fair, so nobody has to be the bad guy.
  6. One sentence and one step. Words you could actually say out loud, and a step small enough to try this week that helps both people.

The order matters. Seeing the logic first makes the other side easier to hear, and the small step comes last, once both sides are on the table.

zqlensxwaysqz

The six-part lens

It runs both ways

Every situation carries one of three small tags.

  • Common for many neurodivergent people a reaction many neurodivergent people have, explained for people who don’t share it, such as headphones at dinner or a long talk about one interest.
  • A common social habit a habit many people find natural, explained in plain logic for anyone who finds it baffling, such as small talk, “I’m fine” and hints.
  • Something anyone can do a reaction anyone can have, such as snapping when hungry or going quiet in a row.

The tag says where a reaction is common. It never tells you what the person in front of you is.

The pages about common social habits matter as much as the others. If you have ever been told you “should have known” what someone meant, it can be a relief to see that there was a system all along, and that it can be learned or simply asked about. And if you use those habits without thinking, seeing their logic written down shows where someone else might miss the signal.

The last section, “Different rulebooks”, is about habits learned in a particular place, family or generation. Two people can each be polite by their own rules and still upset each other.

zqlensxcareqz

The six-part lens

Logic explains. It doesn’t excuse.

Knowing why a reaction happened doesn’t make its effects disappear. If a reaction hurts someone, the kind and logical next step is to say so, put it right and find a better step for next time.

Some pages have a line marked “With care”. It appears where a reaction can land hard on someone else, and it says what keeps things fair.

Seeing the other side doesn’t mean agreeing to everything the other person wants. A “no” or a limit given with care is part of looking after a relationship.

If you’re reading a page with someone, start with the part about their side. People listen more easily once they feel understood.

Plans and change

Last-minute changes, surprises, lateness, and what “sometime” means.

A plan does more than fix a time. For many people it is a map of the day, already walked through in the mind. When it changes at short notice, or was never quite made, the map has to be redrawn. These pages look at what that costs, and at the friendly plans that were never meant to happen at all.

zqsitxplanxchangedqz

1Plans and change · Common for many neurodivergent people

Getting upset when a plan changes at the last minute

What it looks like

The plan changes an hour before, and the person goes quiet, tense or upset, even though the new plan sounds fine.

The logic

  1. A plan is a map the mind has already walked through
  2. A late change means redrawing the map with no warning
  3. Redrawing uses the energy that was meant for the day itself
  4. The upset is the cost of the redraw, not a verdict on the new plan

It’s like

A sat nav recalculating while you’re already on the motorway. For a moment it has no route at all.

Why it fits

For many autistic people, and for many anxious people, not knowing what comes next is costly in itself. A plan lowers that cost. Losing it at short notice is a real loss of something that was holding the day together.

The other side

The other person may have seen the change as small, or even as a nice surprise. They may feel their good intention was missed, or worry they can never change anything.

“I’m not against the new plan. I just need a few minutes to catch up with the change.”

One small step Give changes as early as you can, with the reason. The person who needs time says “Give me ten minutes and I’ll be on board,” and then comes back on board.

zqsitxlatexagainqz

2Plans and change · Something anyone can do

Running late again, despite really caring

What it looks like

Someone who clearly cares about you arrives late, again, with an apology.

The logic

  1. For some minds, time has only two settings, “now” and “not now”
  2. “Not now” turns into “now” all at once
  3. Getting ready takes longer than the mind guessed
  4. Lateness is a clock problem, not a caring problem

It’s like

A watch with only two settings, “plenty of time” and “too late”.

Why it fits

For many people with ADHD, sensing time passing is genuinely harder. Researchers describe it as part of how attention and self-control work. It says nothing about how much someone cares.

The other side

Waiting can feel like being less important, and it costs the other person real time and sometimes money. Repeated lateness can wear down trust.

“I misjudge time. You matter to me, and I’m working on it.”

One small step Set an alarm for the time to leave rather than the time to arrive, and text “Leaving now” when you go. The waiting person can agree a time to start without them.

With care Logic explains lateness; it doesn’t make the other person’s time free. A real fix (alarms, leaving earlier, a text) shows the care that the lateness hides.

zqsitxcoffeexsometimeqz

3Plans and change · A common social habit

“Let’s get coffee sometime” with no date

What it looks like

Someone says “We should get coffee sometime!” and then never sets a day.

The logic

  1. The phrase is a friendly signal rather than a booking
  2. It says “I like you and I’m open to more”
  3. A fixed date would turn it into a commitment neither of you has checked
  4. Leaving it vague keeps it warm and low-pressure for both

It’s like

A wave across the street. It means “hello, I see you”, not “come over”.

Why it fits

Many people use loose invitations to show warmth without putting anyone on the spot. Either person can turn it into a real plan later, or let it rest without anyone losing face.

The other side

Someone who takes words literally hears a plan, waits for it, and then feels let down when nothing happens. It can feel like a broken promise.

“When I say ‘sometime’, I mean I’d like to. If you want a real date, ask me for one and I’ll say yes or no.”

One small step If you want it to happen, ask “Shall we pick a day?” If you said “sometime” and don’t mean it yet, don’t worry. The other person can ask too.

zqsitxmaybexmeansxnoqz

4Plans and change · A common social habit

“Maybe” that means no

What it looks like

Someone answers an invitation with “Maybe, I’ll see,” and everyone else seems to know they won’t come.

The logic

  1. A flat “no” can feel like a door shut in someone’s face
  2. “Maybe” lets the other person down slowly
  3. Both people keep their dignity, because nobody was refused out loud
  4. Many listeners hear the no inside the maybe

It’s like

A dimmer switch instead of a light switch. The light still goes off, just gently.

Why it fits

Indirect answers are a common way to say something awkward while keeping the relationship safe. Researchers describe this kind of speech as a way for both people to avoid an open refusal.

The other side

For someone who takes words at face value, a “maybe” leaves a real question open. They plan around it, wait for an answer, and later feel misled.

“I say ‘maybe’ when I don’t want to hurt your feelings. I’ll try to give you a clear yes or no.”

One small step It’s fair to ask kindly, “Is that a maybe-yes or a maybe-no?” And a clear “No, but thank you” is kinder than it sounds.

zqsitxleftxpartyqz

5Sensory and energy · Something anyone can do

Leaving a party early, without a long goodbye

What it looks like

Partway through the party, someone slips out with a quick wave, or with no goodbye at all.

The logic

  1. Energy is a budget
  2. The party spent it faster than expected
  3. Staying past zero risks a shutdown or a sharp word
  4. Leaving quietly protects the evening and the friendship

It’s like

A phone at 3% switching off everything but the call.

Why it fits

A long goodbye costs energy too, with a round of hugs and small talk at the door. For people who find social time draining, including many autistic people, the quiet exit still leaves them able to get home safely.

The other side

The host may feel unappreciated or worry something went wrong. Friends may not know whether the person was upset.

“I had a good time. When my battery is gone I need to go quickly, before it runs out completely.”

One small step Send a short text afterwards, such as “Thanks for tonight, I had to go when my battery ran out.” Hosts can say up front, “Slip out whenever you need to.”

zqsitxheadphonesqz

6Sensory and energy · Common for many neurodivergent people

Wearing headphones or earplugs around other people

What it looks like

At a family meal or in the office, someone has headphones or earplugs in.

The logic

  1. Sound arrives whether you want it or not
  2. A brain that filters less hears all of it at once
  3. Filtering by hand uses up attention
  4. Headphones do the filtering, so attention is left for people

It’s like

Sunglasses on a bright day. You still see everything, just without squinting.

Why it fits

Many autistic people, and many people with ADHD or a sensitive nervous system, take in more of the background. Some researchers think this is partly about how much the brain predicts and smooths out what it hears. Turning the volume down can be what makes staying possible.

The other side

The people around may feel shut out, or think the person doesn’t want to talk to them.

“These help me stay at the table. Tap my arm and I’m all yours.”

One small step Agree a signal, such as a tap or a wave, that means “I’m talking to you.” Where you can, lower the noise for everyone, with the music down and one conversation at a time.

zqsitxhungryxtiredxsnapqz

7Sensory and energy · Something anyone can do

Snapping when hungry or tired

What it looks like

A small question at the end of a long day gets a sharp, short answer.

The logic

  1. Patience runs on fuel
  2. Hunger and tiredness drain the tank
  3. With an empty tank, small things feel big
  4. The snap is the fuel gauge talking, not a verdict on you

It’s like

A phone on 3% that suddenly starts closing apps.

Why it fits

Studies link poor sleep with more conflict between partners the next day, and suggest hunger can colour how harshly we judge things, especially when we don’t notice we’re hungry. The snap is real information about the body’s state.

The other side

The person who asked did nothing wrong. A sharp tone still lands on them, and it can make them feel like they have to tiptoe.

“I’m running on empty. It’s not about you. Can we talk after I’ve eaten?”

One small step Name the state before you answer (“I’m wiped”), eat or rest, then come back and repair. Keep big topics away from the hungry, tired times of day.

With care Explaining the snap doesn’t undo it. A quick “Sorry I snapped, I was starving” after the snack is part of the logic working properly.

zqsitxcancelxtiredqz

8Sensory and energy · Something anyone can do

Cancelling plans after a long week

What it looks like

On Friday afternoon a text arrives. “So sorry, I can’t make tonight.”

The logic

  1. Energy recharges with rest
  2. This week spent all of it
  3. Going out at zero means being there in body but not in mind
  4. Cancelling spares the friendship a flat, tired evening

It’s like

Not driving when you’re too tired to drive safely.

Why it fits

Rest is what makes good company possible. Sometimes the kindest thing a tired person can offer is a better evening later.

The other side

The friend may have cleared their own evening, booked a table or been looking forward to it. Last-minute cancelling can feel like they come last.

“I’m empty, not uninterested. Can we pick a new day now?”

One small step Cancel as early as you can and offer a new date in the same message. If it keeps happening, plan lighter evenings, like a walk or a takeaway at home.

With care Cancelling often, or at the last minute, does cost the other person. Saying so and offering a real alternative keeps it fair.

zqsitxalonexafterxpeopleqz

9Sensory and energy · Something anyone can do

Needing time alone after seeing people

What it looks like

After visitors leave, the person heads straight to another room and shuts the door.

The logic

  1. Time with people can be lovely and still costly
  2. The cost has to be paid back
  3. Quiet time is how it gets paid back
  4. After it, the person comes back recharged

It’s like

Wringing out a sponge so it can soak up more.

Why it fits

People recover from social time at different speeds. For many introverted people, many autistic people and many people who mask, quiet after company is how they recover, not a sign the company was bad.

The other side

A partner or host may feel shut out just when they wanted to chat about how it went, or wonder if they did something wrong.

“I had a good time. I need an hour on my own, then I’d love to hear what you thought.”

One small step Agree a recharge time after visits, with a time to come back together, such as a cup of tea at eight.

Blunt words, literal answers, hints, “I’m fine” and teasing.

The same words can carry a plain meaning and a hidden one. Some people say exactly what they mean and hear exactly what is said. Others hint, soften and tease, and expect the hidden meaning to be heard. Both are systems, and either one can be learned, or simply asked about.

zqsitxbluntqz

10Talking and literal meaning · Common for many neurodivergent people

Giving blunt, direct feedback

What it looks like

You ask for an opinion and get it straight. “The second half doesn’t work.”

The logic

  1. The goal is to help the work be right
  2. Soft words can hide the point
  3. A clear point is quicker to fix
  4. Being direct is a way of respecting your time

It’s like

A sat nav says “Turn left”, not “You might like to consider left at some point.”

Why it fits

For many autistic people, saying what they mean plainly is the honest, respectful option. Research on the “double empathy problem” suggests mix-ups between differently wired people run both ways, so the plain speaker isn’t the only one missing signals.

The other side

Words land on feelings as well as facts. The other person may have put a lot into the work and feel dismissed or attacked.

“I’m direct because I trust you with the real answer. I’m on your side.”

One small step Add one warm line before the fact, such as “There’s a lot here I like.” The listener can ask for it too. “Start with one thing that works, then tell me straight.”

With care If plain words keep hurting someone, the kind, logical move is to add the warm line, because the point lands better when the person isn’t bracing.

zqsitximxfineqz

11Talking and literal meaning · A common social habit

Saying “I’m fine” when you’re not

What it looks like

Someone is clearly having a hard day but answers every question with “I’m fine.”

The logic

  1. Feelings shared in passing can be too much to unpack right now
  2. “Fine” closes the topic politely for now
  3. It protects the moment in the queue, the meeting or the school run
  4. It often means “not here, not now” rather than “never”

It’s like

A “Back in 10 minutes” sign, closed for now and open again later.

Why it fits

“Fine” is a widely understood way to keep a hard feeling private until there is time and space for it. It lets the day keep moving without lying about anything important later.

The other side

People who take words literally believe it, then get blamed for not noticing. Others feel shut out, or keep asking and seem to pry.

“When I say ‘fine’, sometimes I mean ‘not now’. I’ll try to say ‘later’ instead.”

One small step Swap “fine” for “Not now, later tonight?” The other person can ask “Now or later?” instead of “What’s wrong?”

zqsitxhintsqz

12Talking and literal meaning · A common social habit

Hinting instead of asking

What it looks like

“It’s a bit cold in here,” someone says, and seems annoyed when nobody closes the window.

The logic

  1. A direct request can sound like an order
  2. A hint offers the idea without pressure
  3. The other person can say yes as if it were their own idea, or not
  4. Both people avoid an open “no”

It’s like

Leaving a door ajar rather than pushing someone through it.

Why it fits

Researchers who study indirect speech argue that hints let people make requests while leaving everyone room to decline without embarrassment. It is a way of being polite.

The other side

Hints get missed by many people, and not only by autistic people. The hinter then feels ignored, and the other person feels blamed for something nobody actually asked.

“When I hint, I’m trying to be gentle. I’ll say what I need out loud.”

One small step Add the ask to the hint. “It’s cold. Could you close the window?” The listener can check, “Would you like me to close it?”

zqsitxteasingqz

13Talking and literal meaning · A common social habit

Teasing as a sign of affection

What it looks like

Friends call each other names and laugh, and seem closer for it.

The logic

  1. Teasing says “we’re close enough to joke”
  2. The mock insult works because both know it isn’t true
  3. The shared laugh confirms the bond
  4. It’s a test of closeness that both pass together

It’s like

Puppies play-fighting. The teeth never close.

Why it fits

In many friendships and families, gentle teasing is a ritual that shows trust. Both people know where the line is, and the joke reminds them they share that knowledge.

The other side

Someone who takes words literally, or who was teased unkindly in the past, may hear only the insult. It can sting, and they may not feel able to say so.

“When I tease you, it’s because I feel close to you. If it ever stings, tell me and I’ll stop.”

One small step Check now and then with “Was that okay?” Agree what is off limits, and stop any joke that keeps landing as hurt.

With care Teasing that keeps hurting isn’t working as affection, whatever was meant. The logical step is to stop that joke.

zqsitxnoxeyexcontactqz

14Talking and literal meaning · Common for many neurodivergent people

Not looking at you while listening

What it looks like

While you talk, the person looks at the table, the window or their hands.

The logic

  1. Eyes carry a huge amount of information
  2. Taking in a face and words at once splits attention
  3. Looking away frees attention for the words
  4. Not looking can mean listening harder

It’s like

Closing your eyes to hear the music better.

Why it fits

For many autistic people, eye contact is uncomfortable or uses up the attention they need for listening. Studies find non-autistic people often judge autistic people quickly on style like this, even when what was said is just as good.

The other side

Many people learn that eye contact means “I’m with you”. Without it they may feel ignored or think the person is bored or hiding something.

“I’m listening. Looking away helps me hear you properly.”

One small step Check understanding with words instead of eyes, with the listener summing up one line of what they heard. Hard talks can happen side by side, on a walk or in the car.

zqsitxsmallxtalkqz

15Social rules · A common social habit

Small talk about the weather

What it looks like

Two people who barely know each other spend five minutes on the weather and the traffic.

The logic

  1. Strangers need a safe way to check each other out
  2. The weather is a topic with no risk in it
  3. Swapping easy lines shows “I’m friendly, you can relax”
  4. Once both feel safe, real talk can start

It’s like

Musicians tuning up before the concert. There’s no tune yet, but it makes the tune possible.

Why it fits

Small talk isn’t really about information. It’s a way to show goodwill and build a little trust. Studies suggest brief, friendly chats, even with people we hardly know, are linked with feeling more connected and with better days.

The other side

For people who find it pointless or tiring, including many autistic people, it costs energy and gives no new information. They may wonder why everyone pretends to care about rain.

“Small talk is how I say hello and check we’re friendly. It doesn’t have to be about the weather for long.”

One small step Have two easy lines ready and one real question (“What’s keeping you busy at the moment?”). It’s fine to steer to a topic you care about after a minute.

zqsitxwhitexlieqz

16Social rules · A common social habit

A white lie about a present

What it looks like

Someone unwraps a jumper they will never wear and says “I love it!”

The logic

  1. The gift is a message that says “I thought of you”
  2. The truth about the jumper would answer the wrong message
  3. “I love it” answers the care, not the wool
  4. The small untruth protects the bond

It’s like

Thanking a child for a drawing that looks nothing like you.

Why it fits

Many people see small, kind untruths as part of being warm. Studies find that lies told to spare someone’s feelings can actually make people trust the teller’s kindness more, though less so their honesty.

The other side

For people who value plain honesty, including many autistic people, it feels like lying, and they may wonder what else isn’t true.

“When I say I love a present, I mean I love that you thought of me.”

One small step Find something true and kind to say, like “You remembered I like blue. Thank you.” Honest people can use this too, and nobody has to lie.

Fine all day, then falling apart at home

What it looks like

At school or work everyone says they’re fine. At home, they melt down, go silent or snap.

  2. The effort is held in until it is safe to stop
  3. Home feels safe, so the holding lets go
  4. The crash at home shows home is trusted

What it looks like

  3. Silence stops bad words getting out
  4. Going quiet is braking, not punishing
