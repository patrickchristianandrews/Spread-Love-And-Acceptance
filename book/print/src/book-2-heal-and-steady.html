Stress and the nervous system: the ladder, flooding, rest and recovery

What stress does to your body and your mind, why kind people say things they don’t mean when they are flooded, and the kinds of rest that actually help you recover.

In short

  • Stress is your body getting ready for a demand. A little helps you rise to it, but too much for too long wears you down.
  • You move between three everyday states through the day, calm and connected, revved up and running on empty, and each one changes how you think and hear.
  • When you are flooded, careful thinking goes offline, so pause, let your body settle and come back at a set time.
  • Rest is a skill. Different kinds of tired need different kinds of rest, and sleep matters more than most people think.

Why the tools work. Several tools in other chapters lean on what this one explains.

  • How much are you carrying? and the stress tank (Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 55)) work because stress adds up from many small loads. A full tank makes small things feel big, so you check the tank before you judge the moment.
  • The Calm-Down Kit (Book 1, Chapter 2 (page 27)) settles the body before any words, because a flooded body can’t think or listen. Its rule about coming back at a set time keeps a pause from looking like walking out.
  • The setup score (Book 4, Chapter 2 (page 29)) counts stress as well as hours, because a split that looks fair on paper can still leave one person running on empty.

Monday, 7:52am

The bus is late, the kids can’t find a shoe, and your phone buzzes with a work message. Your partner says, “Have you seen my keys?” and you hear yourself snap, “I’m not your secretary!” A second later you wonder where that came from, because the keys were never the problem and you were already running late before anyone spoke.

Feelings are the subject of Book 1, Chapter 4 (page 67). This chapter looks at the system underneath them, your nervous system, and what happens to it under pressure. Understanding stress won’t make a busy life any quieter, but it can make your own reactions, and other people’s, a lot less mysterious.

  1. Stress is your body getting ready.

    When something needs dealing with, your body gears up. The heart speeds up, breathing quickens, muscles tense and stress hormones rise. That is useful when you need to catch a falling glass, meet a deadline or speak up in a meeting. The trouble starts when the body stays geared up for too long and never gets the chance to come back down.

    For example, a nurse handles an emergency calmly and well. Two hours later, at home, she can’t sit still and finds herself crying at an advert for car insurance, because her body is still running the emergency.

    Thoughts you could try “My body is getting ready for something, and that’s its job.” “The emergency is over. I can let my body catch up.”

    Try this. After something stressful, give your body a clear signal that it’s over, such as a walk round the block, a shower, a stretch or one song played loud.

    Dig deeper: the stress response

  2. Stress depends on what you have to meet it.

    The same event can be easy for one person and huge for another. Stress grows when a demand looks bigger than what you have to meet it, whether that is time, energy, money, help, skill or sleep. That is why a small thing can tip you over on a bad day, and why giving someone more support can shrink their stress without changing the task at all.

    Two coworkers get the same tight deadline on a Thursday. One has a quiet evening ahead, and the other has a child at home with a temperature and a partner away for work. On paper it is one task, yet one of them will lie awake over it.

    You could say “It’s less the task than how little I have left today.” “What would make this feel more doable?”

    This week. When something feels huge, write two lists side by side, one for what it asks of you and one for what you have. Then ask what could make the second list a little longer.

    Dig deeper: demands and resources

  3. The ladder: three everyday states.

    The ladder is a picture for three states your body moves between during the day. When you are calm and connected, breathing is easy and you can hear hard things. When you are revved up, you feel fast and tense, and small things seem urgent or personal. When you are running on empty, you feel flat and slow and short of words.

    Most people move up and down the ladder many times a day. The Nervous System Ladder is a thirty-second self-check that gives you a best guess at where you are right now.

    For example, a grandfather minding his toddler granddaughter all afternoon is calm at two o’clock, revved up by four and running on empty by six. Neither of them has changed, only the hour and how much of the day is left in him.

    You could say “I’m revved up right now.” “I’m running on empty. Can this wait till morning?”

    One small step. Set three reminders on your phone for today. Each time one goes off, notice which rung you are on, and leave it at noticing.

  4. When you’re flooded, thinking goes offline.

    At the top of the stress curve the body is in full alarm, and careful thinking, listening and seeing the other side all get much harder. Many people call this being flooded. You might say things you don’t mean, freeze, or only want to get out of the room. A flooded person is still a decent person whose body needs to come down before the talk can go anywhere useful.

    Two adult siblings start sorting their late father’s things. Within minutes they are shouting about a lamp that neither of them wants, while the real subject, that he is gone, sits unspoken in the boxes around them.

    You could say “I’m too flooded to listen well. Can we stop for twenty minutes and come back at four?”

    Something to try. Learn your own flood signs, such as heat in your face, a racing heart, narrowed vision or a loud inner voice. When you notice two of them at once, pause.

    Dig deeper: flooding

  5. Stress adds up, like a tank filling.

    Think of a tank that holds all the stress you are already carrying today. Stress from one place spills into the next, so traffic, a hard meeting, a noisy shop and a worrying text all fill the same tank. By evening a tiny thing makes it overflow. The last drop gets the blame even though the tank was nearly full already, and small daily hassles count as much as big events.

    For example, a teenager explodes when asked to empty the dishwasher. His mum later finds out that his closest friendship fell apart at lunchtime, and the dishwasher simply happened to be the next thing anyone asked of him.

    You could say “My tank was already full when I got home, and that wasn’t really about you.” “Rough day? You don’t have to explain.”

    Try this. Before you react to a small thing in the evening, ask yourself how full you were before it happened. Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 55) has a simple way to tell.

  6. Settle the body first.

    You can’t think your way out of a body in alarm, but you can help the body come down, and the thinking usually follows. Slow breathing with long out-breaths helps many people. So does moving, humming a familiar song, steady pressure such as a heavy blanket, or simply leaving a noisy room. What works differs from person to person, so it is worth finding your own short list on a calm day.

    A manager feels her heart race before she has to give some hard feedback. She walks once round the block, breathing out slowly, and goes into the room a little steadier than she would have been.

    Things to tell yourself “Body first, words second.” “What has helped me settle before?”

    This week. On a calm day, list three things that settle you and keep the list on your phone. The Calm-Down Kit helps you build it.

  7. Rest isn’t one thing.

    An hour on the sofa scrolling your phone may leave you more tired than before. Recovery comes in different kinds. There is sleep, there is switching off from work in your head, there is real relaxation, there is doing something absorbing that you enjoy, and there is the feeling of being in charge of your own time. Being with people restores some people and drains others, so the right rest depends on the kind of tired you are.

    For example, two housemates plan a relaxing Saturday with friends round. One of them wakes up on Sunday refreshed, and the other needs the whole of Sunday alone with a book before she feels like herself again.

    You could say “I need quiet rest tonight rather than people rest. It isn’t about you.” “What kind of tired are you?”

    One small step. Ask yourself whether you are body-tired, people-tired, brain-tired or feelings-tired, and pick a rest that matches.

  8. Sleep and stress feed each other.

    Stress makes sleep harder, and poor sleep makes stress hit harder. After a short or broken night, small things feel bigger, tempers are shorter and people pull away from each other. In a brain-scan study by Yoo and colleagues (2007), one night without sleep made the brain’s alarm centre react more strongly to upsetting pictures.

    In a daily study by Gordon and Chen (2014), people reported more conflict with their partner after nights of poor sleep. And in experiments by Ben Simon and Walker (2018), people who had missed a night’s sleep kept others at a greater distance and felt lonelier. You can’t always fix sleep, but you can notice when it is part of the story.

    A nurse coming off three night shifts finds herself in tears over a parking ticket. Her sister says, “You’ve barely slept. Let’s sort it out on Thursday.” By Thursday it is a parking ticket again, annoying and payable.

    You could say “I slept badly, so if I’m short with you today, that’s why.” “Can we talk about this after we’ve both slept?”

    Something to try. Before a hard conversation, ask whether you have both slept, and if not, pick another time. At night, try the same few calm steps before bed in the same order, so your body learns the signal. If poor sleep goes on for weeks, talk to a doctor, because sleep problems are common and often treatable.

    Dig deeper: sleep and stress

  9. Stress is easier to carry when it’s shared.

    People calm each other. A steady voice, a hand on the shoulder or just a quiet person nearby can help a nervous system settle. Saying your state out loud also helps others read you right, because “I’m stressed about work” stops a partner or friend from wondering whether it is about them. Sharing stress in this way lets someone stand beside you without asking them to carry it for you.

    For example, a father tells his ten-year-old, “I’m a bit stressed about money this month. Sorting it is my grown-up job, and you’re not in trouble.” Over the next few days his son stops tiptoeing round him and starts asking for help with his homework again.

    You could say “I’m stressed, and it isn’t about you.” “Would a hug help, or some space?”

    Try this. Tonight, tell one person your state in one sentence, and ask how they are.

In everyday life

If you live on your own, nobody is there to notice when you are running on empty, so it helps to build rest into the week before you need it. With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day, along with the rule that whoever pauses names a time to come back (One of you wants to talk now, the other needs space has more on this). A child can’t settle until the nearest grown-up does, which means your calm is something they borrow long before they learn it for themselves. At work, a flooded colleague can’t take in feedback, and waiting an hour often works better than pushing on.

Some people run on empty for reasons built into their lives, such as shift work, long-term illness or caring for someone. That calls for a different setup rather than more effort (see Different hours and Caregivers). If you have ADHD, keeping track of everything can quietly fill the tank before the day has started. It helps to move the remembering out of your head with a shared list, labelled alarms and one place for keys, and to plan hard talks for your better time of day. More in the full chapter.

Common mix-ups

  • “All stress is bad.” Short bursts of stress help you rise to a challenge. The harm comes from stress that never gets a recovery.
  • “If I’m calm, I can’t be upset.” Calm means steady. You can be calm and sad, or calm and firm.
  • “Pausing is avoiding.” A pause with a time to come back is a way to finish the talk well. Avoiding is the pause that never comes back.
  • “Rest is lazy.” Recovery is part of the work, and bodies and minds that never recover tend to break down.
  • “Running on empty means they don’t care.” Short replies and a flat face more often mean someone is worn out.

If you want to help someone else with this

A lower voice, slower words and a steady presence help a stressed person more than being told to calm down. Taking one task off someone’s list can do more than any pep talk, so ask what kind of help they want, whether practical help, company, a distraction or some space, rather than guessing. Don’t debate with someone who is flooded. Offer a break and a time to come back, because the facts will still be there in twenty minutes. And if someone has been running on empty for weeks, mention it gently and help them find support.

You could say “You seem really stretched. Is there one thing I could take off your plate?” “We don’t have to sort this now. Shall we pick it up after dinner?”

When to get more help

Stress is normal. Constant stress that never lets up is a warning sign. It may be time to see a doctor, counsellor or other trained professional if:

  • you’ve felt exhausted, dread-filled or numb most days for several weeks, even after rest;
  • you can’t sleep, or sleep but never feel rested;
  • you have physical signs that worry you, such as chest pain, constant headaches or stomach trouble (always get chest pain checked straight away);
  • you’re relying on alcohol, drugs or other habits to get through the day;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that is happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line.

If the stress comes from being afraid of someone, such as how they will react, what they will do or whether you are allowed to leave, that is not ordinary stress. Your body may be warning you. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has free, private help.

In short

  • The first minutes are about the body. Slow down, breathe out long, and step away with a time to come back.

Tuesday, 6:10pm

Your brother texts to say he can’t take Mum to the hospital on Thursday after all, with two exclamation marks after the sorry. It’s the third time this month. Your jaw sets, your face goes hot and your thumbs are already typing “TYPICAL. You never…” with the message one tap from sent.

Many of us were taught that anger was rude or dangerous, or grew up around anger that was frightening. Anger itself is a normal human feeling, though, and it often points at something real. This chapter looks first at how anger works and then at what to do with it.

  1. Anger is a signal about something that matters.

    Anger usually says that something is unfair, that someone is in your way or that a line has been crossed. It pushes us towards a problem so that we change it. It can also land on the wrong person, or be far bigger than the problem in front of it.

    For example, a woman keeps finding her lunch gone from the office fridge. By the third week her anger has told her that a line has been crossed, and it is the anger that finally gets her to raise it at the Monday meeting.

    Thoughts you could try “What is this anger trying to protect?” “Is this about fairness, a blocked plan or feeling unsafe?”

    Try this. Next time you feel angry, finish this sentence privately. “I’m angry because something I care about, which is ___, seems ___.”

    Dig deeper: what anger is for

  2. Anger often guards a softer feeling.

    Anger can arrive first because it feels stronger and safer than what sits under it, which might be hurt, fear, shame, worry or plain tiredness. Book 1, Chapter 4 (page 67) calls this layers. Finding the layer underneath often shows what you actually need.

    A man snaps at his teenage daughter for coming home an hour late. Under the anger is an hour of fear spent imagining the worst. When he tells her about that hour instead of the curfew, she hears it and says sorry.

    You could say “I’m angry, and I think underneath I was really scared.” “I came across cross, but I think I’m actually hurt.”

    This week. After the next angry moment, once you are calm, ask whether there was hurt, fear or tiredness under it.

    Dig deeper: what anger can cover

  3. Anger rises in the body fast.

    A hot face, a tight jaw, a pounding heart and a louder voice are the body getting ready for a fight, and they can arrive in seconds. When anger is high, careful thinking and listening get much harder. Chapter 1 (page ) calls this being flooded, when you are so stirred up that the thinking part of you goes quiet for a while.

    For example, arguing about the bins, a woman notices her hands shaking and realises she hasn’t heard a word her housemate has said for a minute or more, which is her sign to stop.

    Things to tell yourself “My body is ready for a fight, but I don’t have to have one.” “Where is this in my body?”

    One small step. Learn your own early signs and write down two or three of them, such as a tight jaw or talking faster. They are your cue to slow down.

  4. In the hot minutes: slow the body first.

    When anger is high, settle your body before you try to sort anything out. Stop talking or typing, breathe out slowly so the out-breath is longer than the in-breath, unclench your hands and, if you can, move away for a while. A large review of 154 studies by Kjærvik and Bushman (2024) found that activities which calm the body, like slow breathing, relaxation and mindfulness, reduced anger, while ones that rev you up showed little or no benefit.

    A dad feels his temper rising while he helps with fractions homework. He says, “I’m getting frustrated, and that’s not your fault. I’m going to get a glass of water and come back in five minutes.” He comes back in five, and they finish the sheet together.

    You could say “I’m too angry to talk well right now. I’ll come back at eight.” “I need ten minutes. I’m coming back to this conversation.”

  5. Letting rip doesn’t empty it.

    Many people believe anger works like steam, so that shouting, slamming a door or hitting a pillow lets it out and leaves you calmer. Research doesn’t support this. In a well-known study by Bushman (2002), angry people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them ended up angrier than before.

    Going over what happened again and again in your head has a similar effect. In studies by Bushman and colleagues (2005), people who dwelt on a provocation were later more aggressive, even towards someone who only mildly annoyed them. Anger tends to fade faster when the body is calmed and the mind has something else to do for a while.

    For example, after a row with a coworker, a man spends his lunch break telling three people how awful she is, and by the afternoon he is angrier than he was at noon. The next time it happens he takes a walk instead, and by two o’clock he can think about the problem clearly.

    Thoughts you could try “Going over this again is feeding it.” “I can come back to this later, when I’m calmer.”

    Try this. If you catch yourself replaying an argument, set a timer for twenty minutes and do something absorbing, then come back to the problem.

    Dig deeper: venting and going over it

  6. Step back and look from the outside.

    Once the body has settled a little, it helps to look at the moment as if you were watching from across the room. What happened, and what might the other person have been dealing with? In experiments by Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman (2012), people who looked back on an annoying moment “like a fly on the wall”, instead of reliving it, felt less angry and acted less aggressively. Rethinking what happened, which psychologists call reappraisal (Gross, 1998), helps too.

    Furious that a friend has cancelled again, a woman imagines telling the story to a kind stranger on a train. Telling it that way, she remembers that her friend is caring for a sick parent, and the anger turns slowly into worry for her.

    Things to tell yourself “If I were watching this from the side, what would I see?” “What else might be going on for them?”

    This week. Write two lines about the moment that start with your own name in place of “I”, as in “Wes got angry because…”

  7. Say it, don’t throw it.

    Anger is often a fair message sent in a hurtful way. Once you are calmer, you can still say that you were angry, and why, without attacking anyone. A simple shape helps. Say what happened, how you felt and what you are asking for.

    For example, in place of “You always let me down,” a woman says to her sister, “When you cancelled Mum’s appointment again, I felt really angry and alone with it. Can we make a plan for who takes her over the next three months?”

    You could say “I was angry about what happened on Monday. Can I tell you why?” “When ___ happened, I felt ___, and what I’m asking for is ___.”

    One small step. Write your angry message, then rewrite it in that shape before you send anything. Book 3, Chapter 2 (page 25) has more on saying hard things.

  8. After anger, repair.

    Everyone gets angry, and most of us say something sharp now and then. What happens next matters a great deal. A short, real repair helps rebuild trust, which means naming what you did without a “but” and saying what you will do differently.

    A grandmother shouts at her grandson for breaking a vase, and he cries. An hour later she sits beside him on the stairs and says, “I shouted, and that was scary. You matter far more to me than the vase. I’m sorry.” He leans against her shoulder and asks whether they can glue it.

    You could say “I was sharp with you earlier. That wasn’t fair, and I’m sorry.” “I’m still upset about what happened, and I also shouldn’t have spoken to you that way.”

    Something to try. Think of one recent moment when your anger landed on someone. Is there a short repair you could still make? Chapter 5 (page ) has more on apologies that land.

  9. When anger becomes harm.

    Feeling angry is human. But some things done in anger are harm, whatever the feeling behind them: hitting, pushing, throwing things at or near someone, breaking their things, threatening, shouting so someone is scared, or using anger to control what another person does, wears, spends or who they see. People walking on eggshells around you is a sign too. Anger is never a reason someone deserves to be hurt.

    For example, a man realises his partner and children go quiet when he comes home in a bad mood. He doesn’t hit anyone, but they are afraid of him. He talks to his doctor and joins an anger course.

    You could say “I think my anger is scaring people I love. I want help with it.” (If it’s happening to you) “I’m frightened of how they get when they’re angry.”

In everyday life

Anger at yourself counts too. “I’m so stupid” is anger turned inward, and it helps to speak to yourself as you would to a friend who had made the same mistake (Book 1, Chapter 5 (page 85) goes further). With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day and a time you will always come back. Children copy what we do with anger far more than what we say about it. At work, anger is common and rarely said out loud, and “I’m frustrated about the deadline change. Can we talk about what’s realistic?” keeps it useful.

If you have ADHD, anger can arrive fast and big and then pass quickly, so plan for the speed with a three-word pause line such as “Back in ten” and a rule to wait before you send anything. If you are managing a clash between two other people, hear each of them alone first and then together, about what happened and never about each other. The fight is often about the setup, such as two people who both believe they own the same job. The full chapter has a worked example.

Common mix-ups

  • “Anger is a bad feeling.” Anger is a normal feeling that carries information. It is some of the things done in anger that cause harm.
  • “Letting it out is healthy.” Shouting or hitting things tends to keep anger going, and calming the body before talking helps more.
  • “They made me angry, so it’s their fault I shouted.” Someone can do something unfair. What you do next is still yours.
  • “Holding it in is the safe option.” Anger swallowed for years tends to come out sideways, as coldness, sarcasm or a sudden explosion. Saying it calmly and early is safer.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Keep yourself safe first. If someone’s anger scares you, step away. You don’t have to stay and calm them down.
  • Lower, slower, fewer words. Speak quietly and simply, and leave the argument until they have come down.
  • Hear the message under it. There is often something fair under the anger. You can take that part seriously and still say that the way it was said wasn’t okay.

You could say “I want to hear this, and I can’t while we’re both shouting. Can we try again at seven?” “I can tell this really matters to you.”

When to get more help

Everyone gets angry. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or an anger management course if:

  • your anger feels out of control, or you often regret what you do or say when angry;
  • people close to you seem nervous or walk on eggshells around you;
  • you have hit, pushed, thrown things, broken things or threatened anyone;
  • anger is hurting your work, your friendships or your health;
  • you are using alcohol or drugs to cope with angry feelings.

Help for anger works for many people: a review of anger treatments by Del Vecchio and O’Leary (2004) found medium to large benefits. It is good for your health too: a review by Chida and Steptoe (2009) linked anger and hostility with a higher risk of heart disease. Asking for help is a responsible thing to do. Sunday, 10:50pm

The house is quiet and you have checked the front door twice. Tomorrow is the hospital appointment, and the same three thoughts keep going round. What if it’s bad, what if you can’t cope, and what if there is nobody to tell? Your chest is tight and your mind won’t stop. If you are honest, you haven’t really wanted to see anyone for a few weeks either, and even replying to messages has started to feel like too much effort.

Almost everyone worries, feels low or goes through something frightening at some point. For some people these feelings settle by themselves, and for others they stick and grow until they shape every day. This chapter explains how they work, what tends to help and when it is time to ask for more help. It can’t diagnose anyone. If any of this is about you right now, you are not alone, and things can get better.

  1. Worry and anxiety are the body’s alarm.

    Fear is the body’s alarm for danger that is here now. Anxiety is the same alarm ringing about something that might happen, and worry is the thinking part, the “What if…?” that plays on repeat. All three run through the stress system described in Chapter 1 (page ), with a racing heart, a tight chest, shallow breathing, a churning stomach and poor sleep. The alarm is trying to protect you. The trouble is that it can be set too sensitive, and it can’t tell a real tiger from a worrying email.

    For example, a teenager’s heart pounds before every class presentation. Nothing bad has ever happened in one, yet her alarm still treats a room of classmates as if it were dangerous.

    Things to tell yourself “This is my alarm talking, and alarms can be wrong.” “My body is getting ready to protect me. I can thank it and carry on.”

    Try this. When worry rises, breathe out slowly five times, with each out-breath longer than the in-breath, and then name three things you can see. It tells the body that the danger isn’t here right now.

    Dig deeper: how anxiety works

  2. Avoiding keeps fear going.

    When something makes us anxious, stepping away brings quick relief, and the relief teaches the brain that escaping was a good idea. Next time the fear is a little bigger and the world a little smaller. What helps is going back towards the feared thing in small, chosen steps and staying long enough to learn that you can cope. Therapists call this exposure. Craske and colleagues (2014) describe how it works best when each step tests what you expect to happen.

    After a panic attack on a bus, a man starts walking everywhere, even in the rain. With his sister beside him he rides one stop, then two, and some weeks later a whole journey into town, while his brain slowly learns that buses are survivable.

    Things to tell yourself “What do I think will happen? Let’s see what actually happens.” “Uncomfortable isn’t the same as dangerous.”

    This week. Pick one small thing you’ve been avoiding. Break it into three steps, from easiest to hardest. Do the easiest one this week.

    Give worry a time and a place.

    Trying hard not to worry rarely works. What helps many people is postponing it, which means noticing a worry, writing it down and saving it for a set “worry time” later in the day. In an early study by Borkovec and colleagues (1983), people who practised this for four weeks reported worrying less each day. In worry time, ask of each worry whether there is something you can do about it. If there is, plan one step, and if there isn’t, practise letting it go.

    For example, a father caring for his own dad keeps a worry notebook by the kettle. At six in the evening he gives it fifteen minutes, by which time many of the morning’s worries already look smaller.

    Thoughts you could try “Noted. I’ll think about that at six.” “Can I do anything about this today? If not, it can wait.”

    One small step. Choose a fifteen-minute worry time well before bed, and keep a note on your phone for worries that pop up in between.

  4. Low mood is common. Depression is more than a bad week.

    Everyone has low days, and sadness after a loss or a disappointment is normal. Depression is different. It means low mood, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy, for most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, often with poor sleep, tiredness, changes in appetite, feeling worthless or guilty, and finding it hard to think. It can look like sadness, or like irritability, numbness or simply running on empty. It is a health problem, and nobody gets it by being weak.

    A retired teacher notices he has stopped going to his walking group, isn’t reading, and wakes at four every morning. He tells himself he is just getting old, until his daughter gently suggests it might be depression and that depression can be treated.

    You could say “I’ve not been myself for a few weeks, and I don’t think it’s just tiredness.” “I don’t enjoy things I used to. Can I talk to you about it?”

    Something to try. If you’ve felt low or lost interest most days for two weeks or more, book a talk with a doctor this week. Write down what you’ve noticed to take with you.

    Dig deeper: low mood and depression

  5. Doing often comes before feeling better.

    Low mood says to wait until you feel like it. Withdrawing makes life emptier, though, and that deepens the low. A well-tested approach called behavioural activation turns this round. You plan small, doable things that bring a bit of pleasure, meaning or achievement, and you do them whether you feel like it or not. A review of 26 trials by Ekers and colleagues (2014) found it helped with depression, though many of the studies were small.

    For example, a woman living alone after her divorce plans three small things a day, a ten-minute walk, one call or message and one task she can finish. Some days they feel pointless, and she does them anyway. Over a few weeks the days begin to feel less flat.

    Things to tell yourself “I don’t have to feel like it to do it.” “Small counts.”

    Try this. Write down three tiny things for tomorrow, one for pleasure, one for connection and one you can finish, and tick them off however small they are.

    Dig deeper: behavioural activation

  6. Sleep, moving and people do a lot of the work.

    These sound too simple to matter, but the evidence for them is solid. Improving sleep can lift mood. In a large trial with university students, Freeman and colleagues (2017) found that an online sleep course reduced insomnia, and with it some other mental health difficulties.

    Exercise helps depression too. A review of 218 trials by Noetel and colleagues (2024) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all helped. Belonging to groups also protects. Cruwys and colleagues (2013) found that people with depression who joined groups were less likely to relapse.

    A new dad, exhausted and flat, agrees with his partner on one protected long sleep each weekend each, a pram walk every morning, and a weekly game of five-a-side.

    Words you could use “I need help protecting my sleep for a while.” “Would you come for a short walk with me? I find it hard to go on my own.”

  7. Trauma is about what happened, and what it left behind.

    Trauma usually means going through, or seeing, something that threatened life or safety, such as violence, abuse, a serious accident, war, or the sudden death of someone close. It is very common. In surveys across 24 countries, Kessler and colleagues (2017) found about 7 in 10 adults had been through at least one.

    Most people recover over time. A smaller number develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with memories that come back vividly, avoiding reminders, feeling on high alert, and sleep problems. Ordinary hard times and stress hurt too, even when they aren’t trauma in this sense.

    For example, months after a car crash, a woman still jumps at screeching brakes and avoids driving at night. Her alarm learned that cars can mean danger and has not yet unlearned it, which is a long way from overreacting.

    Things to tell yourself “My reactions make sense after what happened.” “It’s in the past, even if my body doesn’t feel that yet.”

    One small step. If something frightening happened more than a month ago and it still shapes your days, talk to a doctor. Trauma-focused talking therapies help many people.

    Dig deeper: what trauma is and isn’t

  8. After something frightening: safety, choice and no pressure.

    After a frightening event, people need safety, practical help, warmth and choice. They don’t need to be made to talk. In fact, a review of single-session “debriefing” after trauma by Rose and colleagues (2002) found it did not prevent PTSD, and might make things worse for some. Services that are “trauma-informed” (SAMHSA, 2014) put safety, trust, choice and working together first. The same ideas help in families, friendships and workplaces.

    After a break-in, a grandmother doesn’t want to talk about it. Her grandson doesn’t push. He fixes the lock, stays the night, and says, “If you ever want to talk about it, I’m here.” A week later, she does.

    Ways to say it “You don’t have to tell me anything. What would help right now?” “You’re safe now. I’m staying.”

    Something to try. If someone you love has been through something frightening, ask one practical question (“What do you need today?”) and one open one (“How are you doing, really?”), and let them choose.

    Dig deeper: trauma-informed care

  9. Talking therapies help many people, and medicines can too.

    Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) teaches practical ways to notice and change patterns of thinking and doing. It is one of the most studied talking therapies, and a review of over a hundred meta-analyses by Hofmann and colleagues (2012) found the strongest support for anxiety problems and stress, and support for many other problems too.

    Guidelines such as NICE (2022) also recommend guided self-help, behavioural activation, exercise groups and, for some people, antidepressants. Questions about medicine, including starting, changing or stopping it, are for a doctor or prescriber. Please don’t stop a medicine suddenly without advice.

    For example, a young man is embarrassed to ask for help with panic attacks. His GP refers him to a short course of CBT, and after eight sessions he is taking the train to work again, with a book and a bottle of water for the bad days.

    Words to try “I’d like to talk about my mood. It’s been going on for a while.” “What are my options, and what do you recommend for someone like me?”

    Try this. Before an appointment, write three lines about what you have noticed, how long it has lasted and what it is stopping you doing. It makes the first conversation easier.

A friend who keeps cancelling may be struggling, so keep inviting them, with no pressure to say yes.

Common mix-ups

  • “Anxiety means something is wrong with me.” Anxiety is a normal alarm. It becomes a problem when it rings too loud or too often, or starts shrinking your life.
  • “Depression is just sadness. Snap out of it.” Depression affects sleep, energy, thinking and body as well as mood. Nobody snaps out of it, but many people recover with the right help.
  • “Everything bad that happens is trauma.” Trauma usually means threat to life or safety. Other hard things still hurt and still deserve care, and the word doesn’t need to fit for help to be fair.
  • “Talking about trauma straight away helps everyone.” Some people want to talk, some don’t. Forcing it can make things worse. Safety and choice come first.
  • “Asking someone about suicide puts the idea in their head.” It doesn’t. A review by Dazzi and colleagues (2014) found no evidence that asking increases suicidal thoughts, and it may help.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Notice and ask. Something like “You’ve seemed low lately. How are you doing, really?” opens the door, and then it helps to listen more than you talk.
  • Leave the feeling alone. “Cheer up” and “It could be worse” push people away, while “That sounds really hard” keeps them close.
  • Offer something specific, such as a walk, a meal, a lift to the doctor or an evening sitting together. A small, practical offer is easier to accept than a big, vague one.
  • Ask directly about safety if you’re worried. “Are you having thoughts of ending your life?” If yes, stay with them, and get help together today.
  • Look after yourself too. Supporting someone who is struggling is tiring. See Book 3, Chapter 7 (page 115).

Words you could use “I’m not trying to fix it. I just want you to know I’m here.” “Would it help if I came with you to the doctor?”

Six months on, you are doing fine, or so you tell people. Then in the supermarket you see the cereal your dad always bought, the one with the faded blue box, and you have to leave your trolley in the aisle and go and sit in the car. Nobody around you knows why.

This chapter is for anyone who has lost someone or something, whether a person, a pet, a marriage, a friendship, their health, a home, a job, a country or a future they had pictured. It looks at how grief works and then at what can help. There is no right way to grieve, and nothing here is a timetable.

  1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone, and it isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted away or children leaving home can all bring real grief. Its size usually matches the size of the bond, whatever other people think the loss ought to mean. A man may be surprised by how hard he takes his dog’s death while his friends tell him it was only a dog, yet that dog walked beside him every morning for twelve years, through two jobs and a divorce.

    It can help to say a loss out loud to yourself, especially one you have never called grief. “I’m grieving the friendship” or “I’m grieving the life we planned” is a small sentence that often brings some relief, because it gives the feeling a name and a reason.

    Dig deeper: what grief is, and what counts

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages of grief, running from denial through anger, bargaining and depression to acceptance. They came from listening to people who were dying, and they were never meant as steps that everyone follows in order. Most people swing back and forth, spending some hours deep in the loss and some hours getting on with life, and that swing is healthy. A widow may feel guilty for enjoying lunch with her sister and then cry all evening, and both the lunch and the tears are part of grieving.

    Letting yourself have a small break from grief, such as a walk, a programme you like or a meal with a friend, takes nothing away from the person you lost. When a wave comes, it usually passes, even if it knocks you over first.

    Dig deeper: waves, and the swing between loss and life

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, poor sleep, no appetite or extra appetite, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there has gone, and that learning takes time and uses energy. A teacher whose mother has died keeps losing her keys and forgetting meetings, and she starts to worry that something is wrong with her memory. It is grief fog, and it eases as the months pass, though nobody can tell her exactly when.

    While it lasts, protect a few basics, such as one regular meal, a set bedtime and a short walk outside. It is fine to tell people at work or at home, “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

    Dig deeper: the grieving body and brain

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain to anyone else. Someone may still be alive but changed, through dementia, addiction or illness, or gone without having died, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Other losses go unrecognised, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pregnancy loss or a friend you only ever knew online. A woman whose father has dementia still visits him every Sunday, but the dad who knew her is fading, and she is grieving someone who hasn’t died. Grief without a ceremony, or without anyone noticing, can feel very lonely.

    If a loss of yours had no ceremony, you can make a small one of your own by lighting a candle, writing a letter, planting something or visiting a place that mattered. You might also tell one person, “I’m grieving something people might not see as a loss. It is one for me.”

    Dig deeper: ambiguous and unrecognised grief

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying, and others by doing, whether that means fixing things, organising, working or building something. Neither way is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks another isn’t grieving when they are grieving differently, and culture, faith, age and how your mind works all shape the way it comes out. After their son’s death, one parent wants to talk about him every evening while the other spends every weekend in the garage restoring his old bike. Each feels the other is getting it wrong, and each is grieving the same boy.

    A question can bridge the gap. “What has helped you most, even a little?” asked of someone who shares your loss, and listened to without correcting, often shows that the two of you want the same thing by different roads.

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said that healthy grief meant moving on and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently. People often keep a bond with the person they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head and values they carry forward, so the relationship changes shape without having to end. A teenager wears her grandfather’s watch to every exam. She doesn’t believe in luck, and she would find it hard to explain why the watch matters, except that it feels as if he is in the room.

    You might choose one way of keeping a bond, such as cooking their recipe, telling a story about them at a family meal or keeping one of their habits. Some people find it helps to ask, in a hard moment, what the person they lost would have said.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retirement, moving country, a child starting school, coming out or getting well after a long illness can all bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts, an ending, a confusing time in between and a new beginning, and the middle part is both the hardest and the easiest to rush. A couple moves to a new city for a job they both wanted. Six months in, one of them feels low and then guilty for feeling low, because this was the plan. What they also lost, though, was a circle of friends, a favourite café and a whole way of life.

    You can be glad about a change and still miss what came before. For one change you are going through, it may help to write down what ended, what still feels unclear and one small thing that is new and good.

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that, over time, many people find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, even though they never forget. Being with people who let you talk, or let you stay quiet, helps. For some people, though, grief stays at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard.

    Doctors call this prolonged grief. It means a strong longing for the person, or being taken over by the loss, most days, still going many months after the death (6 to 12 months or more, depending on the guideline) and getting in the way of life. It has nothing to do with weakness, and there is specific help for it. A year after his wife died, a man still can’t go into their bedroom or see his friends, and his doctor refers him to a grief counsellor. Things begin to move slowly, starting with one evening a week at his brother’s.

    On a good day, write down two people you could text on a hard one, and one place you could call if those people aren’t enough. If it has been a long time and nothing is easing, it is reasonable to say, “I think I need more help.”

In everyday life

After a miscarriage, one partner may want to talk while the other goes quiet, and each can end up worrying that the other doesn’t care.   1. Hurt is real, and healing takes time.

    Being hurt by someone shows that something mattered, whether the person, your trust or your sense of being respected. The mind replays hurts because it is trying to understand them and keep you safe. Healing rarely runs in a straight line, and the feeling can come back on an anniversary or when something reminds you, without meaning you have failed.

    Months after a friend said something cruel at a party, Kwesi feels a jolt when the song that was playing comes on the radio. He tells himself it is an old bruise being pressed, and turns the volume down.

    Thoughts you could try “This hurts because it mattered.” “A feeling coming back doesn’t mean I’m back at the start.”

    Try this. When a hurt replays, notice it, name it (“there’s that memory again”), and gently bring your attention to something in the room you can see or touch.

  2. Every close relationship has breaks. Repair is what counts.

    No relationship is smooth all the time, and people snap, forget, misread and let each other down. Research on parents and babies, on couples and on therapy points the same way. Breaks are normal, and what matters most is whether they get mended. A small repair, made soon, often leaves a relationship feeling safer than before.

    For example, a dad snaps at his son for spilling juice. Twenty minutes later he sits down next to him and says, “I was grumpy and I shouted. That wasn’t fair. Spills happen. Want to help me make a new one?”

    You could say “Can I have a do-over? That came out badly.” “I didn’t like how I spoke to you earlier. I’m sorry.”

  5. Forgiveness isn’t forgetting, excusing or going back.

    Forgiveness is usually described as letting go of resentment and the wish to get even, and it happens inside you. Forgiving someone doesn’t mean saying that what happened was okay, or forgetting it, or trusting the person again, or keeping them in your life. It is a choice, often a slow one, and you never owe it on someone else’s timetable.

The plan was a walk before work, and the trainers are by the door. Somehow the phone is in your hand instead, and at 7:40 you are still scrolling beside a cup of cold coffee. By evening a quiet voice is telling you that you never stick to anything, though what you actually ran into this morning was a habit with years of practice behind it.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    Much of what we do each day runs on autopilot, which saves energy and is the reason habits exist at all. The downside is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one, so change asks your brain to do something effortful again and again until the new way becomes the easy one.

    For example, Jodie wants to stop snapping at her children in the morning rush, and she means it every night. Every morning the same rush brings the same snap, somewhere between the lost reading book and the cold toast, because the morning has worn a groove deeper than her good intentions.

    Things to tell yourself “This is hard because it’s a habit.” “Old grooves take time to fill in.”

    Try this. Pick one thing you would like to change and write down when it usually happens, where, and what is going on just before.

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger, such as a time, a place, a feeling, a person or the thing you have just done. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward, like relief, comfort or a little buzz, keeps it going. Once you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    A man notices that he reaches for snacks when he opens his laptop after dinner, whether he is hungry or not. Since the laptop is the cue, he starts making a pot of tea before he opens it.

    Things to tell yourself “What happened right before I did that?” “What was I getting from it?”

    This week. For three days, each time the habit happens, jot down the time, the place, the feeling and what you were doing just before.

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring but easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and they work because they are easy enough to do on a bad day. It helps to tie the new step to something you already do, as in “After I put the kettle on, I’ll…”, and to grow it only once it has stuck.

    For example, two housemates want a tidier kitchen. They have been promising each other a big weekly clean for months, so they agree instead that after dinner each of them wipes one surface. It takes two minutes, and by the second week the hob has stopped being a source of tension.

    You could say “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I ..., I will ...”

    One small step. Shrink your change until it takes two minutes or less. Write it as “After I ___, I will ___.”

  5. Being in two minds is normal.

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

Common mix-ups

  • “If I really wanted it, I’d just do it.” Wanting is only the start, and habits, setup, tiredness and stress all play a part.
  • “It takes 21 days to form a habit.” There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range.
  • “Being hard on myself will keep me on track.” Harshness tends to make people give up, while kindness makes it easier to try again.
  • “Routines mean being rigid.” For many people, especially autistic people, routines are what make life manageable. Change can respect that.

The week ahead is full, and none of it feels like yours. You aren’t sad exactly. You just can’t remember the last time you did something because it mattered to you, rather than because it was next on the list.

This chapter is about values, the things you care about and the direction you want your life to face, and about what makes a life feel worth living. It borrows from psychology and from philosophy, which has been asking how we should live for more than two thousand years. You don’t need to have read any of it, and everything here is in plain words.

  1. Values are directions, not finish lines.

    A goal is something you can tick off, such as running a 5k, painting the hall or passing a test. A value is a direction you keep walking in, such as looking after your health, making your home feel cared for or being a fair friend. Nobody ever finishes being kind, which is exactly why a value still works on a day when a goal falls apart.

    Bethan planned to go to the gym three times this week and went once, and on Thursday evening she felt like a failure. When she asked herself what the gym was for, the answer was looking after her body so that she had energy for her children. On Friday she walked for twenty minutes at lunchtime, round the park behind the office, and it counted. If you have a goal that has stalled, try asking “what is this for?” three times in a row, and write down where you end up.

    Dig deeper: goals and values

  2. Your strong feelings point to what you value.

    You don’t have to invent your values from nothing, because they show up in your strongest reactions. Anger often means that something you care about has been stepped on, envy can point to something you want for yourself, and sadness often shows what you love. Sacha is furious when a colleague takes credit for the team’s work. Under the anger is a value, which is fairness and seeing people’s effort, and once he names it he can raise fairness at the next team meeting instead of seething at one person for a week. He might say, “I think I’m upset because fairness really matters to me, more than winning this one.”

    Think of the last time you felt strongly, in a good way or a bad one, and finish the sentence “That mattered to me because I care about…”

    Dig deeper: finding your values

  3. Two good things can pull in different directions.

    Most hard choices are between two good things, such as honesty and kindness, freedom and safety, or family and rest. Feeling torn usually means you care about more than one thing, and even when you choose well it is normal to feel a little sad about the side you didn’t choose. A mum cares about being honest and about protecting her eight-year-old. When the family dog is very ill, she finds a way to honour both and tells the truth gently, in words a child can hold. “Biscuit is very poorly. The vet is helping, and we’ll look after him together.”

    If you feel stuck on a choice, write down the good thing on each side. It often helps to ask whether you can honour some of both, even if you can’t have all of either.

    Dig deeper: when values clash

  4. Put your effort where it can land.

    About two thousand years ago, a teacher called Epictetus, who had been born into slavery, taught that some things are up to us and some aren’t. Your own choices, effort and treatment of other people are up to you, while other people’s choices, the weather and the past are not. Worry tends to collect in the second pile, and values do their work in the first. Ingrid wants her partner to enjoy Christmas with her family, and she can’t make him enjoy it. She can plan a shorter visit, include a walk on Boxing Day that he will like and be warm herself, and that is her part.

    One way to try this is to write a worry in two columns, the parts that are up to you and the parts that aren’t, then pick one small thing from the first column and do it.

  5. A good life is more than a good mood.

    Feeling good matters. Many thinkers, from the Greek philosopher Aristotle to psychologists today, also describe a second kind of wellbeing, which comes from growing, using your strengths, being a good friend and doing things that matter even when they are hard. Aristotle called it flourishing, and he thought we build it the way we build any skill, by practice, a little at a time. Jade spent a month helping a friend move out of a damp, unhappy flat. It was tiring and not much fun, and when she looks back on her year she counts it among the best months of it.

    Tonight you might write down one thing that felt good today and one that felt worthwhile, and notice whether they turn out to be the same thing.

  7. Different values aren’t wrong values.

    Two people can share a home, a family or a team and still put the same good things in a different order, with one putting safety first and another adventure. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that there are many real goods in life and that they can’t all be ranked on one scale, which leaves room to disagree without either person being bad. Jasper values a calm, tidy home, and his housemate Darnell values an open door and friends dropping by. For months each privately thought of the other as messy or uptight. Once they named what each of them cared about, they could make a plan, and Friday nights became open house while Sundays stayed quiet.

    In your next disagreement, it may help to ask, “What matters to you about this?”, and to listen for the value underneath the position.

The Chinese teacher Confucius taught that small, everyday courtesies shape who we become, and later thinkers who wrote about care said that being good starts with paying attention to the person in front of you. In everyday life

Partners may rank the same values differently, such as security and adventure, and it helps to say what each of you is protecting before you argue about the plan. If you want to help someone else with this

Asking “What matters most to you about this?” helps more than telling someone what they ought to care about. People often see their values for the first time in someone else’s words, so it can help to notice them out loud, as in “You stayed late to help your friend. Loyalty really matters to you, doesn’t it?” With children, naming the value when you see it teaches more than a lecture. Try not to use values as a weapon, because “I thought you valued family” lands as a guilt trip. You can disagree with someone’s choice and still see the good thing they were reaching for.

You could say “What would a good outcome look like to you?” “What do you want to be able to say about how you handled this, a year from now?”

When to get more help

Feeling a bit lost or flat for a while is part of being human. But some signs mean it’s worth talking to someone trained:

  • Life has felt empty or pointless for more than two weeks, and things you used to enjoy don’t reach you. That can be depression, which is common and treatable. A doctor or counsellor is a good first step.
  • You have thoughts of not wanting to be alive. Please talk to someone today. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • Someone in your life is pushing you to give up what matters to you, cutting you off from friends, or making you afraid to disagree. That’s about control, not values. The page When it’s more than a rough patch can help you think it through.

Some therapies, such as acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), work directly with values. A counsellor can help you find yours when everything feels foggy.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Calm-Down Kit
The Calm-Down Kit is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
If-then plan
A small plan written as “If this happens, then I will do that”, which makes a new habit easier to start (Peter Gollwitzer).
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Prolonged grief
Grief that stays intense and gets in the way of daily life for a long time after a death (usually a year or more for adults). It is treatable; a GP or doctor is a good first step.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
Rumination
Going over the same worry or regret again and again without getting closer to an answer.
Second arrow
The extra pain we add on top of a hard moment, such as being angry at ourselves for being sad. The first arrow is the event; the second is what we tell ourselves about it (a Buddhist image).
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?: a load score from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 to 0.59 moderate, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Window of tolerance
The range in which you can feel strong feelings and still think and talk. Above it you are flooded or revved up; below it you shut down. A phrase from the psychiatrist Dan Siegel.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A workpaper is a short worksheet. Each person fills in their own, then you read them together.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
