Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

Stress and the nervous system: the ladder, flooding, rest and recovery

What stress does to your body and your mind, why kind people say things they don’t mean when they’re flooded, and which kinds of rest actually put something back.

Monday, 7:52am

The bus is late. One school shoe is missing, last seen under the sofa on Saturday. Your phone buzzes with a message from work that begins “Quick one,” which it never is. From the hall your partner calls, “Have you seen my keys?” and you hear yourself shout, “I’m not your secretary!”

A second later you’re standing there wondering where that came from. It wasn’t the keys. You were behind before anyone had said a word, before the shoe went missing, probably before you were even out of bed.

Feelings got their own chapter (Book 1, Chapter 4, page 61). This one goes a layer down, to the nervous system underneath them, and what it does when the pressure’s on. Knowing how stress works won’t make Monday any quieter. It does make your own reactions, and other people’s, a lot less mysterious.

It also explains why some of the tools elsewhere work at all. The stress tank in Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 51) asks how full you are before you judge the moment, because stress piles up from small loads. The Calm-Down Kit in Book 1, Chapter 2 (page 25) settles the body before any words, and it asks you to name a time to come back, so a pause doesn’t look like walking out. And the setup score in Book 4, Chapter 2 (page 27) counts stress as well as hours, since a split can look fair on paper and still leave one person running on empty.

In quick strokes: the surface is a missing shoe. Underneath it, a body already in alarm before anyone spoke. Underneath that, a week with no recovery in it. The roots are limited capacity and state and setting. Now one at a time, starting with the body.

  1. Stress is your body getting ready.

    Something needs dealing with, and your body gears up before you’ve decided anything. Heart faster, breath quicker, muscles tight, stress hormones up. Which is exactly what you want when a glass is sliding off the table. The trouble starts when the body stays geared up for hours and never gets the message to stand down.

    Morag is a nurse. She handles a cardiac arrest on the ward calmly and well. She gets home at nine, eats half a sandwich standing up at the counter, and at 10:30 finds herself sobbing at an ad for car insurance. Car insurance has never moved her to anything before. Her body is still running the emergency it thinks she’s in.

    So after something stressful, give your body a clear sign that it’s over, whether that’s a walk around the block or a hot shower or one song played much too loud in the car on the way home. The body takes its cue from what you do far more than from what you tell it.

    Dig deeper: the stress response

  2. Stress depends on what you have to meet it.

    The same deadline lands on two colleagues on a Thursday afternoon, and one of them has a quiet evening ahead and a takeout menu. The other has a seven-year-old at home with a temperature and a partner away in Chicago till Saturday. On paper it’s one task. Guess which of them is awake at 3am.

    Stress grows when a demand looks bigger than what you’ve got to meet it with, whether that’s time or energy or money or help or sleep. That’s why a small thing tips you over on a bad day. It’s also why giving someone more support can shrink their stress without changing the job at all. When something feels huge, I’d write two lists side by side, one for what it asks of you and one for what you’ve got. Then ignore the first list for a minute and ask what would make the second one a line longer.

    You could say “It’s less the task than how little I’ve got left today.” “What would make this feel more doable?”

    Dig deeper: demands and resources

  3. The ladder: three everyday states.

    The ladder is a picture of the states your body moves between in a day. Near the bottom you’re calm and connected, breathing easily, able to hear something difficult without bristling. Higher up you’re revved up, tense, and small things start to feel urgent or personal. Then there’s running on empty, which is low and slow, with the words just out of reach.

    Most people go up and down it many times a day. Derek minds his granddaughter on Wednesdays, and at two o’clock he’s calm and connected and they’re doing a jigsaw. By four she has found the felt-tips and the wall, and he’s revved up. By six, when her mom comes, he is sitting on the kitchen floor eating a fish stick off her plate and can’t remember the word for colander. Neither of them had changed, only the hour, and how much afternoon was left in him.

    The Nervous System Ladder is a thirty-second check that gives you a best guess at where you are. If you’d rather not use a screen, set three reminders on your phone for today, and each time one goes off, notice which called you’re on. Don’t do anything about it, just notice.

  4. When you’re flooded, thinking goes offline.

    At the top of the stress curve your body is in full alarm, and the things a hard conversation needs most (careful thinking, listening, seeing the other side) get much harder to reach. Lots of people call this being flooded. You might say something you don’t mean, or freeze, or want nothing in the world except to be out of the room.

    Two brothers start clearing their dad’s house one wet Saturday in March. Within twenty minutes they are shouting at each other about a standard lamp. Neither of them likes the lamp. Neither of them wants it. One of them says “Fine, take it then!” and the other says “I don’t want it!” and they stand there either side of it, breathing hard, while the actual subject sits in the boxes all around them and nobody says it.

    A flooded person is still a decent person whose body has to come down before the talk can go anywhere. Know your own flood signs in advance. Heat in the face is a common one. So is a racing heart, or the room going narrow, or a voice in your head that has gone very loud and very certain. Two at once is your cue to stop. Then say so in plain words, something like “I’m too flooded to listen well. Can we stop for twenty minutes and come back at four?”

    Dig deeper: flooding

  5. Stress adds up, like a tank filling.

    Picture a tank holding everything you’re already carrying today. The traffic goes in. So does the meeting that overran, and the noisy shop, and the text from your mom that just says “Call me when you can.” It’s all one tank, and by the evening something tiny makes it overflow and gets the blame for the whole lot.

    Hassles beat headlines In a classic study, everyday hassles predicted how people were doing better than major life events did, even once the big events were taken into account. Which is why a missing shoe can be the thing that tips you over.

    Kanner and colleagues (1981) · Moderate: a classic study; many later studies agree

    Asked to empty the dishwasher, Kyle, who is fifteen, explodes. Really explodes, a whole speech about how he does everything in this house. It’s days later, from a friend’s mom at school pickup, that his own mom finds out his closest friendship fell apart at lunchtime that Monday, over something said in a group chat. The dishwasher was just the next thing anyone asked of him.

    Small daily hassles count as much as big events here, which surprises people. Before you react to a small thing in the evening, ask how full you were before it happened. How much are you carrying? is five quick questions, and Book 4, Chapter 3 (page 51) uses it to tell old stress from new.

  6. Settle the body first.

    You can’t think your way out of a body in alarm. You can help the body come down, though, and the thinking usually follows. Slow breathing with long out-breaths works for a lot of people. So does moving, or humming something you know by heart, or steady pressure like a heavy blanket, or simply leaving the noisy room. What works is personal, which is why it’s worth finding your own short list on a calm day and not in the middle of a fight.

    Before she has to give some difficult feedback, Bridie feels her heart going. She walks once around the staff parking lot, breathing out slowly past the dumpsters and the smokers’ shelter, and goes in a little steadier, if not exactly calm. Put your list on your phone. The Calm-Down Kit helps you build it. If you only keep one line from this section, keep “body first, words second.”

  7. Rest isn’t one thing.

    An hour on the couch scrolling can leave you more tired than when you sat down. That’s because recovery comes in different kinds, and sleep is only one of them, alongside switching off from work in your head, real relaxation, doing something absorbing that you enjoy, and the plain feeling of being in charge of your own time for once. None of them stands in for the others.

    Four kinds of recovery Researchers who study unwinding after work separate four things: switching off in your head, relaxing, doing something absorbing that stretches you a little, and being in charge of your own time. An evening that gives you none of the four isn’t much of a rest, however long you sit down.

    Sonnentag and Fritz (2007) · Strong: a widely used measure; recovery research is large and consistent

    Two roommates have friends over on a Saturday for a takeout and a game show on TV. One wakes up on Sunday buzzing. The other spends the whole of Sunday in her room with a library book and the door shut, and comes out at dinnertime feeling human again. Company fills some people up and drains others, and neither of them is doing rest wrong.

    Ask yourself which kind of tired you are, whether it’s your body or your brain, or people, or feelings, then pick a rest that matches rather than the one you always go for. Calm sounds and music is one kind, if you like that sort of thing.

  8. Sleep and stress feed each other.

    Stress makes sleep harder, and poor sleep makes stress land harder. After a short or broken night small things feel bigger, tempers are shorter, and people pull away from each other. This one is well studied. In a brain-scan study by Yoo and colleagues (2007), one night without sleep made the brain’s alarm center react more strongly to upsetting pictures.

    In a daily study by Gordon and Chen (2014), couples reported more conflict after nights of poor sleep. In experiments by Ben Simon and Walker (2018), people who had missed a night’s sleep kept others at a greater distance and felt lonelier.

    Saoirse comes off her third night shift at the nursing home and bursts into tears in the parking lot over a parking ticket. Her sister, on speakerphone, says, “You’ve had no sleep, leave it, we’ll deal with it Thursday.” By Thursday it is a parking ticket again, annoying and payable, and she pays it online on her lunch break between a granola bar and a yogurt.

    You can’t always fix sleep, but you can notice when it’s part of the story. Before a hard conversation, ask whether you’ve both slept, and if not, pick another time. At night, do the same few calm things before bed in the same order, so your body learns the signal.

    You could say “I slept badly, so if I’m short with you today, that’s why.” “Can we talk about this after we’ve both slept?”

    Dig deeper: sleep and stress

  9. Stress is easier to carry when it’s shared.

    People calm each other down. A steady voice, a hand on the shoulder, or just a quiet person in the same room can help a nervous system settle. Saying your state out loud helps too, because “I’m stressed about work” stops your partner wondering if it’s about them.

    Fish sticks again, Tuesday. Kwasi says to his ten-year-old, without quite looking at him, “I’m a bit stressed about money this month. Sorting it is my grown-up job, and you’re not in trouble.” His son nods and carries on eating. Nothing else is said. Over the next few days the boy stops tiptoeing around him, and on Friday he brings his math homework to the table again and asks for help with it, which he hasn’t done since school break.

Two ways to read this

Snapping when hungry or tired

From outside A small question at the end of a long day gets a sharp answer.

The logic Patience runs on fuel. Hunger and tiredness drain the tank. With an empty tank, small things feel big. The snap is the fuel gauge talking.

It’s like A phone on 3% that starts closing apps.

Why it fits Poor sleep is linked with more conflict the next day, and hunger can color our judgments without our noticing.

The other side The person who asked did nothing wrong, and the tone still lands on them.

You could say “I’m running on empty. Can we talk after I’ve eaten?”

One small step Eat first. Then come back and say sorry for the tone.

Both directions For some people, stress shows up loudly: a sharp voice, a slammed cupboard. For others it goes inward and quiet, and the first sign anyone sees is a person who has stopped answering. If you get loud, the quiet one may be just as full, only less visible. If you go quiet, the loud one is probably not angry with you. They are usually full, and it’s coming out sideways.

Where the fields meet Neurobiology’s idea is that your state sets the volume, and stress left over from earlier still counts. Economics says the same thing in money terms: a household or a person running at full stretch has no margin left for an ordinary bad day.

In everyday life

Living on your own means nobody’s there to notice when you’re running on empty, so build rest into the week before you need it. With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day, plus the rule that whoever pauses names a time to come back (One of you wants to talk now, the other needs space, spreadloveandacceptance.com/pursue-withdraw, has more on this). A child can’t settle until the nearest grown-up does. Your calm is something they borrow for years before they can make their own. At work, a flooded colleague can’t take in feedback, and waiting an hour usually beats pushing on.

Some people run on empty for reasons built into their lives, like shift work, a long-term illness or caring for someone. What that needs is a different setup. More effort won’t do it (see Different hours, spreadloveandacceptance.com/different-hours, and Caregivers, spreadloveandacceptance.com/caregivers). If you have ADHD, just keeping track of everything can fill the tank before breakfast. Get the remembering out of your head and into the world, with labeled alarms and one hook for the keys, and plan hard talks for your better time of day. More in the full chapter.

Common mix-ups

People often think all stress is bad, and it isn’t. Short bursts help you rise to something, and the harm comes from stress that never gets a recovery. Being calm doesn’t mean not being upset, either. You can be calm and sad, or calm and extremely firm. Pausing gets mistaken for avoiding, but a pause with a time to come back is how you finish a talk well. Avoiding is the pause that never comes back. Rest gets called lazy, though bodies and minds that never recover tend to break. And short replies with a flat face get read as not caring, when far more often they mean someone is worn out.

If you want to help someone else with this

Nobody in the history of the world has calmed down because they were told to. A lower voice, slower words and a steady presence do far more. Taking one job off someone’s list beats any pep talk. Ask what kind of help they’d like (practical help, company, distraction, some space) instead of guessing. Don’t argue with someone who’s flooded. Offer a break and a time to come back, because the facts will still be there in twenty minutes. And if someone has been running on empty for weeks, say so kindly and help them find support.

You could say “You seem really stretched. Is there one thing I could take off your plate?” “We don’t have to sort this now. Shall we pick it up after dinner?”

Thursday is Mom’s hospital appointment. The one with the specialist, the one you’ve been going on about for three weeks, and it’s the third time this month he’s done this. Your jaw goes and your face goes hot. Your thumbs are already moving, TYPICAL. You never, and the message is one tap from sent.

Plenty of us were taught that anger was rude, or dangerous, or grew up around anger that was frightening. Anger itself is an ordinary human feeling, though, and it very often points at something real. It also sits under two tools elsewhere. The Calm-Down Kit (Book 1, Chapter 2, page 25) works because anger rises in the body first and slowing the body is what makes words possible. And letting a comment land before you react (Book 4, Chapter 4, page 73) is the stepping back described further on.

The canceled appointment, seen quickly. The surface is a sibling who let you down again. Underneath, anger, fast and hot. Underneath that, fear about Mom and tiredness. The roots are signal and noise, and state and setting. The chapter goes back through those layers slowly.

  1. Anger is a signal about something that matters.

    Anger usually says something is unfair, or something’s in your way, or a line has been crossed. It pushes you toward a problem so you’ll change it. It can also land on the wrong person, or come out far bigger than the thing in front of it.

    Anger moves you toward things Most unpleasant feelings make us back away. A review by Carver and Harmon-Jones argued that anger usually does the opposite: like eagerness, it pushes you toward the goal or the obstacle. That’s what makes it good at showing what matters, and risky to act on at full heat.

    Carver and Harmon-Jones (2009) · Moderate: an influential review; the approach view is still debated

    Somebody keeps eating Shabnam’s lunch from the office fridge. Week one, she assumes a mistake. Week two, she writes her name on the box in marker pen. Week three, the box is empty again and has been put back in the fridge, neatly, with the lid on, which is the bit that does it. Her anger has now told her very clearly that a line has been crossed, and it’s the anger, frankly, that gets her to raise it at the Monday meeting. (It was the new starter. He thought the fridge was communal. He bought her a meal deal and looked like he wanted to die.)

    Next time you’re angry, finish this privately. “I’m angry because something I care about, which is ___, seems ___.”

    Dig deeper: what anger is for

  2. Anger often guards a softer feeling.

    Anger can arrive first because it feels stronger and safer than whatever is under it, which is often hurt, or fear, or shame, or plain exhaustion. Book 1, Chapter 4 (page 61) calls this layers, and the layer underneath usually tells you what you actually need.

    Frankie was an hour late home. She is sixteen. Her dad was waiting in the hall when the key went in the door and what came out of him was mostly about curfews and rules and respect, at volume. She gave as good as she got. It was only later, sitting on the stairs, that he told her about the hour itself. Standing at the front window. Phone in his hand. Checking the bus app over and over. Working out which ER. She went quiet then. “Sorry,” she said, and this time she meant it.

    You could say “I’m angry, and I think underneath I was really scared.” “I came across as angry, but I think I’m actually hurt.”

    Dig deeper: what anger can cover

  3. Anger rises in the body fast.

    A hot face, a tight jaw, a pounding heart and a voice going up are the body getting ready for a fight, and it takes seconds. When anger is high, careful thinking gets a lot harder, and listening harder still. Chapter 1 (page ) calls this being flooded.

    Halfway through an argument about the trash (whose turn, and also, it emerged, whose turn for the last four years) Meera noticed that her hands were shaking. She also realized she hadn’t heard a single thing her roommate had said for at least a minute. She’d been composing, and that was her sign. Learn your own early signs, if you can, whether it’s a tight jaw or talking faster or being very, very reasonable in a slightly high voice.

    Dig deeper: anger in the body

  4. In the hot minutes: slow the body first.

    When anger is high, settle your body before you try to sort anything out. Stop talking. Stop typing. Breathe out slowly, so the out-breath is longer than the in-breath. Unclench your hands. If you can, get out of the room for a bit. A large review of 154 studies by Kjærvik and Bushman (2024) found that activities which calm the body, like slow breathing, relaxation and mindfulness, reduced anger, while ones that rev you up showed little or no benefit.

    Fractions, at the kitchen table, 7:30, question four. Ben could feel his voice getting tighter with every “But why is it a half?” “I’m getting frustrated,” he said in the end, “and that’s not your fault. I’m getting a glass of water. Five minutes.” He stood at the sink, looking out at the dark backyard, and drank the whole glass. When he came back they did the rest of the sheet together, and both of them got number six wrong, confidently.

    Make the plan on a calm day. Your early sign, the words you’ll say, where you’ll go for ten minutes. Upset right now? has steps for the very worst moments.

  5. Letting rip doesn’t empty it.

    A lot of people believe anger works like steam. Shout, slam a door, hit a pillow, and it’s out, and you’re calmer. It’s a lovely tidy idea. The research doesn’t support it. In a well-known study by Bushman (2002), angry people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them ended up angrier than before.

    Going over what happened again and again in your head has a similar effect. In studies by Bushman and colleagues (2005), people who dwelt on a provocation were later more aggressive, even toward someone who only mildly annoyed them. Anger fades faster when the body is calmed and the mind has something else to do for a while.

    After a fight with a colleague, Neville spent his lunch break telling three separate people how awful she was. Each telling got a bit better. By two o’clock he was angrier than he’d been at noon, and had a headache. The next time it happened he walked to the river and back instead and didn’t tell anyone anything, which nearly killed him. By two he could think about the actual problem. He still thinks she was out of order, and says so, but at a normal volume and to fewer people.

    Dig deeper: venting and going over it

  6. Step back and look from the outside.

    Once your body has come down a bit, try looking at the moment as if you were watching from the other side of the room. What actually happened? What might the other person have been dealing with? In experiments by Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman (2012), people who looked back on an annoying moment “like a fly on the wall,” instead of reliving it, felt less angry and acted less aggressively. Rethinking what happened, which psychologists call reappraisal (Gross, 1998), helps too.

    One way in is to write two lines about it in the third person, with your own name. “Wes got angry because…” It feels a bit mad. It works annoyingly well. Wes himself, canceled on by a friend for the third time, got as far as “Wes got angry because Matt canceled again, and Matt is…” and stopped, because Matt is looking after his dad, who has been in and out of the hospital since June. The anger didn’t go. It turned, slowly, into worry, which was at least pointing the right way.

    Dig deeper: stepping back

  7. Say it, don’t throw it.

    Anger is often a fair message sent in a hurtful way. Once you’re calmer you can still say you were angry, and why, without going for anyone, by keeping to what happened and how you felt and what you’re asking for.

    Two versions of the same fight When people wrote about a time they angered someone and a time someone angered them, the two kinds of story differed in how serious the harm seemed, how understandable it was and whether it was over. Same sort of event, told from two different places.

    Baumeister, Stillwell and Wotman (1990) · Moderate: a classic study of people’s own written memories

    “You always let me down” was what Carol wanted to say to her sister. What she actually sent, after two drafts and a cup of tea, was this. “When you canceled Mom’s appointment again I felt really angry, and on my own with it. Can we sort out who takes her for the next three months?” It’s longer, and it is also the only version that gets anyone to the hospital. Book 3, Chapter 2 (page 23) has more on saying hard things.

    You could say “I was angry about what happened on Monday. Can I tell you why?” “When ___ happened, I felt ___, and what I’m asking for is ___.”

  8. After anger, repair.

    Everyone gets angry, and most of us say something sharp now and then. What happens next matters a lot, because a short, real repair rebuilds trust. Name what you did, with no “but” on the end, and say what you’ll do differently.

    The blue vase from the hall table is on the floor in about nine pieces and Grandma Jean has just shouted, really shouted, and Jayden is crying. An hour later she finds him on the stairs. She sits down next to him, which takes her a while with her knees. “I shouted, and that was scary,” she says. “You matter to me far more than any vase. I’m sorry.” He leans on her shoulder for a bit, and then he asks if they can glue it, so they try. It now has a very visible crack and lives on the landing, where she says it reminds her of something. Chapter 5 (page ) has more on apologies that land.

    You could say “I was sharp with you earlier. That wasn’t fair, and I’m sorry.” “I’m still upset about what happened, and I also shouldn’t have spoken to you that way.”

    Dig deeper: repair after anger

  9. When anger becomes harm.

    Feeling angry is human. But some things done in anger are harm, whatever the feeling behind them: hitting, pushing, throwing things at or near someone, breaking their things, threatening, or shouting so someone is scared. Anger is never a reason someone deserves to be hurt.

    Being angry, even loudly, is different from intimidation. Anger becomes intimidation when these are present together: it happens again and again, it arrives when the other person disagrees or says no, it decides what happens next, and people start changing what they say and do to avoid it. A raised voice in one hard conversation that gets repaired isn’t that. Neither is being loud by nature, saying a strong feeling plainly, or crying and shouting during a meltdown. Ask yourself whether your anger passes and gets repaired, and whether the other person can still say no to you.

    For example, a man realizes his partner and children go quiet when he comes home in a bad mood. He doesn’t hit anyone, but they have started keeping out of his way, and he decides to do something about it.

    You could say “I think my anger is scaring people I love. I want help with it.”

    Dig deeper: when anger becomes harm

Your part, and only your part. You can learn your early signs, settle your body, say what made you angry in words, and repair when you get it wrong. You can’t make someone else calm down.

In everyday life

Anger at yourself counts. “I’m so stupid” is anger turned inward, and it helps to talk to yourself the way you would to a friend who’d done the same thing (Book 1, Chapter 5, page 79, goes further). With a partner, agree a pause word on a calm day, and a time you’ll always come back. Children copy what we do with anger far more than anything we say about it. At work anger is common and rarely spoken, and “I’m frustrated about the deadline change, can we talk about what’s realistic?” keeps it useful.

If you have ADHD, anger can come fast and big and go just as fast, so plan for the speed. A three-word pause line (“Back in ten”) and a rule to wait before you send anything. If you’re managing a clash between two other people, hear each of them on their own first and then together, always about what happened and never about each other. Quite often the fight is about the setup, two people who both believe they own the same job. The full chapter has a worked example.

Common mix-ups

Anger itself isn’t a bad feeling. It’s normal and it carries information, and it’s some of the things done in anger that cause harm. Letting it out isn’t the healthy option it sounds like, because shouting and hitting things tend to keep anger going. “They made me angry, so it’s their fault I shouted” mixes up two separate things. Someone can do something unfair, and what you do next is still yours. And holding it in isn’t as safe as it looks. Anger swallowed for years comes out sideways, as coldness or sarcasm or a sudden explosion over a teaspoon, so saying it calmly and early is the safer road.

If you want to help someone else with this

You don’t have to stay and calm someone down. If you do stay, speak lower and slower and leave the argument until they’ve come down. Then listen for the message underneath. There’s often something fair in it, and you can take that part seriously while still saying the way it came out wasn’t okay. Something like “I want to hear this, and I can’t while we’re both shouting. Can we try again at seven?” keeps hold of both halves.

Sunday, 10:50pm

The house is quiet. You’ve checked the front door twice and the back door once, and you’re thinking about the front door again. Tomorrow is the hospital, ten o’clock, and it goes round and round. What if it’s bad. What if I can’t cope. Who would I even tell.

And if you’re honest, it isn’t only tonight. You haven’t really wanted to see anyone for a few weeks. Even replying to a text has started to feel like lifting something.

Almost everyone worries, feels low or goes through something frightening at some point. For some people it settles by itself, and for others it sticks, and grows, until it shapes every day. This chapter is about how these things work, what tends to help, and when it’s time to ask for more. It can’t diagnose anyone. If any of this is about you right now, you aren’t alone in it, and it can get better.

Some tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. The daily check-in’s “low, medium or high” (Book 3, Chapter 1, page 7) lets people adjust when worry or low mood has shrunk the room you have. “A full tank means later, not never” (Book 4, Chapter 3, page 51) names a time to come back, because avoiding a thing keeps fear going. Quickly, then slowly. On the surface, checking the door twice. Underneath, an alarm that keeps going off with nothing to point at. Underneath that, sleep, people and days that have narrowed. The roots are state and setting, and feedback loops, since avoiding keeps the alarm going. The rest of the chapter takes each part in turn.

  1. Worry and anxiety are the body’s alarm.

    Fear is the body’s alarm for danger that’s here now. Anxiety is the same alarm ringing about something that might happen. Worry is the thinking part, the “What if…?” going round. All three run through the stress system from Chapter 1 (page ), which is why they come with a racing heart and a tight chest and a churning stomach and nights of bad sleep. The alarm is trying to protect you. The trouble is it can be set far too sensitive, and it can’t tell a tiger from an email.

    Fear of not knowing One review argues that fear of the unknown, the not-knowing itself, may sit underneath a lot of anxiety. It’s a proposal rather than a settled finding, but it fits the night before a hospital appointment better than most explanations do.

    Carleton (2016) · Theory: an influential review; the central claim is a proposal

    Before every presentation in class, Leah’s heart hammers so hard her hands shake her notes. She is fifteen, and nothing bad has ever happened to her in a presentation. Her alarm still treats a room full of people she’s known since first grade, one of whom once ate a worm for 50 cents, as somewhere dangerous.

    When it rises, try breathing out slowly five times, each breath out longer than the breath in. Then name three things you can see. It tells the body the danger isn’t here right now, and while it won’t switch the alarm off, it can usually turn it down a notch.

    Dig deeper: how anxiety works

  2. Avoiding keeps fear going.

    When something makes us anxious, stepping away brings quick relief, and the relief teaches the brain that escaping was clever. So next time the fear is a little bigger and the world a little smaller. What helps is going back toward the feared thing in small, chosen steps, and staying long enough to find out you can cope. Psychologists call this exposure. Craske and colleagues (2014) describe how it works best when each step tests what you expect to happen.

    After a panic attack on the 42, Graham started walking everywhere, in the rain, three miles to work and three miles back. His sister didn’t argue. One Saturday she just stood at the bus stop with him and they got on, and off again, after one stop. A week later, two stops. It took most of the spring before he did the whole run into town, sitting by the doors, feeling sick, reading the same ad for a lawyer over and over. But he did it, and his brain had slowly learned that buses are survivable.

    Give worry a time and a place.

    Trying hard not to worry rarely works. What helps a lot of people is postponing it. You notice a worry, write it down, and save it for a set “worry time” later in the day. In an early study by Borkovec and colleagues (1983), people who practiced this for four weeks reported worrying less each day. In worry time, go through the list and ask of each one whether there’s anything you can do about it. If there is, plan one step. If there isn’t, practice letting it go, which is easier said than done, I know.

    Desmond looks after his dad and keeps a small red notebook by the kettle. Worries go in it all day. At six, with a cup of tea, he gives the notebook fifteen minutes. By then half the morning’s worries look smaller, and a couple have quietly worked themselves out. The one about the water heater has been in there since August, and he has decided to let it stay. When one turns up at the wrong time of day, the trick is to tell it, more or less, “Noted. I’ll think about that at six.”

  4. Low mood is common. Depression is more than a bad week.

    Everyone has low days, and sadness after a loss or a let-down is normal. Depression is different. It means low mood, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy, for most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, often with poor sleep, tiredness, appetite changes, feeling worthless or guilty, and finding it hard to think. It can look like sadness. It can also look like irritability, or just running on empty. It is a health problem, and nobody gets it by being weak.

    Lionel taught geography for thirty-one years. Lately he has stopped going to the Thursday walking group. He isn’t reading. He wakes at four every morning and lies there listening to the water heater come on. Getting old, he tells himself. It was his daughter, up for the weekend, who said it, carefully, while they were doing the dishes. “Dad, I think this might be depression. And that can be treated.” He was pretty annoyed with her for about a day.

    You could say “I’ve not been myself for a few weeks, and I don’t think it’s just tiredness.” “I don’t enjoy things I used to. Can I talk to you about it?”

    Dig deeper: low mood and depression

  5. Doing often comes before feeling better.

    Low mood tells you to wait until you feel like it. But withdrawing makes life emptier, and that deepens the low. A well-tested approach called behavioral activation turns this around. You plan small, doable things that bring a bit of pleasure or a sense of having done something, and you do them whether you feel like it or not. A review of 26 trials by Ekers and colleagues (2014) found it helped with depression, though many of the studies were small.

    After her divorce, living on her own, Lorraine planned three small things a day, a ten-minute walk to the postbox and back, one call or message, and one job she could finish, like wiping the stove. Most days, honestly, they felt pointless. She did them anyway, and over a few weeks the days started to feel less dull, though she couldn’t tell you which day it changed.

    Dig deeper: behavioral activation

  6. Sleep, moving and people do a lot of the work.

    These sound too simple to matter. The evidence is solid all the same. Improving sleep can lift mood: in a large trial with university students, Freeman and colleagues (2017) found that an online sleep course reduced insomnia, and with it some other mental health difficulties.

    Exercise helps depression too. A review of 218 trials by Noetel and colleagues (2024) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all helped. Belonging to groups protects as well. Cruwys and colleagues (2013) found that people with depression who joined groups were less likely to relapse.

    People are not an optional extra A meta-analysis of 148 studies, with over 300,000 people, found that people with stronger social ties had about 50% better odds of survival over the years they were followed. The studies observe rather than prove cause, but it’s hard to read that and call calling a friend a luxury.

    Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010) · Strong: a large meta-analysis of observational studies; cause not established

    A new dad, exhausted and low, sat down with his partner and a calendar and between them they blocked out one protected long sleep each on the weekend, a stroller walk every morning around the park whatever the weather, and his old Wednesday five-a-side, which he’d dropped when the baby came and missed more than he’d admitted. He was terrible at five-a-side the first week, and went back anyway. Calm sounds help some people wind down at night, too, and Cool-down corner has some.

    Words you could use “I need help protecting my sleep for a while.” “Would you come for a short walk with me? I find it hard to go on my own.”

  7. Trauma is about what happened, and what it left behind.

    Trauma usually means going through, or seeing, something that threatened life or safety, such as violence, abuse, a serious accident, war, or the sudden death of someone close. It is very common. In surveys across 24 countries, Kessler and colleagues (2017) found about 7 in 10 adults had been through at least one.

    Most people recover over time. A smaller number develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with memories that come back vividly, avoiding reminders, feeling on high alert, and sleep problems. Ordinary hard times and stress hurt too, even when they aren’t trauma in this sense.

    Months after a crash at a wet intersection, Shirin still jumps at the sound of brakes and won’t drive after dark. Her partner has started saying “It’s fine, it’s fine” before she’s even flinched, which she finds both sweet and maddening. Her alarm learned that cars can mean danger and hasn’t unlearned it yet. That is a long way from overreacting. Trauma-focused therapies help many people.

    Dig deeper: what trauma is and isn’t

  8. After something frightening: safety, choice and no pressure.

    After a frightening event, people need safety, practical help, warmth and choice. They don’t need to be made to talk. A review of single-session “debriefing” after trauma by Rose and colleagues (2002) found it did not prevent PTSD, and might make things worse for some. Services that are “trauma-informed” (SAMHSA, 2014) put safety, trust, choice and working together first, and the same ideas help in families, friendships and workplaces.

    Someone had been in Grandma’s house while she was at the store. They took her purse from the drawer and her late husband’s watch. She didn’t want to talk about it, and said so, twice. Her grandson didn’t push. He fixed the lock on the back door, which took him most of the afternoon and two trips to the hardware shop, and slept on her sofa. “If you ever want to talk about it, I’m here,” he said in the morning, and went to work. A week later she called him at nine at night and talked for an hour, mostly about the watch.

    Dig deeper: trauma-informed care

  9. Therapy helps many people, and medicines can too.

    Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) teaches practical ways to notice and change patterns of thinking and doing. It’s one of the most studied talk therapies, and a review of over a hundred meta-analyses by Hofmann and colleagues (2012) found the strongest support for anxiety problems and stress, and support for many other problems too.

    Guidelines such as NICE (2022) also recommend guided self-help, behavioral activation, exercise groups and, for some people, antidepressants. Medicines work differently for different people, and stopping one suddenly can cause problems of its own.

    Zack was too embarrassed to ask anyone about the panic attacks. Then one stopped him getting on the 7:40 for the second week running, and he stood on the platform watching it go. His doctor referred him for a short course of CBT, eight sessions. He takes the train to work again now, with a book and a bottle of water for the bad days, and there are still some bad days. Before an appointment, it helps to write three lines, on what you’ve noticed, how long it has gone on, and what it’s stopping you doing.

    Words to try “I’d like to talk about my mood. It’s been going on for a while.” “What are my options, and what do you recommend for someone like me?”

    Dig deeper: what treatment involves

Your part, and only your part. You can learn how your alarm works, keep the basics going, take small steps back toward life, and ask for help when it’s more than you can carry. You can’t think your way out of depression by trying harder, and you can’t fix someone else’s. Being kind to yourself, and getting the right help, are part of the work.

Common mix-ups

Anxiety doesn’t mean something is wrong with you. It’s a normal alarm. It only becomes a problem when it rings too loud or too often, or starts shrinking your life. Depression isn’t just sadness, and nobody snaps out of it. It affects sleep, energy, thinking and the body as well as mood, and many people recover with the right help. Not everything bad that happens is trauma in the clinical sense, which usually means a threat to life or safety. Other hard things still hurt and still deserve care, and the word doesn’t need to fit for help to be fair. Talking about trauma right away doesn’t help everyone either. Some people want to and some don’t, forcing it can make things worse, and safety and choice come first.

It might be a person, or a pet, or a marriage or a friendship, their health or a home or a job, a country they had to leave or a future they’d pictured.   1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone, and it isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted off, children leaving home. Its size usually matches the size of the bond, whatever anyone else thinks the loss ought to mean.

    When Bramble died, his owner was shaken by how hard he took it, and his friends at the bar told him, kindly enough, that it was only a dog. That dog walked beside him along the same stretch of river every morning for twelve years, through two jobs and a divorce. The lead is still on its hook by the back door, and he can’t move it yet.

    It can help to say a loss out loud to yourself, especially one you’ve never called grief. “I’m grieving the friendship.” “I’m grieving the life we planned.” They’re small sentences, and they can bring a surprising amount of relief.

    Dig deeper: what grief is, and what counts

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages of grief, running from denial through anger, bargaining and depression to acceptance. They came from listening to people who were dying, and they were never meant as steps that everyone goes through in order. Most people swing back and forth, some hours deep in the loss and some hours getting on with life, and researchers who study grief see that swing as healthy.

    Back and forth is normal The dual process model describes healthy grief as moving back and forth between facing the loss and getting on with life, with breaks from grief included. In this model Val’s laugh at lunch isn’t a lapse. It’s part of the grieving.

    Stroebe and Schut (1999) · Moderate: a widely used framework with partial support

    Three months after Gerald died, Val laughed properly at lunch. Her sister had told a story about a man at the bowls club and a wasp, and Val laughed till she had to put her fork down. On the bus home she felt terrible about it. That evening she cried for two hours. The lunch was grief too, and so was the bus.

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, bad sleep, no appetite or too much, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there has gone, and the learning takes time and uses a lot of energy.

    A assistant principal whose mother has just died loses her car keys three times in one week. She drives to school on a Saturday, and walks into a school board meeting that is happening on Thursday when it is in fact Tuesday. She starts to wonder, quietly, if something is wrong with her memory. It’s grief fog. It lifts as the months go on, though nobody can tell her when, and she’d really like somebody to.

    While it lasts, look after a few basics, like one real meal a day and a regular bedtime and a short walk outside, and it’s fine to tell people, “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain to anyone else. Someone may still be alive but changed by dementia or illness, or gone without having died, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Other losses go unrecognized, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pregnancy loss or a friend you only ever knew online.

    A loss without an ending Pauline Boss named this ambiguous loss: someone gone but still present in the mind, or present but changed, as with dementia. Part of what makes it so hard is that there’s no clear ending to grieve.

    Boss (1999) · Moderate: a widely used framework, backed by clinical and interview research

    Every Sunday at two, Teresa visits her dad at the nursing home. He’s always pleased to see her. He thinks she’s his sister, Connie, and asks after their mother, who died in 1987. Teresa has stopped correcting him, and they talk about Connie’s life, which Teresa now knows a surprising amount about. She is grieving someone who hasn’t died. There’s no funeral for that, no cards, and at work nobody thinks to ask.

    If a loss of yours had no ceremony, you can make a small one, a candle or a letter or a bulb in the yard, or just going to stand for a while somewhere that mattered.

    Dig deeper: ambiguous and unrecognized grief

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying. Others grieve by doing, fixing things, organizing, working, building. Neither is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks another isn’t grieving, when they’re grieving differently, and culture and faith and age and how your mind works all shape how it comes out.

    After their son died, Janet wanted to talk about him every evening. Steve went into the garage every weekend and worked on the old Raleigh that Ryan had never got around to fixing. She thought he was hiding. He thought she was making it worse. It took a bereavement counselor about forty minutes to point out, gently, that they were both doing exactly the same thing. In March Steve brought the bike out, finished. Janet cried and he let her. Then she asked him what was wrong with the gears, and he told her, for some time.

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said healthy grief meant moving on and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently. People often keep a bond with the person they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head and the values they carry on, so the relationship changes shape without having to end.

    Continuing bonds This is the book that challenged the cut-the-ties view of mourning, showing that inner bonds with someone who has died are common across many groups and often healthy. Later research adds a fair caveat: for some people the bond comforts, and for others it hurts.

    Klass, Silverman and Nickman (1996) · Moderate: influential; effects differ from person to person

    Ama wears her grandpa’s watch to every exam. It’s far too big. She has to push it up her arm to write. She doesn’t believe in luck, and if you asked her why she does it she would probably shrug and say “dunno,” but the honest answer is that it feels as if he’s in the room.

    You could pick one way of keeping a bond, cooking their recipe, or telling one of their stories at Christmas, or keeping one of their habits going. Some people find it helps, in a hard moment, to ask what the person would have said. Often it’s something rude.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retiring, moving country, a child starting school, coming out, getting well after a long illness. All of them can bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts, an ending, a confusing middle bit, and a new beginning. The middle is the hardest part and also the easiest to rush.

    Ifeoma and Olu moved to Denver for a job they both wanted. They toasted it with fizz in the empty apartment. Six months in, Olu felt low, and then guilty for feeling low, since this was the plan. What nobody had toasted was what they’d left behind, a circle of friends, a café where nobody had to ask what they wanted, and a whole Saturday that used to run itself. You can be glad about a change and still miss what came before.

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that over time many people find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, though they never forget. Being around people who let you talk, or let you stay quiet, helps. For some people, though, grief stays at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard.

    Doctors call this prolonged grief. It means a strong longing for the person, or being taken over by the loss, most days, still going many months after the death (6 to 12 months or more, depending on the guideline) and getting in the way of life. It has nothing to do with weakness, and there is specific help for it.

    A year after his wife died, Dennis still couldn’t go into their bedroom. He slept on the settee and had stopped answering the phone to his friends. His doctor referred him to a grief counselor and he went, mainly so his daughter would stop asking. Things moved, slowly, starting with one evening a week at his brother’s, watching the snooker and not saying much. He’s back in the bedroom now, on his own side of the bed.

Grief, read two ways

Both directions Some people grieve by talking, crying and remembering out loud. Others grieve by doing, mending, fixing the bike. If you talk, the doer may look as if they’ve moved on. Usually they haven’t, and the doing is the grief. If you do, the talker isn’t wallowing. Telling the story again is how they carry it.

Where the fields meet Philosophy has asked for a long time what makes a life go well, and part of its answer is that the people we lose stay part of it. Laughter, oddly, belongs here too: a shared laugh can tell a grieving body it’s safe for a minute. The research on laughter is still young, so read that as a small comfort.

In everyday life

It was one sentence, three weeks ago, said over your shoulder in somebody’s kitchen at a party on the way to the fridge, and they have almost certainly forgotten it.

You haven’t. It plays again, word for word, tone and all, followed by every clever thing you could have said back and didn’t. You’re very tired of carrying it about, and you honestly don’t know where you’d put it down if you could.

Everyone gets hurt, and everyone hurts other people, mostly without meaning to. This chapter looks at how hurt works and how relationships mend, and at how you can heal with or without the other person, for small hurts and bigger ones.

Seen fast, a sentence from three weeks ago is three things. On top, the words, which you can still quote. Under them, what they seemed to say about you. Under that, the question of whether it will happen again. The roots are balance and signal and noise. The chapter slows down on repair.

  1. Hurt is real, and healing takes time.

    Being hurt by someone shows that something mattered, whether it was the person, or your trust in them, or your sense of being respected. The mind replays hurts because it’s trying to understand them and keep you safe from the next one. Healing rarely goes in a straight line. The hurt can come back on an anniversary, or out of nowhere, which doesn’t mean you’ve failed.

    What a hurt is about When Leary and colleagues asked people about times their feelings were hurt, the common thread was feeling less valued by someone, usually someone they knew well. A stranger’s remark stings for an afternoon. A friend’s can stay for months.

    Leary and colleagues (1998) · Moderate: based on people’s recollections; the idea is widely supported

    Months after a friend said something cruel to him at a party, Kwesi still feels a jolt whenever the song that was playing comes on the radio. It happened again on Sunday, chopping onions. He said, out loud, to nobody, “Oh, that,” turned the radio down, and carried on with the onions.

    Thoughts you could try “This hurts because it mattered.” “A feeling coming back doesn’t mean I’m back at the start.”

    Dig deeper: why hurt lingers

  2. Every close relationship has breaks. Repair is what counts.

    No relationship is smooth all the time, because people snap, forget, misread each other and let each other down. Research on parents and babies, on couples and on therapy all points the same way. Breaks are normal, and what matters most is whether they get mended. A small repair, made soon, often leaves a relationship feeling safer than before the break.

    Even therapy has ruptures Across 11 studies, repairing breaks in the relationship between therapist and client went with better results. Training therapists in repair didn’t show a clear effect on outcomes in the six studies that tested it, which is a useful bit of honesty.

    Eubanks, Muran and Safran (2018) · Strong: a meta-analysis, though the main finding is correlational

    Orange juice, a full glass, right across the breakfast table and into the toast rack. Toby’s dad shouted, not a huge shout, but enough. Twenty minutes later he sat down next to him on the couch. “I was grumpy and I shouted. That wasn’t fair, spills happen. Want to help me make a new one?” They went and did it together. Toby poured, very slowly, with his tongue out, and slopped some, and they both pretended he hadn’t.

  3. Telling someone they hurt you.

    People can’t mend what they don’t know about. Saying “that hurt” is a risk, but it gives the relationship a chance. It works best when you describe what happened and how it felt, and leave out what kind of person they are. Pick a calm moment, well away from the middle of a fight.

    Amara had a stronger word ready, one she’d been working on for days. On a walk with her friend, not over the phone, she said this instead. “When you told the group about my breakup before I was ready, I felt exposed. I’d like to be the one who shares my news.” Her friend, who had honestly thought she was helping, went bright red. There was a long, awful pause next to a bench with a brass plaque on it for somebody called Maud. Then the friend said, “God. Yes. Sorry,” and they walked on. It was a bit stiff for the rest of the walk and fine by the following week.

    Before you say it, try writing it down in three short lines, one for what happened, one for how you felt and one for what you’d like.

  4. A real apology names it, owns it, and mends it.

    “Sorry if you were upset” doesn’t heal much. A real apology says what you did, takes responsibility without excuses, shows you understand what it did to the other person, and offers to put it right or do things differently. Research, such as a study led by Roy Lewicki (2016), suggests owning it and offering repair are the parts that matter most.

    At the Tuesday meeting, Leila presented an idea she’d worked out with a colleague as if it were all hers. She saw his face. She spent the evening telling herself it didn’t matter, then the next morning went and found him at the coffee machine. “I presented our idea as mine. That wasn’t fair to you. I’ll email the team today and say it was joint work.” He said, “Okay,” and then, after a moment, “Thanks.” She sent the email at 9.40 and copied him in.

  5. Forgiveness isn’t forgetting, excusing or going back.

    Forgiveness is usually described as letting go of resentment and the wish to get even, and it happens inside you. Forgiving someone doesn’t mean saying what happened was okay. It doesn’t mean forgetting, or trusting them again, or keeping them in your life. It’s a choice, often a slow one, and you never owe it on anyone else’s schedule.

    Danielle forgave her father for missing most of her childhood, though it took her well into her thirties. She keeps his visits to an hour and won’t leave the children with him if he’s been drinking. He knows why. They don’t discuss it. For her the forgiving and the protecting sit side by side without much strain.

    Things to tell yourself “I can let go of wanting to get even, and still keep my limits.” “I don’t have to forgive today. I can just stop feeding the anger today.”

    Dig deeper: what forgiveness is

  6. You can heal even if they never say sorry.

    Waiting for an apology that never comes can keep you stuck for years, because the person may be gone, or unwilling, or unable to understand. Your healing doesn’t have to depend on them. You can make sense of what happened, tell your story to someone safe, and decide what it means for you now.

    Writing helps, a bit Pooling 146 randomized studies, Frattaroli found that writing about something painful had a small but real benefit on average. Small is the honest word, and it varies a good deal from person to person.

    Frattaroli (2006) · Moderate: a real but small effect that varies by person

    After the breakup, Martha wrote a letter she never sent. Four pages in pen, both sides, about what hurt and what she’d hoped for and what she was taking with her, which was mostly the good saucepans. She folded it into the back of a notebook and for a while she read it most nights, then less, and then one day in April she realized she hadn’t looked at it for a month.

    Writing works for a lot of people. Write about the hurt for fifteen minutes on three or four days in a row, the facts and the feelings both. If it ever makes you feel much worse, stop and talk to someone instead.

    Dig deeper: healing on your own

  7. Trust comes back in small, kept promises.

    After trust is broken, one big apology rarely rebuilds it. Trust grows back slowly, in lots of small moments where someone does what they said they’d do. If you broke trust, being patient and consistent matters more than a grand gesture. If you were let down, it’s fair to watch what someone does as well as what they say.

    Declan hid a credit card debt from his partner for most of a year. When it came out it was very bad for a while. After that, on the first of every month, he left his bank statement on the kitchen table without being asked, with no speech, just the statement left by the fruit bowl. About a year in, one ordinary evening, his partner said she could breathe again. He went into the yard so she wouldn’t see his face.

    Dig deeper: how trust is rebuilt

  8. Going back is a choice.

    After a hurt, you can mend the relationship or change its shape. You can also step away. All three can be healthy.

    Ellie’s roommate borrowed money twice and didn’t pay it back. Ellie let the first time go, because it was only ten dollars and she’d been broke herself. The second time it was fifty dollars for a train ticket to Chicago, and she said, “I won’t lend money again. I still want us to get along in the house.” The roommate went quiet for two days and left a slightly pointed note about the recycling. They still do Sunday dinner together most weeks, and nobody mentions Chicago.

    Three questions can guide what comes next. Has the hurt stopped? Has it been owned? Do you want this relationship in your life?

    It also helps to tell one hurt from a pattern of hurt. A single hurt, owned and repaired, is something most relationships survive, and many come out closer. A pattern looks different. The same hurt keeps happening after you’ve named it, apologies arrive without any change, and you notice yourself saying less to avoid the next one. One bad month isn’t a pattern, and neither is a partner who is slow to apologize but changes what they do.

    Dig deeper: limits, distance and safety

  9. Look after the hurt part of you.

    Hurt lives in the body as well as the mind, as poor sleep or a jumpy feeling that won’t settle. Healing goes better with basic care, which means rest and food and moving about, people who are kind to you, and talking to yourself the way you’d talk to a hurting friend. Some people find they grow after something painful and some don’t, and neither is a test you can fail.

    A man at the back of the 38 bus shouted at Idris and called him names, the whole way from Angel to Dalston, and nobody else said a word. For days afterward Idris felt shaky. He told a friend. He went for long walks after work. He kept reminding himself it was about the man, and that he was safe now. Over a couple of weeks the shakiness faded, though he sat downstairs on the 38 until well into the spring.

How old hurts shape new reactions

What happened earlier in life can make some reactions more likely now. In surveys of tens of thousands of adults in 24 countries, most had lived through at least one traumatic event (Kessler and colleagues, 2017 · Strong evidence), and many studies have linked hard childhood experiences with health and relationship problems decades later (Felitti and colleagues, 1998 · Strong evidence). That is an association across large groups, not a forecast for any one person. A mark can be left at any age.

  • In early childhood, a child who learned to watch a parent’s mood may grow into an adult who scans every face in a meeting.
  • In the school years, being shamed in front of a class can make any public correction sting.
  • In the teens, months of being left out can make a group chat that goes quiet feel like a verdict.
  • In early adulthood, a first job or first home that went badly can make the next change feel dangerous.
  • In a partnership, a betrayal can make an innocent late text feel like the start of another one.
  • As a parent, a frightening birth or a child’s illness can leave you braced for the next emergency.
  • In midlife, a layoff, a divorce or caring for a parent can wear down the reserves that used to absorb a bad day.
  • In later life, losses and illness can arrive close together, with less time to recover between them.

Most people who go through something terrible recover without lasting problems (Bonanno, 2004 · Strong evidence). For some, especially after repeated or very severe events, the body’s alarm stays switched on. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is the clinical name for one form of this. It follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, and most people who go through such events don’t develop it (Koenen and colleagues, 2017 · Strong evidence). Trauma responses can look like being on edge, startling easily, avoiding reminders, going numb, or meeting a small thing with the force of the old one. Those responses once helped someone get through. In a conversation they usually make things harder, because the person is answering the past as well as the present.

You might recognize it in yourself when a reaction feels older than the moment, arrives in your body before you can think, follows the same kind of trigger each time, or is hard to explain afterward. You might notice it in someone else when their reaction is far bigger than what just happened. These signs are for understanding. They are not a diagnosis, and you can’t diagnose yourself or anyone else from them.

Having lived through something hard is never the person’s fault. The aim is to take back control, so the old event stops running today’s reaction. That usually starts small: noticing “this feels older than today,” naming it, steadying your body, and then choosing what to do next. Trauma-focused therapies have the strongest evidence for PTSD (NICE, 2018 · Strong evidence).

Two ways to read this

Fine all day, falling apart at home

From outside At work or school they’re fine. At home they melt down or go silent.

The logic Fitting in all day takes effort that doesn’t show. The effort is held in until it is safe to stop. Home feels safe, so the holding lets go. The crash shows that home is trusted.

It’s like Holding your breath underwater and gasping at the surface.

Why it fits Autistic adults describe “camouflaging” followed by exhaustion, and many other people do a version of it too.

The other side The family gets the hardest part of the day.

You could say “I hold it together all day. Home is where I can stop.”

One small step A quiet half hour after getting home, with no questions. Later, a line like “That wasn’t about you” helps.

Both directions Some people show how they feel as they go, so a hard day shows during the day. Others hold it together for hours, at work or school, and let go only once they’re somewhere safe, which is often home. If you show it as you go, the person who falls apart at home isn’t saving their worst for you. Home is where it’s safe to stop holding on. If you’re the one who holds it in, saying “I’ve used it all up today” helps the people at home read the crash for what it is.

Where the fields meet Finance gives repair a plain picture: a real apology tops up a reserve of goodwill that small hurts draw down. Psychology adds the reason repairs are needed even between kind people, that intent isn’t impact.

The plan was a walk before work. The sneakers are by the door. Somehow the phone is in your hand instead, and at 7:40 you are watching a man in Finland restore a rusty axe, next to a coffee that went cold twenty minutes ago. You don’t even like axes.

By the evening a quiet voice is telling you that you never stick to anything. What you actually ran into this morning was a habit with about six years of practice behind it. It won, as it usually does for the first few rounds.

Quickly: a phone in your hand at 7:40 is the surface. Underneath, a habit with years of practice behind it. Underneath that, a setup that quietly makes the old way easier. The roots are feedback loops and quiet incentives. Then the chapter slows down and takes them one by one.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    A lot of what we do each day runs on autopilot. That saves energy, and it’s the whole point of a habit. The catch is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one, so changing asks your brain to do something effortful again and again, for weeks, until the new way becomes the easy one.

    Every night Jodie decides she will not snap at the children in the morning, and she means it. Every morning, somewhere between the lost reading book and the toast going cold and Lily’s hair, she snaps. The morning has worn a groove deeper than her good intentions, which says something about mornings and not much about her as a mother.

    Pick one thing you’d like to change, and write down when it usually happens, where, and what’s going on just before.

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger, which might be a time or a place or a feeling or a person, or simply the thing you’ve just done. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward (relief, comfort, a small buzz) keeps it going. Once you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    Stale popcorn In one field experiment, people with a strong movie-popcorn habit ate stale popcorn about as much as fresh, but only in a movie theater. Somewhere else, they didn’t. The setting drove the habit more than taste or good intentions did, rather like Nigel’s laptop.

    Neal and colleagues (2011) · Moderate: two field experiments, in line with wider habit research

    Nigel noticed, eventually, that he reached for the cookie jar the moment he opened his laptop after dinner, hungry or not, every night, and the laptop was the cue. So now he makes tea before he opens it and makes a pot. By the time the tea’s brewed the urge has mostly wandered off. On Fridays it doesn’t wander anywhere and he has a custard cream, and that seems fair.

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring and easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and they work precisely because they’re easy enough to manage on a bad day. Tie the new step to something you already do (“After I make my coffee, I’ll…”), and only grow it once it’s stuck.

    Two roommates had been promising each other a big Sunday clean of the kitchen since March. It had happened once, so they agreed something smaller. After dinner, each of them wipes one surface, which takes two minutes. By the second week the stove had stopped being a source of tension. By the fourth, someone had descaled the kettle without being asked, which caused a small sensation.

    You could say “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I …, I will …”

  4. Change the setup, not just your willpower.

    Willpower runs low when you’re tired or hungry. Your surroundings stay the same all day. So make the new habit easier, by putting it in sight or getting things ready the night before, and make the old one a little harder, by moving it out of reach. I’d back a small change to the setup over a big push of effort almost every time. At home that can be as simple as one owner for each job.

    Willpower isn’t a fuel tank, probably A popular idea says self-control runs out like fuel. When 23 labs tried to repeat the classic experiment together, they found an effect close to zero.   5. Being in two minds is normal.

    Part of you wants to change and part of you doesn’t, and the reluctant part usually has its reasons. The old habit may give comfort, or protect you from something. Pushing harder against it rarely works, and listening to it often does.

    Asif wanted to drink less, and he also loved Friday nights at the bar with his team. When he finally asked himself what Friday actually gave him, the answer wasn’t really the beer. It was the people, the laughing, the end of the week. So he kept the Friday and switched to alcohol-free after the first pint. If anyone at the table noticed, they didn’t say, which is its own sort of kindness.

    Try two lists, one of what you like about how things are, one of what you’d like about changing. You’re allowed both, and I suspect the first one will be the more honest of the two.

  6. Slips are part of change.

    Nearly everyone who changes something slips back sometimes, and one slip doesn’t undo the progress. What does the damage is usually the thought that follows. “I’ve blown it, so why bother?” Being kind to yourself after a slip makes it a good deal more likely you’ll start again.

    There’s a name for “why bother?” Relapse-prevention research calls it the abstinence violation effect: after one slip, guilt and a sense of total failure make a full slide back more likely. It comes from addiction treatment, but the shape is the same for cookies and evening runs.

    Marlatt and Gordon (1985) · Moderate: a foundational clinical model, widely used in addiction treatment

    Three calm weeks, and then Lewis shouted at his son over spellings. Really shouted, the old way. That night he sat on the end of the bed in the dark. “I shouted, and I’m sorry. I’m working on it.” A small voice from under the duvet said “Okay.” The next evening they did the spellings at the kitchen table with the radio on. Nobody raised their voice. The word was “necessary,” which neither of them could spell.

    Things to tell yourself “A slip is one moment out of many.” “What made today harder, and what can I plan for next time?”

    Decide now, while things are going well, what you’ll do after a slip. Have one kind sentence to say to yourself and one small step to start again. A game can help some people practice, and the Re-check Drive turns small kind steps into calm football.

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

    Change made only to please someone, or out of shame, is hard to keep up. Change that connects to what you care about has deeper roots, and each small action becomes a vote for the person you’re turning into.

    Rania kept meaning to be better at keeping in touch, and kept not being. What shifted it was a different sentence in her head. She stopped telling herself she must message people and started thinking of herself as someone who shows up for her friends. A voice note on Sunday evenings became part of that. Some weeks it’s thirty seconds of nonsense and some weeks it turns into an hour on the phone.

  8. You can support change, but not force it.

    It’s natural to want the people we love to change, to drink less, or look after themselves better. Their change belongs to them, though, and pressure tends to make people dig in. It helps more to say clearly what you need, ask what would help, and notice their small steps out loud.

    Grant kept missing deadlines, and his teammate had the lecture about being organized all ready, she’d been polishing it for two weeks, and at the last minute she didn’t give it. She asked what would make the handover easier, and he looked surprised. Between them they set up a shared checklist, and he fills it in every Friday before lunch, mostly. She has kept the lecture, though, in a note on her phone.

The sneakers are still by the door. The phone sleeps in the kitchen now, and the plan has shrunk to shoes on, out to the end of the road. Some mornings that’s all. Two ways to read this

Change, two ways

Both directions Some people love a fresh start and throw themselves at a new plan. Others need to change one small thing and see it hold first. If you’re a fresh-starter, the slow one isn’t lacking willpower. They’re protecting what already works. If you’re slow and steady, the fresh-starter isn’t being reckless. Novelty is part of what keeps them going.

Where the fields meet Behavioral science says habits beat good intentions, and business adds the simple check: one owner for a change and one small look each week at whether it held. The root is feedback loops, small things repeated with a check each time.

In everyday life

Take a couple who want to spend less of the evening on their phones. Earlier attempts failed because each of them watched the other. This time they put a basket by the door, both phones go in at nine, and there’s one exception for whoever’s on call for an elderly parent. The first week is awkward. On the Wednesday one of them sneaks a look at the football. By the third week the basket is part of the furniture and the evenings have a shape again, a movie or a crossword or an early night.

Big life changes, a move or a new baby, break old routines, which is disruptive and also a good moment to start new habits. If you have ADHD, make the cue impossible to miss, do the step the moment it appears, and make the reward come right away. Expect the novelty to wear off, and swap the method when it does instead of blaming yourself. More in the full chapter.

Give it a cue, written as “After I ___, I will ___.” Make one tweak to your surroundings so it’s easier to start. Decide on the smallest version you could still manage on a tired day. Common mix-ups

“If I really wanted it, I’d just do it” is the cruelest of these, and it’s wrong. Wanting is only the start. Habits, setup, tiredness and stress all get a vote. Then there’s the famous 21 days. There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range. Being hard on yourself doesn’t keep you on track, either. Harshness makes people give up, and kindness makes it easier to try again. And routines don’t have to mean being rigid. For a lot of people, autistic people especially, routines are what make life manageable, and change can respect that.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you advise (“Would ideas help, or would you rather just talk it through?”), and help them find their own reasons by asking what would be different for them if this changed. Practical help with the setup, like a ride to the gym, often does more than encouragement. Notice small steps out loud and be kind about slips. Look after yourself too. You can care a great deal and still have limits on what you’ll put up with.

Sunday, 9pm

The week ahead has the car inspection on Tuesday, a dentist, two late meetings and your turn for the school drop-off, twice, and none of it feels like yours, somehow. You’re not sad, exactly. You just can’t remember the last time you did something because it mattered to you, and not because it was next on the list.

This chapter is about values, the things you care about and the direction you want your life to face, and about what makes a life feel worth living. It borrows from psychology, and from philosophy, which has been asking how we ought to live for more than two thousand years. You don’t need to have read any of it. I’ve tried to keep the old names to a minimum and the words plain.

In quick strokes, a full week looks like a diary problem. Underneath, it is a values problem, two good things wanting the same evening. Underneath that, what quietly gets rewarded at home. The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives. The chapter then slows down on each.

  1. Values are directions you keep walking in.

    A goal is something you can tick off, like painting the hall or passing the test. A value is a direction you keep walking in, like looking after your health, or making home feel cared for, or being a fair friend. Nobody ever finishes being kind. That’s exactly why a value still works on a day when a goal falls apart.

    Bethan was going to go to the gym three times this week. She went once, on the Monday. On Thursday evening she was on the couch in her gym clothes eating a family bag of fries and feeling like a failure. So she asked herself what the gym was for. Looking after her body, was the answer, and when she asked what that was for, it was so she’d have energy for the kids. On Friday she walked twenty minutes at lunchtime, around the little park behind the office, past the man who feeds the pigeons, and as far as she was concerned it counted.

    Dig deeper: goals and values

  2. Your strong feelings point to what you value.

    You don’t have to invent your values from scratch. They show up in your strongest reactions. Anger often means something you care about has been stepped on. Envy can point to something you want for yourself. Sadness tends to show what you love.

    A colleague stood up in the quarterly review and took credit for a whole team’s work, and Sacha was so angry he couldn’t eat his lunch. He spent the afternoon drafting emails he didn’t send. On the train home it dawned on him that it wasn’t really about the colleague. It was fairness. Seeing people’s effort. That was what had been stepped on. At the next team meeting he raised it as a fairness thing (“Can we agree how we credit joint work?”) instead of seething at one person for a month, and it went better than any of the emails would have.

    Dig deeper: finding your values

  3. Two good things can pull in different directions.

    Most hard choices aren’t between something good and something bad. They’re between two good things, honesty and kindness, say, or family and rest. Feeling torn just means you care about more than one thing. Even when you choose well, it’s normal to feel a bit sad about the side you didn’t pick.

    Pickles, the family Labrador, was very ill, and Megan’s eight-year-old kept asking. Megan cares about being honest, and she also cares about protecting her son. She sat on the kitchen floor next to the dog’s bed with him and tried to do both. “Pickles is very sick. The vet is helping. We’ll look after him together.” Her son thought about it for a long time, picking at the edge of the dog blanket. “Is he going to die?” “He might,” she said. “We don’t know yet.” He put his hand on Pickles’s side, very carefully, and left it there.

    If you’re stuck on a choice, write down the good thing on each side. You may be able to honor some of both, even if you can’t have all of either.

    Dig deeper: when values clash

  4. Put your effort where it can land.

    About two thousand years ago a teacher called Epictetus, who had been born into slavery, taught that some things are up to us and some aren’t. It’s one of the oldest ideas in this book and still one of the most useful. Your own choices and how you treat people are up to you. Other people’s choices, and the weather, and the past, are not. Worry tends to pile up in the second heap. Values do their work in the first.

    Ingrid wants her partner to enjoy Christmas at her mom’s, and she can’t make him. She has tried, for six years, mainly by telling him it’ll be fun. What she can do is plan a shorter visit, put a long muddy walk on the day after Christmas into it, which he’ll like, and be warm herself. That’s her part, and it turns out to be quite a lot.

    Dig deeper: what is up to us

  5. A good life is more than a good mood.

    Feeling good matters. But many thinkers, from the Greek philosopher Aristotle to psychologists now, also describe a second kind of wellbeing. It comes from growing, using your strengths, being a decent friend, and doing things that matter even when they’re hard. Aristotle called it flourishing, and he thought you build it the way you build any skill, by practice, a little at a time.

    Jade spent a whole month of evenings helping a friend move out of a damp, miserable apartment. She scrubbed black mold off a bathroom ceiling with a toothbrush. She carried boxes down three flights of stairs while the friend cried on the landing. It was tiring and not much fun, and at the end of the year, when she thinks back over it, she counts it as one of the best months she had.

    Dig deeper: happiness and flourishing

  6. Meaning comes from ordinary places.

    Researchers who study meaning often describe three parts to it: your life makes some sense to you, you’re heading somewhere, and your life matters to someone. Most meaning comes from people, from work done with care and from small roles other people count on, far more than from big moments.

    Three meanings of meaning The three parts above come from Martela and Steger, who split meaning into coherence (life makes sense), purpose (a direction) and significance (your life matters). It’s a way of thinking that has shaped later research rather than a finding in itself.

    Martela and Steger (2016) · Theory: a conceptual paper that has shaped later measurement

    Walter has lived alone since his wife died, and for a long time his days went very quiet. Now he reads to the under-fives at the library on Wednesday mornings. He irons a shirt for it on Tuesday nights. He does the voices, badly. When he had a cold in February and missed two weeks, a four-year-old called Albie asked the librarian every single day where the man was.

    Try listing the roles you play, big or small (neighbor, sister, the one who waters the office plants), and next to each one write who it matters to.

  7. Different values aren’t wrong values.

    Two people can share a home, a family or a team and still put the same good things in a different order, one putting safety first and another adventure. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin argued that there are many real goods in life and they can’t all be ranked on one scale, which leaves room to disagree without either person being bad.

    People who love you will sometimes try to change your mind, and that’s ordinary influence. They tell you why, they listen to your reasons, and the choice stays yours. It becomes pressure when it keeps coming after you’ve said no, when saying no costs you something, such as days of coldness, money or being left out, or when it’s aimed at the people and things that matter most to you. A good test is whether you can say “I see it differently” and still be treated warmly the next day.

    Jasper likes a calm, tidy house. His roommate Darnell likes an open door and people dropping in. For months each of them privately thought the other was a bit much, uptight in one direction, messy in the other. It came out one night over takeout. “I just like knowing who’s going to be in the kitchen,” Jasper said. Darnell said, “I just like it when anyone is.” Fridays are open house now, and Sundays are quiet, or as quiet as Darnell can manage.

    In your next disagreement, you might ask, “What matters to you about this?” and listen for the value under the position.

    Dig deeper: living with different values

  8. Values live in small, everyday acts.

    A good life is built mostly from small things done again and again. How you say hello. Whether you keep small promises. How you talk about someone who isn’t in the room. The Chinese teacher Confucius taught that small, everyday courtesies shape who we become, and later thinkers who wrote about care said that being good starts with paying attention to the person in front of you.

    Kindness does something for the giver A meta-analysis of 27 experiments found that acts of kindness gave the person doing them a small-to-medium lift in wellbeing. The authors flagged worries about study quality, so take the size with a pinch of salt and the direction with rather less.

    Curry and colleagues (2018) · Moderate: the authors noted concerns about study quality and possible publication bias

    An older man at the self-checkout can’t get the card machine to take his card. The line behind him is growing and somebody near the back tuts, loudly. You’re next, and you don’t sigh. You say, “No rush.” He says, “It’s always this one, isn’t it,” and you agree that it is. The whole thing costs you about forty seconds.

    Pick one value, choose one tiny act that shows it, and do it every day this week. You’ll drift, as everyone does, and turning back is the practice.

Two ways to read this

Values, two ways

Both directions Some people hold a value as a rule: honesty means saying the true thing, every time. Others hold it as a direction, adjusted to the moment. If you hold rules, the direction person isn’t being slippery. They’re weighing two good things at once. If you hold directions, the rule person isn’t being harsh. A clear rule is how they stay steady.

Where the fields meet Politics has a lot to say about two people with different values under one roof: a fair way to decide, and a say for everyone, and a way to change the rule later. The root is ownership and structure, plus quiet incentives, because a home drifts toward whatever it rewards.

In everyday life

Partners may rank the same values differently, security and adventure, say, and it helps to say what each of you is protecting before you argue about the plan. Two generations of a family can both value family and mean very different things by it, from living around the corner to being free to go, so it’s worth asking what the word means to each of you. When a job keeps asking you to act against a value, the strain often shows up as tiredness or cynicism, and naming the clash is a first step. A club, a faith group or some volunteering can give you a place to act on a value alongside other people.

If you want to help someone else with this

“What matters most to you about this?” helps more than telling someone what they ought to care about. People often see their values for the first time in someone else’s words, so it can help to notice them out loud. “You stayed late to help your friend. Loyalty really matters to you, doesn’t it?” With children, naming the value when you see it teaches more than any lecture. Try not to use values as a weapon, because “I thought you valued family” lands as a guilt trip. You can disagree with someone’s choice and still see the good thing they were reaching for.

You could say “What would a good outcome look like to you?” “What do you want to be able to say about how you handled this, a year from now?”

Questions people ask

Some questions this book tends to raise, with short answers and where to read more.

I lost my temper and said something I regret. Is it too late to put it right?

It rarely is. A short apology that names what you did, without a “but,” and a plan for next time go a long way, even days later. Healing after hurt (Chapter 5, page ) shows how a repair goes, and Anger (Chapter 2, page ) has a pause plan for the next time the heat rises.

Someone I love is grieving. What should I say?

Something simple and true, such as “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.” Saying the name of the person who died is usually a kindness. Practical help, like a meal, a ride or a walk, often means more than the right words, and so does still being there a few months on. Loss, grief and big life changes (Chapter 4, page ) has more.

I keep starting good habits and dropping them. What am I doing wrong?

Probably nothing, except starting too big. Habits stick when the first step is tiny, tied to something you already do and easy to restart after a missed day. Habits and change (Chapter 6, page ) shows how to shrink a step until it takes two minutes or less.

Do I have to forgive someone who hurt me?

No. Forgiveness, trust and going back are all yours to choose, in your own time, and you can heal even if the other person never says sorry. Healing after hurt (Chapter 5, page ) explains what forgiving means and what it doesn’t.

If you’re having thoughts of hurting yourself or not wanting to be here, please tell someone you trust today.

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Prolonged grief
Grief that stays intense and gets in the way of daily life for a long time after a death (6 to 12 months or more, depending on the guideline). It is treatable.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill out on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
