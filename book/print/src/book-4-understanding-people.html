Attachment, trust and belonging

Why we need each other, how the bonds we had early on shape what we expect today, and how trust and belonging are built, lost and rebuilt, one small moment at a time.

Sunday, 9:30pm

Kai sent a long message to a friend at lunchtime. It’s been read, but there’s no reply. By evening, Kai has gone from “she’s busy” to “she’s annoyed with me” to “nobody really likes me.” The friend, meanwhile, is at her nephew’s birthday, phone in a coat pocket. One silence, two very different nights.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out, it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That isn’t weakness. It’s a signal, like hunger, telling us to reach for connection.

    For example: Rhea moves to a new city for work. She’s busy and doing well, but by the third month she feels flat and teary on Sundays. Nothing is wrong with her. She’s lonely, and loneliness is a real need asking to be met.

    Thoughts you could try: “Feeling lonely doesn’t mean I’m unlikeable. It means I need connection, like anyone.”

    Try this: This week, reach out to one person, even with a short “thinking of you” message.

    Dig deeper: why we need each other

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children, we learn from the people who cared for us whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder or to stop asking. These lessons are a starting point, not a life sentence. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    For example: A toddler at the park runs off to explore, then runs back to check his grandmother is still on the bench, then runs off again. She is his safe base. Knowing she’s there makes him brave enough to explore.

    Words you could use: (to a child) “I’m right here. Go and look, and come back whenever you want.”

    Try this: Think of one person who was a safe base for you, at any age. What did they do that helped?

    Dig deeper: what attachment is

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close, and need more space. Many of us feel mostly secure, and most of us mix all three.

    These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they are sliding scales, not boxes.

    For example: When things feel tense, Ronan wants to talk it through right now. His wife Clara needs to go quiet first. He reads her quiet as “she’s leaving.” She reads his questions as pressure. Once they see the pattern, they agree: Clara says “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and Ronan waits.

    Words you could use: “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet. Let’s talk at eight.”

    Try this: Notice what you do when you feel unsure of someone: reach out more, or pull back? No judgement, just notice.

    Dig deeper: anxious, avoidant and secure

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust doesn’t usually come from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again: doing what you said, turning towards someone when they reach out, being the same person on a hard day as on a good one.

    For example: A new team member says she’ll send the figures by Thursday, and she does. The next week, she flags early that something will be late. By the third month, her manager trusts her completely, not because of one big moment, but dozens of small ones.

    Words you could use: “I said I’d do it by Friday. I’m running late, so here’s where it’s at.”

    Try this: Keep one small promise today, and notice how it feels to be someone who does what they said.

    Dig deeper: how trust grows

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle our nervous system. In one study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. This works both ways: we can be that safe presence for others too, often without saying much.

    For example: After a panic attack, Kieran’s friend doesn’t try to fix anything. He sits next to him on the step and says, “Take your time. I’m here.” Kieran’s breathing slows.

    Words you could use: “You don’t have to talk. I’ll just sit with you.” “What would help right now: company, quiet, or both?”

    Try this: Notice who helps you feel calmer. Tell them, simply: “I always feel better after seeing you.”

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Groups like teams, families, clubs and communities can offer both. Being left out of a group hurts, even when the group doesn’t matter much to us. Real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    For example: At a new job, Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny and hides that she doesn’t drink. She’s fitting in, and she’s exhausted. At a book club, she says what she really thinks and people ask her to say more. That’s belonging.

    Words you could use: (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it okay if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    Try this: Think of one place where you can be fully yourself. How could you spend a little more time there?

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It returns slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    For example: Two adult sisters fall out after one shares a private story at a family dinner. The apology is a start. What rebuilds trust is the months afterwards, when the story never comes up again and the sister checks before sharing anything.

    Words you could use: “I know sorry isn’t enough. I’ll show you, and you can take whatever time you need.”

    Try this: If you’ve broken someone’s trust, name one specific thing you’ll do differently, and do it.

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can feel out of reach. But belonging starts small: a chat with a neighbour, a regular class, an online group about something you love, a kind word to yourself. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    For example: After his divorce, Martin starts saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall. A few weeks later they talk football every morning. It isn’t a deep friendship, but it makes Mondays easier, and it reminds him he can still connect.

    Words you could use: “I’m new around here. Is there anything you’d recommend?” “I loved what you said in the group. Would you like to get a coffee sometime?”

    Try this: Have one small, friendly exchange with someone you don’t know well this week.

If you’re a teenager

A made-up example. Ezra, 16, sees a photo of his three closest friends at the cinema, and finds out there’s a group chat he isn’t in. It hurts like a punch. Being left out really does register a bit like pain. Instead of posting something sharp, he messages one friend: “Bit gutted not to be asked. Did I do something?” It turns out they thought he’d be at football. One friendship really is drifting, though, and that’s allowed to hurt too.

Words you could use: “Bit gutted not to be asked. Did something happen?” “I’d like to come next time, even if I can’t always make it.” “I miss how we used to be. Are we okay?”

In everyday life

  • Partners: One partner texts a lot when apart; the other forgets. Agreeing on one goodnight message keeps both feeling connected.
  • Parents and children: A child who acts up at pick-up may simply be releasing a long day with the safest person they know.
  • Friends: A friend who always cancels may be overwhelmed, not uncaring. Asking “Is it easier if we do something short?” keeps the door open.
  • Coworkers: A team where mistakes can be admitted without blame builds trust faster than one where everyone hides them.
  • Housemates: A weekly shared meal, even a simple one, can turn people who share a kitchen into people who look out for each other.
  • Community: A neighbour who waves every morning is a small, real part of feeling at home.

Belonging isn’t only about other people. It also includes how you treat yourself. If you’re kind to yourself on a bad day, you carry a little safe base wherever you go. People who learn to comfort themselves often find it easier to let others in, too, because they’re less afraid of being let down.

Thoughts you could try: “I’m allowed to need people.” “Today was lonely. That’s hard, and it won’t always be like this.”

More for people on their own: On my own (spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own).

Common mix-ups

  • “Needing people means I’m needy.” Needing connection is human. Everyone has this need, even those who hide it well.
  • “My attachment style is fixed for life.” Patterns can change, especially through steady, kind relationships and reflection.
  • “An ‘anxious’ or ‘avoidant’ label explains everything.” Labels can help you notice patterns, but people are more than a type. Use them for curiosity, not judgement.
  • “Jealousy and checking up show how much they love me.” Reaching for reassurance is human, and so is asking for something out of care for the relationship. Controlling who someone sees, or checking their phone without agreement, is not love and is not an attachment style. See signs to look out for (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety).
  • “If I forgive, I have to trust again straight away.” Forgiving and trusting are different. Trust can return slowly, or not at all, and that’s your call.
  • “Belonging means everyone likes me.” Belonging usually means a few people who know you as you are. That’s enough.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be a steady presence. Reliability matters more than big gestures. Turn up when you said you would.
  • Take loneliness seriously. If someone says they feel alone, don’t rush to “But you have so many friends!” Listen first.
  • Invite, and keep inviting. People who feel left out may say no a few times. A warm, no-pressure invitation still matters.
  • Make room in groups. Notice who is on the edge, and ask their view.
  • Don’t diagnose. Avoid telling someone they’re “so avoidant” or “so clingy.” Ask what would help instead.

Words you could use: “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” “We’re having a few people over on Friday. No pressure, but I’d love you to come.”

When to get more help

  • If loneliness has lasted a long time, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of not wanting to be here, please talk to a doctor or a mental health service. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number or see Upset right now (spreadloveandacceptance.com/upset-right-now).
  • If fear of being left, or of getting close, keeps hurting your relationships, a counsellor or therapist can help you understand where it comes from and how to change it.
  • If someone uses your need for them against you, by cutting you off from friends, checking where you are, or threatening to leave to get their way, that is not about attachment. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
  • If trust was broken in a way that frightened you, you don’t owe anyone a second chance. Your safety comes first.

  • Small and set up beats big and willpower. Make the first step tiny and change the setup around you.
  • Slips are part of change. A missed day is information about the plan, not proof about you.

Monday, 7:05am

The plan was a walk before work. The trainers are by the door. But the phone is in your hand, and somehow it’s 7:40 and you’re scrolling with cold coffee. By evening, a quiet voice says: “See? You never stick to anything.” That voice is wrong. You aren’t weak. You’ve met a habit.

  1. Change is hard for good reasons.

    Much of what we do each day runs on autopilot. That saves energy, which is why habits exist. The downside is that the old way is always quicker and easier than the new one. Change asks your brain to do something effortful, again and again, until the new way becomes the easy one.

    For example: Jodie wants to stop snapping at her kids in the morning rush. She means it every night. Every morning, the same rush brings the same snap. She isn’t a bad parent; the morning is a well-worn groove.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is hard because it’s a habit, not because I’m failing.” “Old grooves take time to fill in.”

    Try this: Pick one thing you’d like to change and write: “When does it usually happen? Where? What’s going on just before?”

    Dig deeper: why change is hard

  2. Habits run on cues.

    Most habits have a trigger: a time, a place, a feeling, a person, or the thing you just did. The cue starts the routine, and some kind of reward (relief, comfort, a little buzz) keeps it going. If you know the cue, you can plan for it.

    For example: A man notices he reaches for snacks not when he’s hungry but when he opens his laptop after dinner. The cue is the laptop. He starts making tea first instead.

    Thoughts you could try: “What happened right before I did that?” “What was I getting from it?”

    Try this: For three days, each time the habit happens, jot down the cue: time, place, feeling, or what you were doing.

    Dig deeper: cues and routines

  3. Start smaller than feels useful.

    Big goals are inspiring but easy to drop. Tiny steps feel almost silly, and that’s why they work: they’re easy enough to do on a bad day. Tie the new step to something you already do: “After I put the kettle on, I’ll...” Once it sticks, you can grow it.

    For example: Two housemates want a tidier kitchen. Instead of a big weekly clean they never do, they agree: “After dinner, we each wipe one surface.” It takes two minutes, and the kitchen stays nicer.

    Words you could use: “Let’s start with something tiny, and see if it sticks.” “After I ..., I will ...”

    Try this: Shrink your change until it takes two minutes or less. Write it as “After I ___, I will ___.”

    Dig deeper: why small steps work

  4. Change the setup, not just your willpower.

    Willpower runs low when we’re tired, hungry or stressed. Your surroundings don’t. Make the new habit easier (put it in sight, get things ready the night before) and the old one a little harder (move it out of reach, add a step). Small changes to your setup can do more than any amount of trying harder.

    For example: A teenager wants to sleep better. Instead of “trying not to scroll,” he charges his phone in the kitchen. It works far better than willpower ever did.

    Words you could use: (to yourself or a partner) “What could we change about the setup so this gets easier?”

    Try this: Make one small change to your space today that makes the new habit easier or the old one harder.

    Dig deeper: shaping your setup

  5. Being in two minds is normal.

    Part of you wants to change. Part of you doesn’t, and that part usually has its reasons: the old habit gives comfort, saves effort, or protects you from something. Pushing harder against that part rarely works. Listening to it often does.

    For example: Asif wants to drink less, but he also loves the Friday pub with his team. Instead of “never again,” he asks what Friday gives him: friends, laughing, unwinding. He keeps the Friday, and switches to alcohol-free after the first drink.

    Thoughts you could try: “What does the old habit give me?” “What would make the change worth it to me, not just to others?”

    Try this: Write two lists: “What I like about how things are” and “What I’d like about changing.” Both lists are allowed.

    Dig deeper: readiness and mixed feelings

  6. Slips are part of change.

    Nearly everyone who changes something slips back sometimes. One slip doesn’t undo your progress. What often does is the thought that follows: “I’ve blown it, so why bother?” Treating yourself kindly after a slip makes it more likely you’ll start again, not less.

    For example: After three calm weeks, a dad shouts at his son over homework. That night he says, “I shouted, and I’m sorry. I’m working on it.” The next day he’s back on track. The slip became a repair, not a reason to give up.

    Thoughts you could try: “A slip is a moment, not a verdict.” “What made today harder, and what can I plan for next time?”

    Try this: Decide now what you’ll do after a slip: one kind sentence to yourself, and one small step to start again.

  7. Change sticks when it fits who you want to be.

    Change done only to please others, or out of shame, is hard to keep. Change that connects to what you care about, the kind of person, parent, friend or colleague you want to be, has deeper roots. Each small action is a vote for that person.

    For example: A woman who wants to “be better at keeping in touch” reframes it: “I’m someone who shows up for my friends.” Sending one voice note a week becomes part of who she is, not a chore.

    Thoughts you could try: “Who am I becoming with this step?” “This matters to me because...”

    Try this: Finish this sentence for your change: “I want to do this because I care about...”

  8. You can support change, but not force it.

    It’s natural to want people we love to change: to drink less, tidy more, look after themselves. But other people’s change belongs to them. Pressure tends to make people dig in. What helps more: saying clearly what you need, asking what would help, and noticing their small steps out loud.

    For example: A coworker keeps missing deadlines. Instead of “You need to be more organised,” his teammate asks, “What would make the handover easier for you?” They set up a shared checklist, and the deadlines improve.

    Words you could use: “I can’t change this for you, but I’d love to help if you want me to.” “I noticed you did that this week. That really helped.”

    Try this: Notice one small positive change someone else made this week, and tell them.

On your own: a kind plan for change

If you’re working on something alone, put the ideas together in one small plan:

  • One change. Not five. Pick the one that would make the most difference, or the one that feels most doable.
  • One cue. “After I ___, I will ___.”
  • One setup tweak. Make it easier to start.
  • One tired-day version. The smallest version you can still do on a bad day.
  • One kind sentence for slips. Written down, ready to use.

The individual program walks through this step by step: The self path (spreadloveandacceptance.com/self-path) and Know yourself, next.

Common mix-ups

  • “If I really wanted it, I’d just do it.” Wanting is only the start. Habits, setup, tiredness and stress all matter.
  • “It takes 21 days to form a habit.” There’s no magic number. In one study, led by Phillippa Lally (2010), it took about two months on average, with a very wide range.
  • “One slip means I’ve failed.” Slips are normal. Starting again is the skill.
  • “Being hard on myself will keep me on track.” Harshness tends to make people give up. Kindness makes it easier to try again.
  • “If I nag enough, they’ll change.” Pressure often makes people dig in. Ask, support and notice instead.
  • “Routines mean being rigid.” For many people, especially autistic people, routines are what make life manageable. Change can respect that.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask before advising. “Would ideas help, or would you rather just talk it through?”
  • Help them find their own reasons. “What would be different for you if this changed?”
  • Help with the setup. Offer practical things: a lift to the gym, a shared calendar, a quieter evening.
  • Notice small steps out loud. Encouragement for small wins often helps more than pointing out what’s still missing.
  • Be kind about slips. “That’s okay, tomorrow’s a new go” helps more than “I knew it.”
  • Look after yourself. You can care deeply and still set limits on what you’ll put up with.

Words you could use: “I’m on your side with this. What would help?” “You’ve done this three days running. That’s not nothing.”

When to get more help

  • If a habit is harming your health or life, such as drinking, drugs, gambling, eating or self-harm, please talk to a doctor or a specialist service. Needing help with this is common and nothing to be ashamed of.
  • If you can’t stop even though you’ve tried many times, professional support can make a real difference. It isn’t a sign of weakness.
  • If change feels impossible because you’re exhausted or low most days, that may be burnout or depression. A doctor or counsellor can help.

Six months on, you’re doing fine. Then, in the supermarket, you see the cereal your dad always bought, and you have to leave your trolley in the aisle and sit in the car. Nobody around you knows. It feels like day one again.

This chapter is for anyone who has lost someone or something: a person, a pet, a marriage, a friendship, health, a home, a job, a country or a future you had pictured. It explains how grief works first, then what can help. There is no right way to grieve, and nothing here is a timetable.

  1. Grief is love with nowhere to go.

    Grief is what we feel when something we were attached to is gone. It isn’t only for deaths. A divorce, a miscarriage, a diagnosis, a move, a friend who drifted, or children leaving home can all bring real grief. The size of the grief usually matches the size of the bond, not what other people think the loss “should” mean.

    For example: A man is surprised how hard he takes his dog’s death. His friends say “it was just a dog.” But that dog walked with him every morning for twelve years.

    Thoughts you could try: “This hurts because it mattered.” “My grief doesn’t need anyone’s permission.”

    Try this: Name one loss you’ve been carrying that you haven’t called grief. Say it to yourself: “I’m grieving…”

    Dig deeper: what grief is, and what counts

  2. Grief comes in waves, not neat stages.

    You may have heard of five stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. They came from listening to dying patients, and were never meant as steps everyone follows in order. Most people swing back and forth: some hours deep in the loss, some hours getting on with life. That back-and-forth is healthy. Laughing at a funeral, or having a good day, doesn’t mean you’ve stopped caring.

    For example: A widow feels guilty that she enjoyed lunch with her sister. Then she cries all evening. Both were grief doing its work.

    Thoughts you could try: “A good hour isn’t a betrayal.” “This is a wave. Waves pass.”

    Try this: Let yourself have one small break from grief today, without guilt: a walk, a show, a meal with a friend.

    Dig deeper: waves, and the swing between loss and life

  3. Grief lives in the body, too.

    Grief can bring tiredness, poor sleep, no appetite or extra appetite, aches, a tight chest, forgetfulness and a foggy head. Your brain is slowly learning that someone or something it expected to be there isn’t. That takes time, and it uses energy.

    For example: A teacher whose mother died keeps losing her keys and forgetting meetings. She worries something is wrong with her memory. It’s grief fog, and it eases as the months pass.

    Words you could use: “I’m grieving, so I’m slower than usual. Could you send me a reminder?”

    Try this: Pick three basics to protect this week: one regular meal, a set bedtime, and a short walk outside.

    Dig deeper: the grieving body and brain

  4. Some losses nobody sees.

    Some losses are hard to explain. Someone is still alive but changed, through dementia, addiction or illness. Someone is gone but not dead, like an estranged parent or a missing relative. Some losses aren’t recognised by others: an ex-partner’s death, a pregnancy loss, a friend you met online. Grief without a ceremony, or without other people noticing, can feel very lonely.

    For example: A woman’s father has dementia. He’s still here, and she still visits, but the dad who knew her is fading. She’s grieving someone who hasn’t died.

    Words you could use: “I’m grieving something people might not see as a loss. It is one for me.”

    Try this: If a loss of yours had no ceremony, make a small one: light a candle, write a letter, plant something, visit a place.

    Dig deeper: ambiguous and unrecognised grief

  5. People grieve in different ways.

    Some people grieve by talking and crying. Others grieve by doing: fixing, organising, working, building something. Neither is more loving. Families often clash because one person thinks the other “isn’t grieving” when they are just grieving differently. Culture, faith, age and how your mind works all shape it, too.

    For example: After their son’s death, one parent wants to talk about him every evening. The other spends weekends restoring his old bike. Each feels the other is getting it wrong. Both are grieving him.

    Words you could use: “I think we grieve differently. Can you tell me what helps you, and I’ll tell you what helps me?”

    Try this: Ask someone who shares your loss: “What has helped you most, even a little?” Listen without correcting.

    Dig deeper: grieving styles and families

  6. You don’t have to let go.

    An older idea said healthy grief meant “moving on” and cutting ties. Many grief researchers now see it differently: people often keep a bond with who they lost, in memories, rituals, conversations in their head, and values they carry forward. The relationship changes shape. It doesn’t have to end.

    For example: A teenager wears her grandfather’s watch to exams. She doesn’t believe in luck. It just feels like he’s with her.

    Thoughts you could try: “What would they say about this?” “I carry this part of them with me.”

    Try this: Choose one way to keep a bond: cook their recipe, tell a story about them, or keep one of their habits.

    Dig deeper: continuing bonds

  7. Big changes are losses too, even the good ones.

    A new baby, a promotion, retirement, moving country, a child starting school, coming out, or getting well after a long illness can all bring grief for the old life. Change often has three parts: an ending, a confusing in-between time, and a new beginning. The middle part is the hardest, and the easiest to rush.

    For example: A couple moves to a new city for a great job. Six months in, one of them feels low and guilty: “We wanted this.” They also lost friends, a favourite café and a whole way of life.

    Thoughts you could try: “I can be glad about this and still miss what came before.” “I’m in the in-between. It’s supposed to feel unsettled.”

    Try this: For one change you’re going through, write: what ended, what feels unclear, and one small thing that’s new and good.

    Dig deeper: endings, the in-between, beginnings

    Professor Puddles

    Tool: Talk it through in writing spreadloveandacceptance.com/write-it-through

  8. Most people find a way through, and support helps.

    Research suggests that many people, over time, find they can live with a loss and still feel joy, even though they never forget. Being with people who let you talk, or not talk, helps. For some, though, grief stays stuck at full strength for a long time and makes daily life very hard.

    Doctors call this prolonged grief: a strong longing for the person, or being taken over by the loss, most days, still going many months after the death (usually a year or more for adults) and getting in the way of life. That isn’t weakness, and there is specific help for it.

    For example: A year after his wife died, a man still can’t go into their bedroom or see friends. His doctor refers him to a grief counsellor. Slowly, things begin to move.

    Words you could use: “It’s been a long time and it isn’t getting any easier. I think I need more help.”

    Try this: Write down two people you could text on a hard day, and one place you could call if those people aren’t enough.

Your part, and only your part. You can’t control the loss, how long grief lasts, or how other people react to it. You can control small things: eating something, resting, telling one person how you are, and saying no to what you can’t manage right now. That is enough for a hard season. On your own: carrying it gently

Grief can be lonely, even with people around. Some things help when you’re carrying it alone:

  • Give the day a small shape: one meal, one walk, one person you message.

Common mix-ups

  • “There are five stages, in order.” Most people go back and forth. There is no correct order.
  • “After a year you should be over it.” Grief changes over time, but there’s no deadline. Anniversaries and birthdays often bring it back.
  • “If I’m not crying, I’m not grieving.” Some people grieve by doing, thinking or staying busy. That counts.
  • “Moving on means forgetting.” You can build a new life and keep a bond with who you lost.
  • “Good changes shouldn’t make me sad.” Every change ends something. Missing the old life is normal.
  • “I shouldn’t mention their name, it’ll upset them.” Many grieving people say hearing the name is a comfort. Ask.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Show up, and keep showing up. Support often floods in for two weeks and then stops. A text at three months, or on a birthday, can mean a great deal.

  • Say the name. Share a memory. Let them talk, or not.
  • Don’t fix or explain. Skip “everything happens for a reason” and “at least…”. Try “I’m so sorry. I’m here.”
  • Comfort in, vent out. If you’re upset too, lean on people further from the loss, not on those closest to it.

Words you could use: “I don’t know what to say, but I’m here.” “I was thinking about your mum today. I remember her laugh.” “I’m going to the shop, what can I bring you?”

Grief is not an illness.   1. Brains vary, like bodies do.

    People’s brains differ in how they take in the world, focus, move, read, remember and connect with others. “Neurodiversity” is the idea that this variety is natural. “Neurodivergent” describes people whose minds work differently from what is treated as typical. Different isn’t broken. And at the same time, differences can be disabling, especially when the world is built for one kind of mind.

    Try this: Think of one thing that’s hard for you that’s easy for others. Ask: is it effort, or is it a different kind of brain?

    Dig deeper: neurodiversity and disability

  2. Autism is a different way of sensing and connecting.

    Autistic people often communicate more directly and literally, have deep and focused interests, feel senses more (or less) intensely, and find predictability calming. Autism isn’t a line from “mild” to “severe.” Each autistic person has their own mix of strengths and needs, which can change day to day. Misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people usually run both ways.

    For example: An autistic coworker says, “Your slides have three typos.” She means it as help. Her colleague hears criticism. Neither is wrong; they’re using different rules for what kind means.

    Words you could use: “I like it when people say exactly what they mean. You can be direct with me.” “Do you want my honest view, or just support right now?”

    Try this: In one conversation today, say exactly what you mean, with no hints. Notice whether it makes things easier.

    Dig deeper: autism and the double empathy problem

  3. ADHD is about steering attention, not lacking it.

    People with ADHD often have plenty of attention, but find it hard to point it where they choose. Boring tasks can feel impossible; interesting ones can swallow hours. Time can feel slippery (“now” and “not now”), and feelings can come fast and big. It’s a real difference in how the brain manages itself, not a lack of effort or care.

    For example: A woman with ADHD forgets her friend’s birthday, then spends six hours making her an amazing handmade card. The forgetting and the focus come from the same brain.

    Words you could use: “I care about this. My brain drops things. A reminder really helps, and it doesn’t annoy me.”

    Try this: Move one thing you keep forgetting out of your head and into the world: an alarm, a note on the door, or a basket by the exit.

    Dig deeper: ADHD, time and motivation

  4. Everyone has a sensory profile.

    We all take in sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement and signals from inside the body. Some people feel these strongly and get overloaded by noise or scratchy clothes. Others need more input: movement, strong flavours, pressure. Overload isn’t being fussy. It’s a body at its limit.

    For example: Two housemates argue about the kitchen radio. One finds silence eerie; the other finds the radio painful. A rule helps: radio on mornings, headphones in the evening.

    Words you could use: “It’s not you. The noise in here is too much for me right now. Can we move somewhere quieter?”

    Try this: Notice one sound, light or texture that drains you, and one that calms you. Make one small change today.

    Dig deeper: senses, overload and needs

  5. Learning differences aren’t about intelligence.

    Dyslexia (reading and spelling), dyscalculia (numbers), dyspraxia (coordination and planning movement) and language disorders are specific differences, not a measure of how clever someone is. Many people have a “spiky” profile: very strong in some areas, struggling in others. The right tools, like audiobooks, spoken notes or extra time, can level the ground.

    For example: A teenager with dyspraxia is called clumsy at football. In drama, he writes and directs the school play. Same boy, different task.

    Words you could use: “I learn better when I can listen than when I read. Could you send a voice note instead?”

    Try this: Ask someone you live or work with: “What’s the easiest way for you to take in information?” Then use it.

    Dig deeper: spiky profiles and learning tools

  6. Masking costs energy.

    Many neurodivergent people learn to hide their differences: copying others, forcing eye contact, holding back movements, rehearsing small talk. This is called masking. It can help someone get through a day, but it uses a lot of energy, and over years it can lead to exhaustion, anxiety and burnout.

    For example: A student seems fine all day at college, then comes home and can’t speak for an hour. She’s not being rude. She’s recovering from a day of performing.

    Words you could use: “I need some quiet time after work. It’s how I recharge, not a sign I’m upset with you.”

    Try this: Notice one place where you can drop the mask a little, and one person you can be fully yourself with. Spend a bit more time there.

    Dig deeper: masking, energy and burnout

  7. Autistic burnout is more than being tired.

    Autistic burnout is a deep, long-lasting exhaustion. Working with autistic adults, Raymaker and colleagues (2020) described it as long-term exhaustion, loss of skills (like speech, planning or self-care) and lower tolerance of noise, light and other input. It comes from long stress without enough support, and often lasts months. Rest, fewer demands and less masking help. Pushing harder usually makes it worse.

    Words you could use: “I’m in burnout. I need fewer demands for a while, not a pep talk.” “I can’t do phone calls at the moment. Can we message instead?”

    Try this: If you think you might be in burnout, list three demands you could drop, delay or share for a month. If it goes on, or you feel hopeless, talk to a doctor.

    Dig deeper: burnout, rest and recovery

  8. Meltdowns and shutdowns aren’t choices.

    A meltdown is an involuntary loss of control when overwhelm gets too much: crying, shouting, running off or lashing out. A shutdown is the quiet version: going still, losing speech, not being able to respond. Neither is a tantrum. In interviews by Phung and colleagues (2021), autistic young people described meltdowns and shutdowns as separate experiences, both linked to overload. What helps is less: less noise, fewer words, fewer questions, more space and safety. Talking can wait.

    Words you could use: (to someone else) “You’re safe. I’m right here. No need to talk.” (for yourself) “I’m overloaded. I need quiet for twenty minutes.”

    Try this: On a calm day, agree a simple signal for “I’m overloaded” and a quiet place to go.

    Dig deeper: meltdowns and shutdowns

  9. Stimming helps, so let it be.

    Stimming means repeated movements or sounds, like rocking, flapping, tapping, humming, pacing or fiddling with something. Most people stim a little (think of a jiggling foot). For many autistic people it matters a lot. In interviews by Kapp and colleagues (2019), autistic adults described stimming as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions and sensations, and objected to efforts to stop harmless stimming. If it isn’t hurting anyone, it doesn’t need stopping.

    Words you could use: “Rocking helps me think. It doesn’t mean anything’s wrong.” “You don’t need to stop. It’s fine with me.”

    Try this: Notice what your own hands or body do when you’re stressed, focused or happy. That’s your stim.

    Dig deeper: stimming

  10. Deep focus makes switching hard.

    Some minds, often autistic and ADHD ones, focus deeply on one thing at a time. That focus can be a gift: real expertise, flow and joy. It also makes switching tasks hard, so being interrupted can feel like being pulled out of water. Warnings before a change, and routines, help a lot.

    For example: A dad calls his daughter to dinner three times and gets more frustrated each time. Then he tries a ten-minute warning, and a five. She comes to the table calm.

    Words you could use: “Dinner is in ten minutes. I’ll come and get you at five to.” “Can you give me a warning before we switch?”

    Try this: Before you interrupt someone who’s focused, give a heads-up and a time. See if it goes more smoothly.

    Dig deeper: monotropism, flow and transitions

  11. Deep interests are a source of joy and rest.

    Many autistic people have deep, intense interests, often called special interests. The theory of monotropism (Murray, Lesser and Lawson, 2005) describes autistic attention as pulled strongly into a few things at a time. In a survey of autistic adults by Grove and colleagues (2018), people who were more motivated by and engaged with their special interests reported higher wellbeing, though very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing.

    Words you could use: “Can I tell you about this for five minutes? It’s how I unwind.” “Tell me more. What’s the best bit?”

    Try this: Ask someone about their favourite subject, and let them talk for five minutes without steering.

    Dig deeper: special interests and wellbeing

  12. Meet in the middle, out loud.

    When two kinds of mind live or work together, neither should have to do all the adjusting. Clear, explicit words help everyone: say what you mean, give times, write things down, ask what helps. Changes made for one person, like quiet spaces, written agendas or flexible hours, often help many others too.

    For example: A team starts sending meeting agendas a day early. It was for one autistic colleague. Soon everyone says meetings are shorter and better.

    Words you could use: “What would make this easier for you?” “Here’s what helps me. What helps you?”

    Try this: Swap one “how I work best” fact with someone today: one thing that helps you, and ask for one that helps them.

    Dig deeper: adjustments that help everyone

  13. Labels are tools, and they belong to the person.

    A diagnosis can bring understanding, support and community. Some people find their way to a label through self-discovery first. Many autistic adults prefer “autistic person”; others prefer “person with autism.” The kindest rule is simple: ask, and use the words the person chooses. And never try to diagnose someone else, especially in an argument.

    For example: During a row, a partner says, “You’re so autistic.” It lands as an insult and ends the talk. Later, he apologises and asks what his partner actually needs.

    Words you could use: “How do you like to describe yourself?” “I’m wondering if I might be neurodivergent. I’d like to find out more.”

    Try this: If you’ve wondered about yourself, write down what you’ve noticed, and decide one small next step: reading, talking to someone, or asking a doctor.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis, identity and words

  14. Finding out later in life.

    More and more adults are finding out they are autistic, or have ADHD, in their thirties, fifties or seventies. Many were missed as children, especially women. In interviews by Leedham and colleagues (2020), women diagnosed at forty or older described how exhausting it had been to go so long without an explanation, and how much the diagnosis changed how they understood their lives. Relief, grief and joy can all come together.

    Words you could use: “I’ve just found out I’m autistic. I’m still working out what it means.” “That explains so much about my life.”

    Try this: If you’ve found out later in life, look for one autistic-led group, book or online space, and give yourself time to make sense of it.

    Dig deeper: diagnosis later in life

  15. Getting things started and finished is a skill of its own.

    Planning, starting, switching, remembering what you were doing and keeping track of time are called executive function. Many autistic and ADHD people find some of these harder, on some days more than others. A review of 235 studies by Demetriou and colleagues (2018) found differences in executive function in autism on average, with a lot of variation between people. It isn’t laziness. It’s the brain’s self-management system working differently, and it often helps to move the managing out of your head and into the world.

    For example: A woman can write a brilliant report but can’t get herself to post a parcel. Putting the parcel by the door, with a sticky note and a phone alarm, gets it done.

    Words you could use: “Could you send me that as a list? Spoken steps fall out of my head.” “I’m not ignoring it. Starting is the hard bit for me.”

    Try this: Pick one task that keeps not happening. Make the first step tiny and visible: a note, an alarm, or the thing left where you’ll trip over it. See routines that help (spreadloveandacceptance.com/adhd-kids) and Wired differently (spreadloveandacceptance.com/wired-differently).

    Dig deeper: executive function in daily life

  16. When demands feel like a threat.

    Some people, often autistic, feel strong anxiety when asked or expected to do things, even things they want to do. Avoiding the demand can look like refusing, joking, distracting or a meltdown. The profile was first described as “pathological demand avoidance,” or PDA, by Newson and colleagues (2003).

    It is not in the main diagnostic manuals, and researchers disagree about it: a review by Kildahl and colleagues (2021) found the evidence limited and mostly based on parent reports. Many people who identify with it prefer the phrase “persistent drive for autonomy.” Whatever it is called, what helps tends to be the same: less pressure, more choice, and requests that feel like invitations.

    For example: A boy who refuses “Put your coat on now” happily puts it on when his dad says, “It’s cold out. Coat or jumper, your choice.”

    Words you could use: “I wonder if we could…” “No rush. Which would you rather?” (for yourself) “Being told makes it harder. Can I choose when?”

    Try this: Turn one daily instruction into a choice or an invitation this week, and see what changes.

    Dig deeper: demand avoidance, carefully

  17. Not recognising faces isn’t rudeness.

    Prosopagnosia, or face blindness, means finding it hard to recognise people’s faces, sometimes even close friends out of context. It can be something you’re born with, and it often runs in families (NHS). Many autistic people also find remembering faces harder, as a review by Weigelt and colleagues (2012) found. People who don’t recognise you may be relying on voices, hair, clothes or where they usually see you.

    For example: A man walks past his neighbour in the supermarket without a word. She feels snubbed. Later he explains he didn’t recognise her without her dog. Now she says hello first, with her name.

    Words you could use: “I’m bad with faces, so please say who you are when you say hi.” (to someone else) “Hi, it’s Jo from next door.”

    Try this: If faces are hard for you, tell one or two people you see often. If not, try saying your name when you greet someone out of context.

    Dig deeper: face blindness

  18. Not speaking isn’t the same as having nothing to say.

    Some autistic people speak rarely, sometimes, or not at all, and some lose speech when stressed. Many communicate through AAC (augmentative and alternative communication): typing, letter boards, picture cards, apps or sign. Using AAC doesn’t stop speech developing; a review by Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006) found it did not hold speech back, and may help it. The safest starting point is to presume competence: assume the person understands and has something to say, and give them the time and tools to say it.

    For example: At a family meal, a non-speaking teenager types on her tablet. Her uncle used to talk about her in front of her. Now he waits, reads her reply, and answers her directly.

    Words you could use: “Take your time. I’ll wait.” “Would you rather type or point?” (for yourself) “I can’t talk right now. I can type.”

    Try this: Talk to the person, not about them. Wait longer than feels natural for an answer.

    Dig deeper: non-speaking people and AAC

  19. Telling friends and dates is your choice.

    Whether to tell someone you’re autistic or ADHD, and when, is up to you. Telling can bring understanding: in a study by Sasson and Morrison (2019), people formed better first impressions of autistic adults when they knew they were autistic, especially if they knew more about autism.

    But people don’t always respond well, and interviews by Au-Yeung and colleagues (2025) describe autistic adults weighing hope for support against fear of not being believed. Many people share a little at a time, starting with what helps rather than a label.

    For example: On a second date, a woman says, “I’m autistic, so I’m pretty direct, and I’d rather walk than sit in a loud bar.” Her date says, “Honestly, same.”

    Words you could use: “I’m autistic. For me that means…” “I won’t always pick up hints, so please just tell me.” (if someone shares with you) “Thanks for telling me. What helps?”

    Try this: Write one sentence about what helps you, without a label. Practise it. You can add the label later if and when you want to.

    Dig deeper: disclosure in friendships and dating

  20. Finding your people.

    Many autistic people describe finding other autistic people as a turning point: not having to translate, explain or mask. Research backs this up. Cooper, Smith and Russell (2017) found that autistic people who felt more connected to other autistic people had higher self-esteem, and through that, less anxiety and depression. Being autistic can be an identity and a community, not only a diagnosis.

    For example: After a late diagnosis, a man joins an online group for autistic adults who love maps. For the first time, a conversation doesn’t tire him out.

    Thoughts you could try: “There are people who work like me.” “I can belong somewhere without masking.”

    Try this: Look for one autistic-led group, book, podcast or online space about something you love.

Common mix-ups

  • “Everyone’s a bit autistic.” Everyone has some traits in common, but autism is a whole pattern, present across life. The phrase can make real needs sound small.
  • “ADHD just means you can’t sit still.” Many people with ADHD, especially girls and women, are mostly inattentive or restless on the inside.
  • “They seem fine to me.” Masking can hide a lot. Seeming fine isn’t the same as being fine.
  • “Adjustments are special treatment.” They remove barriers so people can do what’s expected. Glasses aren’t special treatment either.
  • “Autistic people lack empathy.” Many feel others’ feelings strongly. Differences in how empathy is shown run both ways.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Believe them about what’s hard, what hurts and what helps, even if it’s different for you.
  • Be clear. Say what you mean, give times, and write down anything important.
  • Ask, don’t assume. Every neurodivergent person is different. “What helps you?” beats any list.
  • Protect their energy. Quiet time, warnings before changes and a way out of loud places aren’t extras.
  • Enjoy their interests. Listening to someone talk about what they love is one of the warmest things you can do.

Words you could use: “Is there anything about this place, or this plan, that would make it hard for you?” “You don’t have to explain. Just tell me what you need.”

When to get more help

If you wonder whether a difference is part of ongoing difficulties at school, work or home, for you or someone you care for, a doctor, school support staff or a psychologist can talk you through an assessment. Waiting lists can be long, so start early and write down what you notice.

Burnout, depression and anxiety are more common among neurodivergent people, often because of the stress of living in a world that doesn’t fit. If you’re exhausted most days, losing skills you used to have, or feeling hopeless, please talk to a doctor. If you are having thoughts of hurting yourself, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

A difference never excuses harm, and it never makes someone deserve harm. If someone uses your differences to control or belittle you, Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) has free, private help.

Sunday, 10:50pm

The house is quiet. You’ve checked the front door twice. Tomorrow is the hospital appointment, and the same three thoughts go round: what if it’s bad, what if I can’t cope, what if there’s nobody to tell. Your chest is tight and your mind won’t stop. You haven’t really wanted to see anyone for a few weeks either. Everything feels like too much effort.

Almost everyone worries, feels low or goes through something frightening at some point. For some people these feelings settle by themselves. For others they stick, grow and start to shape every day. This chapter explains how they work, what tends to help, and when it’s time to ask for more help. It can’t diagnose anyone. If any of this is about you right now, you are not alone, and things can get better.

  1. Worry and anxiety are the body’s alarm.

    Fear is the body’s alarm for danger that is here now. Anxiety is the same alarm ringing about something that might happen. Worry is the thinking part: “What if…?” on repeat. They use the same stress system as Chapter 2: a racing heart, tight chest, shallow breathing, a churning stomach, poor sleep. The alarm is trying to protect you. The trouble is that it can be set too sensitive, and it can’t tell a real tiger from a worrying email.

    For example: A teenager’s heart pounds before every class presentation. Nothing bad has ever happened. Her alarm still treats a room of classmates like danger.

    Thoughts you could try: “This is my alarm, not a fact.” “My body is getting ready to protect me. I can thank it and carry on.”

    Try this: When worry rises, breathe out slowly, longer out than in, five times. Then name three things you can see. It tells the body the danger isn’t here right now.

    Dig deeper: how anxiety works

  2. Avoiding keeps fear going.

    When something makes us anxious, stepping away brings quick relief. But the relief teaches the brain, “Good thing we escaped.” Next time, the fear is a little bigger and the world a little smaller. What helps is the opposite: going back towards the feared thing in small, chosen steps, and staying long enough to learn that you can cope. Therapists call this exposure. Craske and colleagues (2014) describe how it works best when each step tests what you expect to happen.

    For example: After a panic attack on a bus, a man starts walking everywhere. With his sister’s help he takes one stop, then two, then a whole journey. The fear fades as his brain learns buses are survivable.

    Thoughts you could try: “What do I think will happen? Let’s see what actually happens.” “Uncomfortable isn’t the same as dangerous.”

    Try this: Pick one small thing you’ve been avoiding. Break it into three steps, from easiest to hardest. Do the easiest one this week.

    Dig deeper: avoidance and small steps

    Give worry a time and a place.

    Trying hard not to worry rarely works. What helps many people is postponing it: noticing a worry, writing it down, and saving it for a set “worry time” later in the day. In an early study by Borkovec and colleagues (1983), people who practised this for four weeks reported worrying less each day. In worry time, sort each worry: is there something I can do? If yes, plan one step. If not, practise letting it go.

    For example: A father caring for his own dad keeps a worry notebook by the kettle. At 6pm he gives it fifteen minutes. Many of the morning’s worries already look smaller.

    Thoughts you could try: “Noted. I’ll think about that at six.” “Can I do anything about this today? If not, it can wait.”

    Try this: Choose a fifteen-minute worry time, not just before bed. Keep a note on your phone for worries that pop up in between.

    Dig deeper: worry time and problem-solving

  4. Low mood is common. Depression is more than a bad week.

    Everyone has low days, and sadness after a loss or a disappointment is normal. Depression is different: low mood, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy, most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, often with poor sleep, tiredness, changes in appetite, feeling worthless or guilty, and finding it hard to think. It can look like sadness, or like irritability, numbness or just running on empty. It is a health problem, not a character flaw.

    For example: A retired teacher notices he has stopped going to his walking group, isn’t reading, and wakes at four every morning. He tells himself he’s just old. His daughter gently suggests it might be depression, and that it’s treatable.

    Words you could use: “I’ve not been myself for a few weeks, and I don’t think it’s just tiredness.” “I don’t enjoy things I used to. Can I talk to you about it?”

    Try this: If you’ve felt low or lost interest most days for two weeks or more, book a talk with a doctor this week. Write down what you’ve noticed to take with you.

    Dig deeper: low mood and depression

  5. Doing often comes before feeling better.

    Low mood says, “Wait until you feel like it.” But withdrawing makes life emptier, which deepens the low. A well-tested approach called behavioural activation turns this round: plan small, doable things that bring a bit of pleasure, meaning or achievement, and do them whether you feel like it or not. A review of 26 trials by Ekers and colleagues (2014) found it helped with depression, though many of the studies were small.

    For example: A woman living alone after her divorce plans three small things a day: a ten-minute walk, one call or message, one task she can finish. Some days they feel pointless. Over a few weeks, the days start to feel less flat.

    Thoughts you could try: “I don’t have to feel like it to do it.” “Small counts.”

    Try this: Write down three tiny things for tomorrow: one for pleasure, one for connection, one you can finish. Tick them off, however small.

    Dig deeper: behavioural activation

  6. Sleep, moving and people do a lot of the work.

    These sound too simple to matter, but the evidence for them is solid. Improving sleep can lift mood: in a large trial with university students, Freeman and colleagues (2017) found that an online sleep course reduced insomnia, and with it some other mental health difficulties.

    Exercise helps depression too: a review of 218 trials by Noetel and colleagues (2024) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all helped. And belonging to groups protects: Cruwys and colleagues (2013) found that people with depression who joined groups were less likely to relapse.

    For example: A new dad, exhausted and flat, agrees with his partner on one protected long sleep each weekend each, a pram walk every morning, and a weekly game of five-a-side.

    Words you could use: “I need help protecting my sleep for a while.” “Would you come for a short walk with me? I find it hard to go on my own.”

    Try this: Pick the one basic that is most off for you right now, sleep, moving or people, and make one small change to it this week.

    Dig deeper: sleep, exercise and connection

    Professor Puddles

    Small, steady things help: a walk, a regular bedtime, time with someone kind. Calm sounds can be a soft way to wind down at night.

  7. Trauma is about what happened, and what it left behind.

    Trauma usually means going through, or seeing, something that threatened life or safety, such as violence, abuse, a serious accident, war, or the sudden death of someone close. It is very common: in surveys across 24 countries, Kessler and colleagues (2017) found about 7 in 10 adults had been through at least one.

    Most people recover over time. A smaller number develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with memories that come back vividly, avoiding reminders, feeling on high alert, and sleep problems. Ordinary hard times and stress hurt too, even when they aren’t trauma in this sense.

    For example: Months after a car crash, a woman still jumps at screeching brakes and avoids driving at night. She isn’t “overreacting.” Her alarm learned that cars can mean danger, and hasn’t unlearned it yet.

    Thoughts you could try: “My reactions make sense after what happened.” “It’s in the past, even if my body doesn’t feel that yet.”

    Try this: If something frightening happened more than a month ago and it still shapes your days, talk to a doctor. Trauma-focused talking therapies help many people.

    Dig deeper: what trauma is and isn’t

  8. After something frightening: safety, choice and no pressure.

    After a frightening event, people need safety, practical help, warmth and choice. They don’t need to be made to talk. In fact, a review of single-session “debriefing” after trauma by Rose and colleagues (2002) found it did not prevent PTSD, and might make things worse for some. Services that are “trauma-informed” (SAMHSA, 2014) put safety, trust, choice and working together first. The same ideas help in families, friendships and workplaces.

    For example: After a break-in, a grandmother doesn’t want to talk about it. Her grandson doesn’t push. He fixes the lock, stays the night, and says, “If you ever want to talk about it, I’m here.” A week later, she does.

    Words you could use: “You don’t have to tell me anything. What would help right now?” “You’re safe now. I’m staying.”

    Try this: If someone you love has been through something frightening, ask one practical question (“What do you need today?”) and one open one (“How are you doing, really?”), and let them choose.

    Dig deeper: trauma-informed care

  9. Talking therapies help many people, and medicines can too.

    Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) teaches practical ways to notice and change patterns of thinking and doing. It is one of the most studied talking therapies: a review of over a hundred meta-analyses by Hofmann and colleagues (2012) found the strongest support for anxiety problems and stress, and support for many other problems too.

    Guidelines such as NICE (2022) also recommend guided self-help, behavioural activation, exercise groups and, for some people, antidepressants. Questions about medicine, including starting, changing or stopping it, are for a doctor or prescriber. Please don’t stop a medicine suddenly without advice.

    For example: A young man is embarrassed to ask for help with panic attacks. His GP refers him to a short course of CBT. Eight sessions later, he’s back on the train to work.

    Words you could use: “I’d like to talk about my mood. It’s been going on for a while.” “What are my options, and what do you recommend for someone like me?”

    Try this: Write three lines before an appointment: what you’ve noticed, how long, and what it’s stopping you doing. It makes the first conversation easier.

    Dig deeper: what treatment involves

Your part, and only your part. You can learn how your alarm works, keep the basics going, take small steps back towards life, and ask for help when it’s more than you can carry. You can’t think your way out of depression by trying harder, and you can’t fix someone else’s. Being kind to yourself, and getting the right help, are part of the work, not a failure at it. If you feel unsafe, reaching out today matters more than anything in this book.

Common mix-ups

  • “Anxiety means something is wrong with me.” Anxiety is a normal alarm. It becomes a problem when it’s too loud, too often, or starts shrinking your life.
  • “Depression is just sadness. Snap out of it.” Depression affects sleep, energy, thinking and body as well as mood. Nobody snaps out of it, but many people recover with the right help.
  • “Everything bad that happens is trauma.” Trauma usually means threat to life or safety. Other hard things still hurt and still deserve care, and the word doesn’t need to fit for help to be fair.
  • “Talking about trauma straight away helps everyone.” Some people want to talk, some don’t. Forcing it can make things worse. Safety and choice come first.
  • “Asking someone about suicide puts the idea in their head.” It doesn’t. A review by Dazzi and colleagues (2014) found no evidence that asking increases suicidal thoughts, and it may help.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Notice and ask. “You’ve seemed low lately. How are you doing, really?” Then listen more than you talk.
  • Don’t argue with the feeling. “Cheer up” and “It could be worse” push people away. “That sounds really hard” keeps them close.
  • Offer something specific. A walk, a meal, a lift to the doctor, sitting together. Small and practical beats big and vague.
  • Ask directly about safety if you’re worried. “Are you having thoughts of ending your life?” If yes, stay with them, and get help together today.
  • Look after yourself too. Supporting someone who is struggling is tiring. See Chapter 12 (spreadloveandacceptance.com/book/understanding-12-helping).

Words you could use: “I’m not trying to fix it. I just want you to know I’m here.” “Would it help if I came with you to the doctor?”

We all belong to groups, and we need them. Chapter 5 (page ) looked at belonging: why we need each other and how trust grows. This chapter looks at the other side: how groups pull on us, why we go along, and how to stay true to yourself while staying connected.

  1. The pull to fit in is old and strong.

    For most of human history, being part of a group meant survival. Being left out still feels alarming. In studies by Williams and colleagues (2000), people who were simply left out of an online ball-throwing game, by strangers they would never meet, felt worse and less like they belonged. So we bend to stay in: laughing at jokes, keeping quiet, going along.

    For example: A new starter at a warehouse laughs at a teasing nickname he dislikes, because he wants to be one of the team.

    Thoughts you could try: “Wanting to fit in is normal. I still get to choose what I go along with.”

    Try this: Notice one moment this week when you went along with something to fit in. No judging. Just notice.

    Dig deeper: why being left out hurts

  2. We go along more than we think.

    In Solomon Asch’s famous experiments (Asch, 1956), people judged which of three lines matched another line. When the actors around them all gave the same wrong answer, people went along with the group on about a third of the answers, even though the right answer was obvious.

    Many never went along at all. Later research, such as a review by Bond and Smith (1996), found the effect is real but varies: it has been smaller in more recent studies, and larger in cultures that put the group first.

    For example: In a meeting, everyone praises a plan. One woman sees a big problem, but stays quiet. Afterwards, two colleagues tell her they had doubts too.

    Thoughts you could try: “Would I say this if I were on my own?” “If I’m thinking it, someone else might be too.”

    Try this: Before a group decision, write down your own view first. Then listen to the group.

    Dig deeper: Asch and what came after

  3. Authority can lead people a long way.

    In Stanley Milgram’s experiments (Milgram, 1963), many ordinary people kept giving what they believed were painful electric shocks to a stranger, because a man in a lab coat told them to. (No shocks were real.) Burger (2009) repeated part of it safely and found similar results. But the studies are also debated: later researchers, such as Haslam and Reicher (2012), argue people went along when they believed in the cause, not blindly, and many people refused.

    For example: A nurse is told by a senior doctor to give a dose that looks wrong. She checks, speaks up, and is right. Checking is not disloyalty.

    Words you could use: “Before I do this, can I check one thing?” “I’m not comfortable with this. Can we look at it again?”

    Try this: Think of one person whose word you tend to follow without question. Is there anything you’d want to check next time?

    Dig deeper: Milgram and its critics

  4. “Us and them” forms fast.

    Part of how we see ourselves comes from our groups: our family, town, team, faith or job. Tajfel and Turner (1979) called this social identity. It brings pride and loyalty. It also makes us quick to favour “us” and to see “them” as all the same, even when the groups are formed on almost nothing. Knowing that this happens is the first step to catching it.

    For example: Two branches of a family always sit apart at weddings. Nobody quite remembers why. One year, a cousin swaps the seating plan, and the two sides discover they like the same terrible music.

    Thoughts you could try: “Am I seeing a person, or a group?” “What do we have in common?”

    Try this: Think of a “them” in your life. Name one person from that group as an individual: something they like, something they worry about.

    Dig deeper: social identity

  5. Norms are the rules nobody wrote down.

    Every group has norms: what you wear, how you speak, what you laugh at, what you never mention. We often guess the norms wrong. In studies at a university, Prentice and Miller (1993) found that most students were privately less comfortable with heavy drinking than they thought everyone else was. Each person thought they were the odd one out, so everyone kept going along.

    For example: In an office, everyone answers emails at midnight because they think it’s expected. When one manager says, “I don’t expect replies after six,” relief spreads.

    Thoughts you could try: “Is this really what everyone thinks, or just what everyone does?”

    Try this: Pick one unwritten rule in a group you’re in. Ask one person, privately, what they really think of it.

    Dig deeper: norms and wrong guesses

  6. Cultures differ, and so do people inside them.

    Cultures differ in how much they put the individual or the group first. Researchers call this individualism and collectivism (Hofstede, 1980). In some, “standing out” is admired. In others, fitting in and keeping harmony are signs of maturity. Neither is better. But be careful with labels: a large review by Oyserman and colleagues (2002) found the differences were smaller and less tidy than often claimed, and individuals vary hugely within every culture.

    For example: A woman raised in a close-knit family moves in with a partner who grew up encouraged to “do your own thing.” He sees her daily calls home as clingy; she sees his rare calls as cold. Both are following the rules they learned.

    Words you could use: “Where I grew up, this was normal. What was normal for you?”

    Try this: Name one thing you think of as simply “how people should behave.” Where did you learn it? Might someone else have learned the opposite?

    Dig deeper: culture, with care

  7. Every family is a small culture.

    Families have their own norms: how loud people are, how feelings are shown, who does what, what’s talked about and what isn’t, how you say sorry, or if you do. We often only notice our family’s rules when we live with someone from a different family. Part One looks at the rules you grew up with.

    For example: Two housemates clash over the kitchen. In one’s family, you wash up straight away. In the other’s, you wash up in the morning. Neither is lazy or fussy; they grew up in different small cultures.

    Words you could use: “In my family we always… What did yours do?” “Shall we make our own rule?”

    Try this: Write three rules from the family you grew up in. Keep one you value, and question one you don’t.

    Dig deeper: family cultures

    Online groups pull harder and faster.

    Group chats, forums and social media make group pressure quicker and more public. Likes and replies show the norm instantly. In a study of over half a million posts, Brady and colleagues (2017) found that messages with more moral and emotional words spread further, mostly among people who already agreed. Outrage travels fast; quiet doubts don’t.

    For example: A parents’ group chat turns against a teacher after one angry post. A dad who knows the teacher well sends a calm message: “I’d want to hear her side before we decide.” Two others agree.

    Thoughts you could try: “Would I say this to their face?” “The loudest posts aren’t the whole group.”

    Try this: Before you react to a heated post, wait ten minutes. Then decide whether to reply, reply privately, or not reply at all.

  9. You can belong and still be yourself.

    Belonging is being accepted as you are; fitting in is changing to be accepted (see Chapter 5 (page )). Good groups have room for difference. It helps to know your own values, to have more than one group, and to have at least one ally. In Asch’s experiments, when just one other person gave the right answer, going along with the group dropped sharply.

    For example: A teenager loves chess but his friends tease it as “nerdy.” He joins a chess club as well as keeping his friends. With two groups, neither one can decide who he is.

    Thoughts you could try: “Which groups let me be fully myself?” “Who would back me up if I spoke?”

    Try this: Write down three things you value. Next time a group pulls you, check: is this in line with them?

    Dig deeper: staying yourself

    Disagree kindly, and early.

    You don’t have to choose between going along and starting a war. A kind dissent is short, calm, about the issue rather than the people, and leaves room for others. Speaking early is easier than speaking late. And you don’t have to do it in front of everyone: a private word often works better.

    For example: Back to the group chat. Instead of a laughing face, you message your aunt privately: “I know it was meant as a joke, but it might hurt. Could we leave it?” She deletes it.

    Words you could use: “I see it a bit differently. Can I say why?” “I’m not comfortable with that joke.” “I might be the only one, but I’d like us to think again.” “Can we hear from people who haven’t spoken yet?”

    Try this: Choose one sentence from above that feels like you. Say it out loud a few times, so it’s ready when you need it.

  • At work: Teams where people feel safe to speak up make better decisions. See At work (spreadloveandacceptance.com/work) and the library on teams (spreadloveandacceptance.com/library/teams).
  • Online: Mute, leave or take a break from groups that leave you feeling worse. You’re allowed.
  • When everyone agrees too fast: That’s called groupthink. If you lead, share your view last and ask for one risk from each person before deciding. More in the full chapter (spreadloveandacceptance.com/book/understanding-17-groups-in-depth).
  • Teams rarely in one room: Write decisions down, go round everyone before deciding, and keep a short check-in so people at home aren’t forgotten. More in the full chapter.

Common mix-ups

  • “Only weak people conform.” Everyone is influenced by groups. Knowing that makes you more free, not less.
  • “Milgram proved people will do anything they’re told.” Many people refused, results varied a lot with the setup, and the studies have real critics. People seem most likely to go along when they believe in the cause.
  • “Culture explains everything about a person.” Culture shapes us, but people vary hugely within every culture. Ask the person, don’t assume.
  • “Disagreeing will ruin the relationship.” Calm, kind disagreement often builds trust. Swallowing it for years tends to do more harm.
  • “Fitting in is the same as belonging.” Fitting in means changing to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be the ally. If someone speaks up, back them up, even quietly: “I think that’s a fair point.” One ally makes a big difference.
  • Make room. In any group you lead, invite quieter people and different views: “What haven’t we thought of?”
  • Check in privately. If someone seems to be going along against their wishes, ask gently, away from the group.
  • Watch for exclusion. Notice who is left out of chats, plans and jokes, and bring them in.

Words you could use: “I noticed you went quiet when they said that. Are you okay?” “I’d like to hear what you think, even if it’s different.”

When to get more help

Group pressure is part of life. It may be time for more help if:

  • a group, family or partner controls who you see, what you wear, what you spend or what you believe, or punishes you for disagreeing;
  • you feel you can’t leave a group without being threatened, shamed or cut off from everyone you know;
  • you’re being bullied, at school, at work or online;
  • being left out or pressured is leaving you very low, anxious or hopeless.

Talk to someone you trust, a counsellor or a doctor. Not safe at home? lists free, private help if someone at home controls or frightens you. For low mood or worry, see Chapter 16 (page ). If you are thinking about ending your life, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

Six threads that run through the book

The book rests on five big ideas, set out at the front: see the whole load, fix the setup, not the person, read your state first, tune how you send and receive and notice the quiet incentives. The six threads below are the same ideas said as everyday habits, plus one rule that sits around all of them: safety first. The table after the list shows how they match.

  1. Notice before you judge.

    A sharp word, a forgotten job or a quiet evening usually has a reason: tiredness, a full stress tank, a different family rule, a different kind of mind.   3. Say it plainly and kindly.

    What happened, how it felt, what you’re asking for. Clear is kind, for everyone. See Chapter 4 (spreadloveandacceptance.com/book/understanding-4-communication).

  4. Small steps beat big pushes.

    One job with one owner. One walk. One message. One step on the ladder. Change that lasts is usually small and repeated. See Chapter 6 (page ).

  6. Your part, and only your part. Safety first.

zqglossaryqz

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Alexithymia
Finding it hard to notice, name or describe your own feelings. It is a trait on a scale, not an illness, and it is more common in some autistic people. Naming body signals first can help.
Battery
Your battery is how much energy you have left today. Full means you have room to spare; low means you’re running on empty. “How much are you carrying?” measures the other side of it, your load: a higher load score means a heavier load, and a lower battery.
A bid
A bid is any small reach for attention or connection, like “look at this,” a sigh or a joke.
Calm-Down Kit
The Calm-Down Kit is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately.
Double empathy
The idea, from the autistic researcher Damian Milton, that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways: each finds the other hard to read, so neither side is simply “missing” something.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
Groupthink
When a close group wants agreement so much that doubts go unsaid and a poor decision goes ahead. Irving Janis named it in 1972.
If-then plan
A small plan written as “If this happens, then I will do that”, which makes a new habit easier to start (Peter Gollwitzer).
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. Today’s Weather calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
Masking
Masking is hiding the way you’re wired to fit in, which can be very tiring over time.
Monotropism
A theory of autistic attention: attention gathers deeply on a few things at a time, rather than spreading thinly across many. Switching away can take real effort.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in Today’s Weather, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Prolonged grief
Grief that stays intense and gets in the way of daily life for a long time after a death (usually a year or more for adults). It is treatable; a GP or doctor is a good first step.
Psychological safety
A shared sense in a group or team that you can speak up, ask a question or admit a mistake without being punished or embarrassed (Amy Edmondson).
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
The rulebook from home
The rulebook from home is the unwritten set of rules you grew up with, about love, upset, chores and rest, that you may still expect others to follow.
Setup score
The one number from Is the setup working for everyone?: balance, ownership and stress added up with weights (0.40, 0.35 and 0.25), from 0 to 1. 0.70 and up is working well, 0.40 to 0.69 needs a look, under 0.40 needs a rethink, together. It judges the setup, never a person.
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?: a load score from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 to 0.59 moderate, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Today’s Weather
Today’s Weather is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Window of tolerance
The range in which you can feel strong feelings and still think and talk. Above it you are flooded or revved up; below it you shut down. A phrase from the psychiatrist Dan Siegel.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A workpaper is a short worksheet. Each person fills in their own, then you read them together.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
