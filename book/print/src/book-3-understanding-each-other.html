This book is about the people around you. It looks at how pace and tone pull people out of step, how words land, why two people remember the same moment differently, belonging and trust, love in its many forms, the pull of groups, and how to help someone without burning out.

Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

In short

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.” “I know, I’m just asking when.” Four sentences later nobody is talking about the bins any more, and neither of you could point to the moment it turned.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. It is like two radios slightly out of tune, which together make a crackle that neither was making alone.

    For example, one housemate asks whether anyone is using the oven tonight, and the other asks back whether she left a mess. A plain question has been heard as a complaint.

    You could say “I think the words were fine. Something in how we said them got crossed.”

    Try this. After your next small clash, ask yourself whether it was the words or the way they came out.

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on very different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they are close but not quite matched, which is why people who know each other well can still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we are close to, because we assume they already understand us.

    Two sisters who talk every day argue about holiday plans far more often than either does with the cousins they see once a year.

    Words you could use “I think I assumed you knew what I meant. Let me say it more clearly.”

    This week. With someone close to you, check one meaning today, with “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean…?”

    Dig deeper: why close can be noisy

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    The first is how fast you are talking. The second is tone, as when a simple update is heard as a complaint. The third is how urgent something feels, since “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and next week to another. Texts lose most of their tone, which makes urgency especially easy to misread.

    For example, a business partner texts “Need to talk” between two meetings. The other partner reads it as a crisis and lies awake until two in the morning, and in the end it turns out to be about the printer.

    Ways to say it “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    One small step. Add a time to your next request, so that “at some point” becomes “by Friday”.

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you are tense, you may talk faster and be more likely to hear a pause as a snub. When you are tired and flat, your replies get slower and shorter. The Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check, gives you a best guess at which state you are in.

    A dad gets home from a night shift and answers his teenage son in single words. His son decides that he doesn’t care, when he is simply running on empty.

    Words to try “I’m running low right now, and it isn’t about you. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

    Something to try. Before a tricky talk, name your state to yourself as calm, revved up or running on empty.

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work perfectly will still crackle if they are slightly off, and arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning, which means getting back in step, will.

    For example, a grandmother tells her grown-up son that he looks tired. She means it as care, and he hears that he isn’t coping, so both of them are right about what they meant and what they heard.

    You could say “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Try this. In your next clash, try “we” in place of “you”, as in “We got crossed there.”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say out loud that you think you are out of sync, and check how tense you feel. Either of you can call a pause, and whoever pauses names a time to come back. Slow down gently, with shorter turns and more pauses, and only then go back to the actual topic.

    Two roommates in halls agree on a pause word, “static”, on a calm day. In the middle of an argument about noise one of them says it, and they stop and come back at nine as agreed.

    Words you could use “I need ten minutes. I’ll come back at half past.”

    This week. Agree a pause word with someone today, while you are both calm.

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    When you return to the topic, say what happened, how you feel and what you would like. “The bins go out early on Wednesday morning. I get anxious when they’re late. Could you put them out tonight?”

    For example, a co-parent texts, “The swim kit wasn’t in the bag and I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?”

    One small step. Write one sentence like that about something small, before you need it.

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in, makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big argument. Habit research suggests small routines at a regular time are easier to keep than big ones done rarely. The quiet, easy stretches are the ones that drift most, so keep it going even when nothing seems wrong (see Complacency).

    A long-distance couple in two time zones send each other one 90-second voice note every morning, and on the weeks they forget, they notice the difference by Thursday.

    Ways to say it “Today I was carrying quite a lot. One thing I liked today was…”

    Something to try. Pick a time and a cue, such as straight after dinner, and try the check-in for three days.

For many couples, faith is where they turn first, and a shared faith can be one of the strongest tools for staying in tune. Praying together, or a quiet moment after worship, can serve as a daily check-in. Some couples each pray on their own before they come back to a hard talk, with the pause still ending at a named time. A pastor, imam, rabbi, priest or trusted elder can be a good first person to talk to, and many can suggest a trained counsellor who shares your faith.

Emeka and Ngozi, married for twenty-six years, do their monthly look-back on a Sunday after church, over lunch. When a talk gets heated, either of them can say, “Let’s pray on it and come back at eight,” and they do.

Culture shapes how you talk too. In many families, respect for elders decides who can speak plainly to whom, and one habit helps a great deal, which is that each of you speaks to your own parents and relatives once you have agreed the message together.

Words to try “Can we pray about this and talk again tonight?” “Mum, we love you, and we’ve thought about it carefully. This year we’ll visit in August instead of at Easter.”

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace, tone and sense of urgency, so you notice when you are the one running fast or slow. Learn your early signs of drifting, such as a tight chest, a clipped reply or the wish to win. Learn how you take words in best and say it once, as in “I hear things better in writing.” When you drift, step out, settle and come back, which you can do without anyone’s permission. One example, followed through

Cerys, Marisol and Dougie share a house and a car, and two of them talk fast while the third, Dougie, talks slowly. At house meetings Dougie kept getting talked over, and for a while the other two read his silence as agreement. It came out in March, when he said he had never actually agreed to the new car rota. Common mix-ups

  • “Retuning means giving in.” You still come back to the topic, at a time when you can hear each other.
  • “If we’re out of sync, one of us must be wrong.” Two fair points in two different tones can still clash.
  • “A pause means the topic is dropped.” A pause always comes with a time to come back.

To try it, agree on a shared phrase while things are calm, such as “I think we’re out of tune”, so that it works as a signal when you need it. For one week, each of you can note one moment you felt out of step, and at the end you can compare whether it was mostly pace, tone or urgency. Rewind the talk, Say it so it lands and the 90-second daily check-in are free on the site.

A limit to keep in mind: These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking things through ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private hotlines.

Thursday, 8:15am

Ana texts her brother to ask him to call when he is free. She means there’s no rush and she has a small question about the weekend. He reads it on the bus and spends the whole ride wondering who is ill.

Small habits help a message arrive, such as listening, checking, starting softly and repairing quickly, and a few ready-made lines make small talk easier for anyone who dreads it.

  1. Meaning is made between two people.

    Words don’t carry meaning on their own. The speaker has a meaning in mind, and the listener builds their own from the words, the tone, the timing and everything they already expect. Most of the time the two match well enough, and sometimes they don’t, without either person being able to see the gap from where they stand.

    For example, a manager writes “Let’s chat tomorrow” to a new coworker, meaning a friendly catch-up, and he spends the evening convinced he is in trouble.

    You could say “I think what I meant and what you heard might be two different things. Can we check?”

    Try this. Today, notice one moment when someone took your words differently from how you meant them, and leave blame out of it.

    Dig deeper: how meaning travels

  2. Every message says two things.

    There is what you say, and there is what it says about the two of you. “You’re late” is a fact about time, and it can also mean “I was worried” or “I don’t matter to you.” People often argue about the fact when the hurt is in the second layer.

    When one housemate asks another whether she used his pan, the question is about a pan and also, underneath, about whether his things are respected, which no amount of talk about the pan will settle.

    Words you could use “I don’t really mind about the pan. I think I wanted to know my stuff matters here.”

    This week. Next time a small thing stings, ask yourself what it seemed to say about us.

    Dig deeper: the two layers of a message

  3. Listening is something you do, not something that happens.

    Hearing happens by itself, while listening is a choice. Most of us listen partly to reply, so that we can fix, defend, advise or tell our own story. Real listening means following what the other person is trying to say and letting them know you got it before you add anything of your own.

    For example, a teenager says school is pointless, her dad starts explaining why it isn’t, and she stops talking. The next time she says it, he asks, “Pointless how? Tell me about today,” and she talks for twenty minutes.

    Ways to say it “So what I’m hearing is... Have I got that right?” “Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?”

    One small step. In one conversation today, wait until the other person has finished, then say back what you heard before you reply.

    Dig deeper: what good listening is

  4. Check, don’t guess.

    Once we know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it, so we leave out steps, assume the other person can tell what we mean and guess what they meant instead of asking. We do this most with the people we know best. A short check costs a sentence, while a wrong guess can cost a whole evening.

    Two friends plan something low-key for a birthday. One pictures a takeaway on the sofa and the other books a quiet restaurant, and both are disappointed because each thought it was obvious.

    Words to try “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean...?” “When you say soon, do you mean today or this week?”

    Something to try. Pick one person you know well and check one meaning with them today, even if you feel sure.

  5. Hard things land better when they start soft.

    How a hard talk begins often shapes how it ends. A complaint about one thing (“The bins weren’t taken out”) is easier to hear than a criticism of the person (“You never help”). A simple shape works for most hard things, which is to say what happened, how you feel and what you would like.

    For example, a woman wants her elderly father to stop driving at night. She leaves out “You’re not safe any more” and says, “You had two near-misses last month and I lie awake worrying. Could I drive us in the evenings?”

    You could say “What happened was... I felt... Could we...?”

    Try this. Write one sentence in that shape about something small that has been bothering you. You don’t have to send it yet.

    Dig deeper: saying hard things kindly

  6. Mix-ups are normal. Repair is the skill.

    Even close, kind people misunderstand each other all the time. What keeps a relationship steady is noticing and fixing it with lines like “That came out wrong”, “I think I misread you” or “Can we start again?”, and small repairs made often build trust.

    At the supermarket a stranger snaps “Excuse me!” when you block the aisle, and you snap back. A second later you add, “Sorry, long day,” and she smiles and says the same is true for her.

    Words you could use “Can I try that again?” “I think I took that the wrong way. What did you mean?”

    This week. The next time something comes out wrong, repair it out loud within a minute, and notice how small a repair can be.

  8. Different styles aren’t wrong styles.

    Some people say exactly what they mean and others hint and expect you to read between the lines. Some talk fast and overlap while others need a pause to think. Families, cultures and different kinds of minds all shape this, and a clash between two styles is usually a matter of translation.

  9. Small talk has a job, and it can be short.

    Small talk (“Cold today, isn’t it?” “Busy weekend?”) isn’t really about the weather. Its job is to say that you see the other person and that the two of you are okay. In 1923 the anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski called this “phatic communion”, talk whose main purpose is bonding more than information. Small moments like this matter more than people expect. Sandstrom and Dunn (2014) found that people who had a short, friendly exchange with a coffee-shop barista felt more belonging and a better mood than people who kept it purely efficient.

    Small talk is hard work for many people, though, including many autistic people, shy or anxious people and people speaking a second language. The rules are unwritten, it can feel pointless and it uses energy. A few ready-made lines, sometimes called social scripts, take the guesswork out, and keeping it short is allowed.

    For example, a dad who dreads the school gate keeps three lines ready, starting with “Morning! How’s your week going?”, then “Oh, nice,” and finally “Right, I’d better get going. See you Friday.” Some mornings he still hides in the car until the bell, but most days he gets through it.

    You could say “Morning! How’s your week been?” “How do you know Nico?” “Oh, how was that?” “Lovely to see you. I’d better get on.” And to opt out kindly, “I’m not great at chit-chat, but it’s really nice to see you.”

    Try this. Write your own three lines, one to open, one follow-up question and one to close, and use them once this week. If you would rather skip ahead to a real topic, “Can I ask you about something I’m actually curious about?” works too.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think, and your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well and fast, but it means that what you see is partly what you expected to see, so two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out, it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That hurt works like hunger, telling us to reach for connection.

    For example, Rhea moves to a new city for work. She is busy and doing well, yet by the third month she feels flat and teary on Sundays, when the office is shut and nobody in the city knows her well enough to call.

    Things to tell yourself “Feeling lonely says I need connection, like anyone does.”

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children, we learn from the people who cared for us whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder or to stop asking. These lessons are a starting point that later relationships can change. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    A toddler at the park runs off to explore, runs back to check that his grandmother is still on the bench, and runs off again, braver each time because he knows where she is.

    You could say (to a child) “I’m right here. Go and look, and come back whenever you want.”

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close, and need more space. Many of us feel mostly secure, and most of us mix all three.

    These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they work as sliding scales.

    For example, when things feel tense, Ronan wants to talk it through straight away while his wife Clara needs to go quiet first. He reads her quiet as a sign she is leaving, and she reads his questions as pressure. Once they see the pattern, they agree that Clara will say “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and that Ronan will wait.

    Words you could use “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet. Let’s talk at eight.”

    One small step. Notice whether you reach out more or pull back when you feel unsure of someone, and leave judgement out of it.

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust rarely comes from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again, such as doing what you said, turning towards someone when they reach out, and being the same person on a hard day as on a good one.

    A new team member says she will send the figures by Thursday, and she does. The next week she warns early that something will be late. By the third month her manager trusts her completely, and he would struggle to name a single moment when it happened.

    Ways to say it “I said I’d do it by Friday. I’m running late, so here’s where it’s at.”

    Something to try. Keep one small promise today, and notice how it feels to be someone who does what they said.

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle our nervous system. In one study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. It works both ways, so we can be that safe presence for others too, often without saying much.

    For example, after a panic attack, Kieran’s friend doesn’t try to fix anything. He sits next to him on the step outside the flats and says, “Take your time. I’m here,” and they stay there until Kieran’s breathing slows.

    Words to try “You don’t have to talk. I’ll just sit with you.” “Would company help right now, or quiet?”

    Try this. Notice who helps you feel calmer, and tell them simply that you always feel better after seeing them.

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Groups like teams, families, clubs and communities can offer both. Being left out of a group hurts even when the group doesn’t matter much to us, and real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    At a new job, Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny and hides the fact that she doesn’t drink, and she goes home exhausted every Friday. At her book club she says what she really thinks, and people ask her to say more.

    You could say (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it okay if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    This week. Think of one place where you can be fully yourself, and how you might spend a little more time there.

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It returns slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    For example, two adult sisters fall out after one shares a private story at a family dinner. The apology is a start, and what slowly rebuilds trust is the months afterwards, when the story never comes up again and the sister checks before sharing anything.

    Words you could use “I know sorry isn’t enough. I’ll show you, and you can take whatever time you need.”

    One small step. If you’ve broken someone’s trust, name one specific thing you’ll do differently, and do it.

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can feel out of reach, and belonging can start much smaller, with a chat with a neighbour, a regular class or an online group about something you love. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    After his divorce, Martin starts saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall, and a few weeks later they talk football every morning. It is a small friendship, and it makes Mondays easier.

    Ways to say it “I’m new around here. Is there anything you’d recommend?” “I loved what you said in the group. Would you like to get a coffee sometime?”

    Something to try. Have one small, friendly exchange with someone you don’t know well this week.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be a steady presence. Reliability matters more than big gestures. Turn up when you said you would.
  • Take loneliness seriously. If someone says they feel alone, listen before you remind them how many friends they have.

Friday, 10:30pm

You and Charlie have shared a home for eleven years. Tonight you are both on the sofa and both on your phones, and you realise you can’t remember the last time you talked about anything except bills, the boiler and whose turn it is to cook. You love Charlie, and you feel oddly alone in the same room.

This chapter is about closeness in all its forms, far beyond romance. It looks at what helps people feel near to each other, why closeness can fade and how it can grow again, and it builds on Chapter 4 (page ), on attachment and trust.

  1. Love comes in many forms.

    The ancient Greeks had several words for love, for friends, for family, for romantic partners and for strangers. People love in many ways today too. There are lifelong friendships, partners of any gender, people with more than one partner, chosen family, people who are asexual or aromantic and build deep closeness without romance or sex, and people on their own who pour love into friends, family, pets, work and community. A life can be full of love without a partner in it. A woman in her fifties who has never wanted a partner describes her best friend of thirty years as her great love story. They have been through two house moves, one cancer scare and more Sunday phone calls than either could count.

    It can help to write down three or more people you love, of any kind, and next to each one way you show it.

    Dig deeper: the many forms of love

  2. Closeness grows from being known.

    Intimacy is about much more than touch or romance, and at its heart is being known. The psychologists Reis and Shaver described it as a back-and-forth, in which one person shares something real about themselves and the other responds in a way that leaves them feeling understood, accepted and cared for. In a diary study by Laurenceau, Barrett and Pietromonaco (1998), feeling that the other person really responded was a big part of what made everyday conversations feel intimate.

    Two brothers who mostly talk about football are driving to their dad’s care home when one admits that he finds the visits really hard. The other says he does too, and that he always feels guilty on the way home. It is the longest conversation they have had in years, and it lasts until the car park. Sharing one small, true thing with someone you care about, a worry, a hope or something that made you happy this week, and noticing how they respond, is the same process on a smaller scale.

    Dig deeper: intimacy as being known

  3. Closeness is built a step at a time.

    People usually get close by sharing a little more, and then a little more, when it feels safe. In a well-known study by Aron and colleagues (1997), pairs of strangers who took turns asking gradually more personal questions, now known as the “36 questions”, felt closer after forty-five minutes than pairs who made small talk. The same idea works in friendships and long relationships, where closeness grows when both people take small, matched steps. Two new neighbours start with the weather and the parking. A few months later one asks how the other’s mum is doing, and some time after that one of them says, “Can I tell you what’s really been going on?”

    Next time you talk to someone you would like to know better, ask one question a little deeper than usual, such as what has been on their mind lately, and if they answer, share something of your own back.

  4. Feeling understood matters most.

    What makes someone feel loved is often less about grand gestures and more about feeling understood. Researchers call this responsiveness, meaning noticing, understanding and caring about what matters to the other person, and it shows in small moments such as how you react to good news. Gable and colleagues (2004) found that people whose partners usually met their good news with real interest and warmth felt closer and more satisfied together. When a teenager announces that she has been picked for the band, her grandad stops what he is doing, smiles and asks which instrument and when the first concert is, and she talks for twenty minutes.

    The next time someone shares good news with you, put your phone down and ask two follow-up questions.

    Dig deeper: responsiveness

  5. Old maps show up in love.

    The patterns from Chapter 4 (page ) often come out most strongly with the people we love most. Hazan and Shaver (1987) suggested that adult love uses the same attachment system as childhood bonds. When closeness feels shaky, some people reach harder for reassurance and others pull back to feel safe, and both are ways of protecting something precious. When his partner goes quiet, Leo texts five times in an hour, while his partner, who goes quiet in order to calm down, feels crowded. They agree that his partner will say, “I need an hour, and I’ll message you at eight,” and Leo finds he can wait for a known time.

    Notice what you do when closeness feels shaky, and tell someone you trust what helps you feel safe again.

    Dig deeper: attachment in adult love

  6. Love changes over time.

    Early love is often intense, with racing thoughts and a lot of longing, and over the years it usually settles into something calmer and deeper. A review by Acevedo and Aron (2009) found that some people in long relationships still report strong romantic love, without the anxious, obsessive edge of early love, and that this was linked to being happier together.

    In experiments by Aron and colleagues (2000), couples who tried a new, mildly challenging activity together felt better about their relationship afterwards than couples given a dull task. After twenty-five years, one couple sign up for a pottery class. They are both terrible at it, laugh all evening and bring home a bowl that won’t stand up, and on the way home one of them says it felt like a date. Something new for both of you matters more than something big, whether that is a new walk, a new recipe or a new place.

  7. Love at every age.

    Love looks different across a life. Teenagers’ first loves can be huge and real, and adults juggle love with work, children and caring for parents. Many older people choose to spend their time with fewer, closer people, which Carstensen and colleagues (1999) linked to feeling that time is more limited, so that the most meaningful relationships matter more. People find new love at seventy and eighty, and people lose great loves and carry them always. A widower of seventy-eight meets a woman at his walking group, and when his children are surprised he tells them, “I loved your mother for fifty years. I still do. And I’m allowed to be happy now.”

    If it feels right, ask someone from another generation, an older relative or a younger neighbour, about love in their life.

    Dig deeper: love across the lifespan

  8. Love is something you do.

    Feelings of love come and go, while the actions of love can be steady. They include turning towards someone when they speak, keeping small promises, saying thank you and sorry, and making room for who they are. Physical closeness, like a hug or holding hands, matters a lot to some people and less to others, and sex is part of closeness in some relationships and not in others. What matters is that both people feel comfortable and free to say yes or no. Love never includes fear, threats or control.

    A woman notices that her friend has been quiet since his divorce. She never says she loves him, but every Thursday she texts the single word “Walk?” and turns up at his door. You might ask someone you love what small thing makes them feel cared for, and then do it this week.

In everyday life

On your own

Being single, widowed, divorced or happily alone doesn’t mean a life without love, because friendships, family, community and the way you treat yourself are all places where love lives. If you want a partner and don’t have one, that longing is real and worth being gentle with (see On my own). Most partners drift into talking only about jobs and bills, and one real question a week can bring them back. Friendship needs tending like any other love, and families often love each other deeply and say it rarely, so a short “I’m glad you’re my sister” can mean a great deal.

Why fitting in pulls so hard, how groups shape what we say and do, how culture and family set the unwritten rules, and how to stay yourself, and disagree kindly, without losing your place, whether the group is a family, a friendship, a team, a faith, a neighbourhood or a group chat.

Thursday, 9:20pm

The family group chat is buzzing. Someone has posted a joke about your cousin’s new partner, and the laughing faces are piling up underneath it. You don’t find it funny, and you also don’t want to be the one who can’t take a joke. Everyone else seems fine with it, and your thumb is already hovering over the laughing face.

We all belong to groups, and we need them. Chapter 4 (page ) looks at belonging, at why we need each other and how trust grows. This chapter looks at the other side, at how groups pull on us, why we go along and how to stay true to yourself while staying connected.

  1. The pull to fit in is old and strong.

    For most of human history, being part of a group meant survival, and being left out still feels alarming. In studies by Williams and colleagues (2000), people who were simply left out of an online ball-throwing game, by strangers they would never meet, felt worse and less as if they belonged. So we bend to stay in, by laughing at jokes, keeping quiet and going along. A new starter at a warehouse laughs at a teasing nickname he dislikes, because more than anything that first month he wants to be one of the team.

    This week, notice one moment when you went along with something to fit in. Wanting to fit in is normal, and you still get to choose what you go along with.

    Dig deeper: why being left out hurts

  2. We go along more than we think.

    In Solomon Asch’s famous experiments (Asch, 1956), people judged which of three lines matched another line. When the actors around them all gave the same wrong answer, people went along with the group on about a third of the answers, even though the right answer was obvious.

    Many people never went along at all. Later research, such as a review by Bond and Smith (1996), found the effect is real but varies, smaller in more recent studies and larger in cultures that put the group first. In a meeting where everyone praises a plan, one woman sees a big problem and stays quiet, and afterwards two colleagues tell her they had doubts too. Writing down your own view before a group decision, and only then listening to the group, makes it easier to say what you actually think.

    Dig deeper: Asch and what came after

  3. Authority can lead people a long way.

    In Stanley Milgram’s experiments (Milgram, 1963), many ordinary people kept giving what they believed were painful electric shocks to a stranger because a man in a lab coat told them to, although no shocks were real. Burger (2009) repeated part of the study safely and found similar results. The studies are also debated. Later researchers, such as Haslam and Reicher (2012), argue that people went along when they believed in the cause, and many people refused.

    A nurse is told by a senior doctor to give a dose that looks wrong to her. She checks, speaks up and turns out to be right, and checking was the loyal thing to do. Think of one person whose word you tend to follow without question, and whether there is anything you would want to check next time, perhaps with “Before I do this, can I check one thing?”

    Dig deeper: Milgram and its critics

  4. “Us and them” forms fast.

    Part of how we see ourselves comes from our groups, whether that is a family, a town, a team, a faith or a job. Tajfel and Turner (1979) called this social identity. It brings pride and loyalty, and it also makes us quick to favour “us” and to see “them” as all the same, even when the groups are formed on almost nothing. Two branches of one family always sit apart at weddings, and nobody can quite remember why. One year a cousin quietly swaps the seating plan, and the two sides discover a shared weakness for terrible music.

    Think of a “them” in your life and name one person from that group as an individual, with something they like and something they worry about.

    Dig deeper: social identity

  5. Norms are the rules nobody wrote down.

    Every group has norms about what you wear, how you speak, what you laugh at and what you never mention, and we often guess them wrong. In studies at one university, Prentice and Miller (1993) found that most students were privately less comfortable with heavy drinking than they thought everyone else was. Each person thought they were the odd one out, so everyone kept going along. In one office everyone answers emails at midnight because they think it is expected, until a manager says she doesn’t expect replies after six, and the relief is almost audible.

    Pick one unwritten rule in a group you belong to and ask one person, privately, what they really think of it.

  6. Cultures differ, and so do people inside them.

    Cultures differ in how much they put the individual or the group first, which researchers call individualism and collectivism (Hofstede, 1980). In some, standing out is admired, and in others fitting in and keeping harmony are signs of maturity. Labels need care, though. A large review by Oyserman and colleagues (2002) found the differences were smaller and less tidy than often claimed, and individuals vary hugely within every culture. A woman raised in a close-knit family moves in with a partner who grew up being told to do his own thing. He sees her daily calls home as clingy and she sees his rare calls as cold, while each is following the rules they learned.

    Name one thing you think of as simply how people should behave, then ask where you learned it and whether someone else might have learned the opposite.

    Dig deeper: culture, with care

  7. Every family is a small culture.

    Families have their own norms about how loud people are, how feelings are shown, who does what, what is talked about and what isn’t, and how you say sorry, if you do. We often only notice our family’s rules when we live with someone from a different family, and Where your lens came from (Book 1, Chapter 1, page 9) looks at the rules you grew up with. Two housemates clash over the kitchen because in one’s family you wash up straight away and in the other’s you wash up in the morning, and neither of them is lazy or fussy.

    Write down three rules from the family you grew up in. Keep one you value, and question one you don’t, perhaps by asking, “What did yours do? Shall we make our own rule?”

    Online groups pull harder and faster.

    Group chats, forums and social media make group pressure quicker and more public, because likes and replies show the norm instantly. In a study of over half a million posts, Brady and colleagues (2017) found that messages with more moral and emotional words spread further, mostly among people who already agreed. Outrage travels fast while quiet doubts stay put. A parents’ group chat turns against a teacher after one angry post, and a dad who knows the teacher well sends a calm message saying he would want to hear her side before anyone decides. Two other parents agree with him, and the thread goes quiet.

    Before you react to a heated post, wait ten minutes, then decide whether to reply, to reply privately or to leave it.

  9. You can belong and still be yourself.

    Belonging means being accepted as you are, while fitting in means changing in order to be accepted (Chapter 4 (page ) says more). Good groups have room for difference. It helps to know your own values, to have more than one group and to have at least one ally, and in Asch’s experiments, when just one other person gave the right answer, going along with the group dropped sharply. A teenager who loves chess is teased about it by his friends, so he joins a chess club as well as keeping his friends, and with two groups neither one gets to decide who he is.

    Write down three things you value, and the next time a group pulls on you, check whether the pull is in line with them.

    Disagree kindly, and early.

    You don’t have to choose between going along and starting a war. A kind disagreement is short and calm, it is about the issue rather than the people, and it leaves room for others. Speaking early is easier than speaking late, and a private word often works better than speaking in front of everyone. Some people keep a sentence ready, such as “I see it a bit differently. Can I say why?”, “I’m not comfortable with that joke” or “Can we hear from people who haven’t spoken yet?”

    Choose the sentence that sounds most like you and say it out loud a few times, so it is ready when you need it.

If you’re a teenager

Group chats are where a lot of friendship happens, and a lot of hurt. Rhys is fifteen when he finds a photo of himself from PE being joked about in his class chat. He doesn’t answer back.

He screenshots it, mutes the chat and tells his sister, and together they tell his form tutor. Then a girl in his class messages him privately to say it wasn’t funny and that she is sorry she didn’t say anything. Most people in a group chat don’t like what is happening, and each of them thinks everyone else does.

You could say “I’m not okay with this. Please delete it.” To a friend, “That wasn’t okay. Are you alright?” To an adult, “Something’s happening online and I need help with it.”

In everyday life

Having no group can be as hard as having a pushy one, and groups built around something you enjoy bring belonging along with the activity. Partners bring two families and two sets of rules, which are worth talking about openly before you make your own. Children and teenagers feel peer pressure strongly, and practising a “no” that saves face helps them. Old family roles, such as the joker, the peacekeeper or the black sheep, can be hard to step out of. At work, when everyone agrees too fast, it helps if whoever leads shares their view last and asks each person to name one risk before deciding (more in the full chapter). And online, you are allowed to mute, leave or take a break from groups that leave you feeling worse.

If you want to help someone else with this

If someone speaks up, back them up, even quietly with “I think that’s a fair point,” because one ally makes a big difference. In any group you lead, invite quieter people and different views by asking what nobody has thought of yet. If someone seems to be going along against their wishes, ask gently, away from the group, and keep an eye on who gets left out of chats, plans and jokes, so you can bring them in.

Words you could use “I noticed you went quiet when they said that. Are you okay?” “I’d like to hear what you think, even if it’s different.”

When to get more help

Group pressure is part of life. It may be time for more help if:

  • a group, family or partner controls who you see, what you wear, what you spend or what you believe, or punishes you for disagreeing;
  • you feel you can’t leave a group without being threatened, shamed or cut off from everyone you know;
  • you’re being bullied, at school, at work or online;
  • being left out or pressured is leaving you very low, anxious or hopeless.

Talk to someone you trust, a counsellor or a doctor. Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety) lists free, private help if someone at home controls or frightens you. For low mood or worry, see Book 2, Chapter 3 (page 53). If you are thinking about ending your life, please tell someone today.

In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.

Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend is telling you about her awful week, and you have five good ideas before she has finished her first sentence. You share all of them. She goes quiet and says, “Yeah, maybe,” and you both hang up feeling a little worse without quite knowing why.

Most of us want to help the people around us, and helping well is a skill, because good intentions don’t always land. This chapter looks at how support works, why fixing can backfire and how to care for others in a way you can keep up, whether you are a friend, partner, parent, coworker, carer or neighbour.

  1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in different kinds. Some people want ideas, some want someone to listen, some want a practical hand such as a meal or a lift, and some just want company. Giving the wrong kind, however kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard, and the simplest fix is to ask.

    For example, in the phone call above, a better start might have been, “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to vent?”

    You could say “Would ideas help, or would you rather I just listened?” “What would help most right now?”

    Try this. Next time someone tells you about a problem, ask what kind of help they want before you give any.

    Dig deeper: kinds of support

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being truly heard helps people calm down, think more clearly and often find their own answers. Good listening means paying attention, letting them finish, checking you understood and showing that their feelings make sense, even if you would feel differently.

    Ravi comes home upset about his boss. His partner resists saying “Just quit” and says, “So he criticised you in front of everyone, after you stayed late all week. No wonder you’re furious.” Ravi’s shoulders drop, and ten minutes later he is sketching out his own plan on the back of an envelope.

    Words you could use “So what happened was… Have I got that right?” “That makes sense. Anyone would feel that way.”

    This week. In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice, then sum up what you heard.

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone, we want to make the problem go away. Doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable, though, so helping well often means helping someone do it themselves, which respects that it is their life and their choice.

    For example, Rosalind’s twenty-four-year-old son is struggling with a job application, and she itches to rewrite it. She asks instead whether he would like her to read it and say what stands out, and he makes the changes himself and is quietly proud when he gets the interview.

    Things to tell yourself “Is this mine to fix, or theirs to solve with my support?” “What do they want to do here?”

    One small step. Notice the urge to fix, and before acting ask, “Would you like me to do that, or help you do it?”

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were your own can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion, which is warm concern from a steadier place, still caring deeply without sinking alongside the other person. A steady helper is more use than a second person drowning.

    When Abi’s housemate went through a breakup, Abi cried with her every night and stopped sleeping, and after a week she was exhausted. She stayed kind and present after that while taking breaks, and reminded herself that this was her housemate’s pain to feel and hers to accompany.

    Things to tell yourself “I can be with them in this without making it mine.” “I care, and I’m steady.”

    Something to try. After a heavy conversation, do something that brings you back to yourself, such as a walk, a shower or five slow breaths.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything leads in the long run to resentment, exhaustion and eventually to not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can give, so your yes means something, and a kind no that comes with an offer of what you can do is still help.

    For example, Kojo’s sister keeps asking to borrow money. He loves her and he is running out, so he tells her, “I can’t lend money any more. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you’d like.”

    Ways to say it “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    Try this. Write down one thing you are giving that you can’t keep giving, and plan a smaller version you could offer instead.

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot, such as carers, parents, nurses, teachers and the friend everyone calls, can burn out. The signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, feeling numb or cynical about the people you help, getting ill more often and dreading the next call, and they mean that you need help too.

    Helen has looked after her husband since his stroke. She snaps at him and then feels terrible, and her doctor says it sounds like carer strain. A local carers’ group and a few hours of respite each week give her back a little of herself.

    Thoughts you could try “Looking after myself is part of looking after them.”

    This week. Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love.

  7. In a crisis, stay with them and bring in help.

    If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s okay to ask them directly. Research, such as a review led by Tim Dazzi (2014), suggests asking about suicide doesn’t put the idea in someone’s head; it can bring relief. You don’t have to be their therapist. Your job is to listen, stay with them, and help them reach trained support.

    For example (a coworker): A colleague says, “Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me.” Kirsty says gently: “I’m really glad you told me. Are you thinking about ending your life?” She says she’s had thoughts. Kirsty stays with her and they call a crisis line together.

    Words to try “Are you thinking about suicide?” “I’m glad you told me. You don’t have to go through this alone. Let’s call someone together.”

    One small step. Save a crisis number in your phone now, so you have it if you ever need it. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

    Dig deeper: helping in a crisis

  8. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something go wrong, each person can assume someone else will act. Psychologists call this the bystander effect, and knowing about it helps you break it. Small, safe actions count, such as asking “Are you okay?”, getting staff or calling for help.

    For example (a stranger): On a train, a man is making a young woman uncomfortable. Yvonne doesn’t confront him. She sits next to the woman and asks loudly, “Hi! Is this the train to Leeds?” The woman joins in the chat, and the man moves away.

    You could say “Are you okay? Do you need anything?” To someone else nearby: “Can you get the driver? I’ll stay here.”

    Something to try. Think of one safe thing you could do if you saw someone in trouble in a public place, such as distracting, getting help or checking in afterwards, because having a plan makes it easier to act.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  9. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways, and people who are good at giving help are often bad at receiving it. Letting others help is a gift to them too, because most people like being useful to someone they care about, and relationships where only one person ever helps tend to feel uneven over time.

    For example, Nkechi said “We’re fine, thanks” to every offer after the baby came. When she finally said yes to a neighbour’s offer of a lasagne, the neighbour was delighted, and these days they walk their buggies round the park together.

    Words you could use “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

    Try this. Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage without it.

Common mix-ups

  • “Helping means solving the problem.” Often, helping means listening, being there or helping someone find their own answer.
  • “Good helpers never say no.” Good helpers last because they know their limits.
  • “Asking about suicide might give them the idea.” Research suggests it doesn’t, and asking can help someone open up.
  • “I’ll make it worse if I step in.” There are small, safe ways to help. Doing nothing often feels worse for the person in trouble.

If you want to help someone else with this

The helpers around you need looking after too.

  • Notice the helpers, such as the carer, the friend everyone calls and the coworker who always covers, and ask them how they are as if you mean it.
  • Offer specific help. “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.”
  • Give them permission to rest. “You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this.”
  • If a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love or suggest they talk to a doctor.

  • If someone talks about suicide, stay with them and contact a crisis line together. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.
  • If someone tells you they’re being hurt or controlled at home, believe them, don’t confront the other person yourself, and share Not safe at home? (spreadloveandacceptance.com/safety)
  • If you’re burned out as a helper or carer, talk to your doctor, a carers’ organisation or a counsellor. Respite and support are not luxuries.
  • If helping someone is becoming unsafe for you, step back and bring in professionals. You can still care from a safer distance.

Words for hard moments

Some ways to say the hard parts, collected in one place. Change the words until they sound like you.

To begin

  • “Is now a good time to talk about Saturday, or would tomorrow be better?”
  • “Something has been on my mind, and I’d rather tell you than let it grow.”
  • “I’d like to understand how it looked from your side.”

To check what they meant

  • “When you said ‘fine’, did you mean fine, or not really?”
  • “Can I check I’ve got this right?”
  • “What did you hear me say?”

To pause

  • “I need twenty minutes. I’ll come back at half past.”
  • “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet.”

To put it right

  • “That came out wrong. Can I try again?”
  • “I think we both meant well, and it still went sideways.”
  • “I’m sorry I was sharp with you earlier.”

When someone is struggling

  • “Do you want ideas, or would you like me to just listen?”
  • “That sounds hard. Tell me more.”
  • “I can do Tuesday evenings. Who else could we ask?”

We care about each other but keep falling out. Where do we start?

Start with pace. Two people who both care can still run at different speeds, talk things through at different times and feel urgency about different things. Our messages keep going wrong. Any quick fixes?

Read a message twice before you reply, and assume the warmest tone that fits the words. Short texts lose their tone on the way, so a quick “Do you mean…?” saves a lot. How communication works (Chapter 2, page ) covers this, and the Chat analyzer on the website lets you check how a message may land before you send it.

My family remember things completely differently from me. Who is right?

Often both of you, a little. Memory keeps what mattered to each person and quietly fills in the rest. How we see each other (Chapter 3, page ) explains why, and suggests asking “What do you remember about that day?” before you share your own version.

How do I help someone without wearing myself out?

Ask what kind of help they want, do the part you can keep doing, and say plainly where your limit is. Help that lasts usually comes from more than one person. Helping others well (Chapter 7, page ) has a part on noticing when you are running on empty.

I feel like I don’t quite belong anywhere. Is something wrong with me?

No. Feeling on the edge of a group is common, especially after a move, a breakup or a change of job. Belonging tends to grow from small, repeated contact with the same few people. Attachment, trust and belonging (Chapter 4, page ) and Groups, influence and culture (Chapter 6, page ) both have ideas to try.

zqglossaryqz

Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Calm-Down Kit
The Calm-Down Kit is your own short list of what settles your body, chosen ahead of time so you don’t have to think when you’re wound up.
Carrier wave
The carrier wave is the feeling that travels underneath the words: the tone that arrives before the meaning does.
Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It isn’t laziness or a lack of love. It’s a setup nobody has looked at lately.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The ladder
Three everyday states: calm and connected, revved up, running on empty. My weather today calls them clear, gusty and fogged in. A best guess about your state, not a measurement.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Person A and Person B
Person A and Person B are simply the two people on a two-person worksheet or tool. They are labels, not a ranking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
Retune
To retune is to notice you’ve fallen out of step with someone and adjust together: slower, softer, or at a better time.
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 to 0.59 moderate, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Talk window
The talk window says whether now is a good time for a big conversation: open, wait a little, or closed for today.
Unbilled debt
Unbilled debt is the work of a shared life that got done but was never noticed or thanked.
Wired differently
Being wired differently means your brain works in a less common way, such as being autistic, ADHD, dyslexic or highly sensitive, which changes how words land.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill in on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
