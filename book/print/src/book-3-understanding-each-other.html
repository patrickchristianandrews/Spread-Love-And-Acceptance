Five big ideas

Five ideas run under the whole series.

  • See the whole load. Count the unseen work too, the noticing, remembering and planning, yours and other people’s.
  • Fix the setup, not the person. When something keeps going wrong, change the arrangement around it before you blame anyone.
  • Read your state first. How full your day already is changes how every word sounds, so check yourself before you react.
  • Tune how you send and receive. People differ in pace, tone and wiring, and a mismatch is something to translate, never a fault.
  • Notice the quiet incentives. Jobs nobody owns drift to whoever notices first, and a named owner and a real thank-you stop the drift.

Why we get out of tune: communication at home

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

At a glance

  • A clash is often about how things were said. Pace, tone and urgency can be out of step even when both of you mean well.
  • Nobody has to be wrong. Say that you are out of tune, pause with a time to come back, and slow down.

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.”

“I know. I’m just asking when.”

“Why does it matter when?”

“It doesn’t, I just…”

Four sentences later nobody is talking about the trash. Somebody has brought up last Christmas. Neither of you could point to the moment it turned.

The radio picture in this chapter is my own. When two people who care about each other keep missing, what’s usually lacking is timing and a shared way of putting things, and this chapter is an attempt at the shared way.

Quickly: the trash is the surface. Underneath, two different paces, one fast and one slow. Underneath that, two bodies in two different states at the end of a long day. The roots are signal and noise, and state and setting. Then slowly, one knob at a time.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something perfectly reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. Think of two radios slightly out of tune with each other. Together they make a crackle neither of them was making on its own.

    “Is anyone using the oven tonight?” Lisa asked. Simple question. She had a lasagna in mind. Her roommate went pink and said, “Why, did I leave a mess?” and there was a pause in which the baking pan from Sunday, still soaking, became the only thing in the room. “I meant the lasagna,” Lisa said eventually. “I know,” said the roommate, who hadn’t known, and went and washed the tray.

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on completely different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they’re close but not quite matched, which is why people who know each other well still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we’re close to, because we assume they already understand.

    Two sisters who are on the phone every single day argue about vacation plans far more than either of them ever does with the cousins they see once a year, at baptisms and funerals. The cousins get things spelled out. The sisters get shorthand, half a sentence, “you know what I mean.” Quite often they don’t.

    With someone close to you, check one meaning today. “Just so I’ve got this right, you mean…?” It feels a bit formal, which is rather the point.

    Dig deeper: why close can be noisy

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    Pace is one, and tone is another, as when a plain update lands as a complaint. The third is urgency, since “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and some time before Easter to another. Texts lose most of their tone, so urgency is especially easy to misread there.

    We think our tone travels Across five experiments, people writing emails overestimated how well readers would pick up their tone, sarcasm or seriousness, largely because they couldn’t hear their own message without their own voice in it.

    Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005) · Strong: several experiments, widely cited

    Between meetings, Vikram texted his business partner two words. Need to talk. She read it as a crisis and lay awake until two in the morning running through every disaster available, including the bank, the landlord and a client she’d always slightly distrusted. At nine he called. It was about the printer. She has since asked him, politely and also not entirely politely, to add a time and the word “printer.”

    Ways to say it “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you’re tense you may talk faster and hear a pause as a snub. When you’re tired and low, your replies get slower and shorter. My weather today, a one-minute check, gives you a best guess at which state you’re in. People’s ordinary rhythms differ too, before stress even arrives, and Rhythms side by side lets you compare whose engine runs fast in the morning and whose at night.

    Barry comes in off a night shift at the depot and his son asks him about Saturday. He gets “Fine” and “Yep” and “Later.” His son decides, on the spot, that his dad doesn’t care about Saturday or, by extension, him. Barry is simply running on empty after ten hours of forklifts and a forty-minute drive home with the window down to stay awake. He’d have told you so if he’d had any words left. He went to bed.

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work perfectly will still crackle if they’re slightly off, and arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning will, which just means getting back in step.

    On the doorstep, coat still on, Sylvia tells her grown-up son he looks tired. She means it as care. He hears “you’re not coping,” which is what he has been privately worrying about all week, and says “Thanks, Mom” in a voice that could strip paint. Both of them are right about what they meant and what they heard. That’s exactly why arguing about it goes nowhere. They manage to get through the visit, just, and both feel bad about it on the way home.

    You could say “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say out loud that you think you’re out of sync, and check how tense you are. Either of you can call a pause, and whoever pauses names a time to come back. Slow down, take shorter turns, and only then go back to the actual topic.

    Two students sharing a room in the dorms agreed a pause word one calm Sunday. They picked “static,” mainly because it made them laugh. Three weeks later, at one in the morning, in the middle of a fight about someone’s music, one of them said it, through gritted teeth. They stopped. Both lay there fuming in the dark. They came back to it at nine the next evening, as agreed, and worked it out in ten minutes with headphones, a second set of headphones and a slightly sheepish package of cookies.

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    The first three minutes In 124 newlywed couples, how the first three minutes of a disagreement went predicted which couples had divorced six years later. It’s one lab’s sample and not a crystal ball, but it’s a good argument for opening gently.

    Carrère and Gottman (1999) · Moderate: one research group’s sample; striking, not widely replicated as a predictor

    When you go back to the topic, say what happened, how you feel and what you’d like, in that order and in one go. “The trash goes out early on Wednesday. I get anxious when they’re late. Could you put them out tonight?” Or, from one co-parent to the other, on a Sunday evening, “The swimsuit wasn’t in the bag and I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?” Write one about something small before you need it, so the shape is in your hand when you do.

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in, makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big fight. Habit research suggests small routines at a regular time are easier to keep than big ones done rarely. The quiet, easy stretches are the ones that drift most, so keep it going even when nothing seems wrong (see Complacency, spreadloveandacceptance.com/complacency).

    Orla is in Chicago and Marc is in Toronto. Every morning each of them sends one 90-second voice note, usually something like “Trash went out, my boss is still weird, I miss you.” On the weeks they forget, they notice it by Thursday, usually as an odd phone call about nothing in particular that leaves them both slightly put out.

Your part, and only your part. You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back. Saturday, 10am

“The spare room needs cleaning out at some point.”

She meant this month. He heard today, has already canceled soccer in his head and is quietly furious about it. By lunchtime you’re both upset about a room nobody has touched. When you finally stop and ask each other what “at some point” meant, it turns out you still don’t agree.

zqaxchapterx1xxfaithxcultureqzFaith, culture and family voices

For many couples, faith is where they turn first, and a shared faith can be a real help for many couples in staying in tune. Praying together, or a quiet moment after worship, can serve as a daily check-in. Some couples each pray on their own before they come back to a hard talk, with the pause still ending at a named time. Someone in your community you both trust can be a good first person to talk to.

Emeka and Ngozi, married twenty-six years, do their monthly look-back on a Sunday after church, over lunch, with the radio off. When a talk gets heated, either of them can say, “Let’s pray on it and come back at eight,” and at eight they do. Emeka says this works about nine times in ten. Ngozi says eight.

Culture shapes how you talk, too. In many families, respect for elders decides who can speak plainly to whom, and one habit helps a great deal. Each of you speaks to your own parents and relatives, once you’ve agreed the message together.

Words to try “Can we pray about this and talk again tonight?” “Mom, we love you, and we’ve thought about it carefully. This year we’ll visit in August instead of at Easter.”

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace and your own sense of urgency, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. Learn your early signs of drifting. A clipped reply is a common one. So is a sudden urge to win. Learn how you take words in best and say it, once (“I hear things better in writing”). When you drift, step out, settle, come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission for that. Hamid lives alone and has noticed that his texts come out curt when he’s tired, so he now waits until morning to answer anything that matters (more in On my own, spreadloveandacceptance.com/on-my-own).

One example, followed through

Cerys, Marisol and Dougie share a house and a car. Cerys and Marisol talk fast, while Dougie speaks slowly and thinks before he answers. At house meetings he kept getting talked over, and for a while the other two took his silence as agreement. It came out in March, when he said, quite mildly, that he had never actually agreed to the new car schedule. Marisol said “Since when?” and Dougie said “Since always,” and there was a long, uncomfortable pause. They tried a simple change, a round where each person speaks once before anyone answers. It felt stilted, and Cerys kept wanting to jump in and sat on her hands, literally. By the third meeting Dougie was saying more, and the schedule he suggested is the one they use. Some evenings it still turns into two fast talkers and one quiet one, until somebody says “round” and they reset. Help by relationship has pages for many other kinds of shared life.

Two ways to read this

Out of tune, both ways

Both directions Some people think by talking and need the answer right away. Others think first and talk second, and a quick reply asked for too soon comes out wrong. If you talk to think, the slower person’s pause isn’t agreement, and it isn’t a protest. It’s thinking. If you think first, the fast talker isn’t trying to win. Out loud is where their thinking happens.

Where the fields meet Music has a name for a good conversation: call and response, one phrase answering another at a tempo both can keep. Neurobiology explains why the tempo slips, since your state sets the volume, and a stressed body changes station without asking you.

Common mix-ups

Retuning isn’t giving in, because you still come back to the topic, at a time when you can hear each other. Being out of sync doesn’t mean one of you is wrong, either. Two fair points in two different tones will still clash. And a pause never means the topic is dropped, since a pause comes with a time to come back, or it isn’t a pause.

To try it, agree a shared phrase while things are calm (“I think we’re out of tune” does fine) so that it works as a signal when you need it. For a week, each of you note one moment you felt out of step, and on the weekend compare whether it was mostly pace, tone or urgency. Rewind the talk, Say it so it lands and the 90-second daily check-in are free on the site.

A pause is for both of you. If one person is never allowed to pause, or a “pause” is used to punish with days of silence, that isn’t retuning.

Thursday, 8:15am

Ana texts her brother. Can you call me when you’re free?

She means no rush, small question about the weekend. He reads it at the back of the 8:10 bus, and for eleven stops, past the hospital, which doesn’t help, he works out who is ill.

Small habits help a message arrive. Listening, checking, starting softly, mending quickly. A few ready-made lines make small talk easier for anyone who dreads it. And three tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. Say it so it lands (Book 4, Chapter 4, page 73) asks for what happened, how you feel and one clear request, because every message carries a second message about the relationship. The 90-second daily check-in (Chapter 1, page ) keeps repairs small and frequent. And the Chat analyzer’s whole-conversation view shows where a written conversation lost its tone.

A text with no kisses, in quick strokes. On the surface, three words. Underneath, the tone the reader added. Underneath that, what it seemed to say about the two of you. The roots are signal and noise, and feedback loops, since every reply changes the next one. The chapter slows down from here.

  1. Meaning is made between two people.

    Words don’t carry meaning on their own. The speaker has a meaning in mind, and the listener builds their own from the words, the tone, the timing and everything they already expect. Most of the time the two match well enough. Sometimes they don’t, and neither person can see the gap from where they’re standing.

    “Let’s chat tomorrow,” the manager wrote to the new hire at 4:50, meaning a friendly catch-up and possibly a cookie. The new hire went home and reread his last three emails looking for the mistake. He found two, neither of which existed. In the morning there was a cookie, which he couldn’t face. The sentence that would have saved him an evening is a dull one, “I think what you meant and what I heard might be two different things. Can we check?”

    Dig deeper: how meaning travels

  2. Every message says two things.

    There is what you say, and there is what it says about the two of you. “You’re late” is a fact about time, though it can also mean “I was worried” or “I don’t matter to you.” People argue about the fact when the hurt is in the second layer.

    I take one nine-word sentence apart on the website’s About page: “don’t worry about it, I’ll take care of it.” Said kindly, in passing, it’s reassurance. Said for the third time that month about the same job, it also says a job has no owner and a decision has been put off again, and both of those are in the same nine words.

    Content and relationship Watzlawick and colleagues made this one of their axioms of communication: every message has a content level and a relationship level, and the relationship level often decides how the content is heard. It’s a framework rather than an experiment, and a remarkably durable one.

    Watzlawick, Beavin and Jackson (1967) · Theory: influential in communication and family therapy

    Or take Tomasz, who wants to know whether his roommate used his good pan. On the surface it’s a question about a pan. Underneath, it’s a question about whether his things are respected in this house, and no amount of talk about the pan is going to settle that. “Honestly,” he said in the end, “I don’t care about the pan.” His roommate, who had in fact used the pan, washed it and put it back in the wrong cupboard, looked baffled. “Then why have we been talking about it for ten minutes?”

    Dig deeper: the two layers of a message

  3. Listening is something you do.

    Hearing happens by itself. Listening is a choice, and most of us do a lot of it partly in order to reply. To fix, or defend, or advise, or tell our own story. Real listening means following what the other person is trying to say, and letting them know you got it, before you add anything of yours.

    “School is pointless,” Mollie announced, thirteen, coat still on. Her dad started explaining, at some length and with examples, why it wasn’t. She stopped talking and went upstairs, and the door shut harder than it strictly needed to. The next time she said it, a week later, he bit his tongue, hard. “Pointless how?” he said. “Tell me about today.” She talked for twenty minutes, mostly about a substitute teacher and a seating chart, and he said almost nothing, which nearly killed him.

    Dig deeper: what good listening is

  4. Check, don’t guess.

    Once we know something it’s very hard to imagine not knowing it, so we leave out steps, assume the other person can tell what we mean, and guess what they meant instead of asking. We do it most with the people we know best, which is unfortunate, because they’re the ones we most want to get it right with. A short check costs you a sentence, whereas a wrong guess can cost a whole evening.

    Closeness makes us lazy listeners In three small experiments, people overestimated how well they got through to friends and spouses even more than to strangers. Closeness seemed to switch off the checking we do without thinking with people we don’t know.

    Savitsky and colleagues (2011) · Moderate: three small experiments, extended in later work

    “Something low-key,” they agreed, for Fliss’s fortieth. Fliss pictured a Chinese takeout on the couch, in pajamas, with the good crackers. Her best friend booked a quiet table at the Italian, ironed a shirt, and turned up at seven with flowers. Fliss opened the door in her pajamas, and for a second they just looked at each other, both disappointed, both having thought it was obvious. They ate the takeout in the end, the friend in her ironed shirt, and it was a good night, eventually.

  5. Hard things land better when they start soft.

    How a hard conversation starts often decides how it ends. A complaint about one thing (“The trash wasn’t taken out”) is easier to hear than a criticism of the person (“You never help”). A simple shape works for most hard things, which is to say what happened, how you feel and what you would like.

    A woman wants her eighty-one-year-old father to stop driving at night. She has “You’re not safe anymore” on the tip of her tongue. She leaves it there and says, “You had two near-misses last month and I lie awake worrying. Could I drive us in the evenings?” He says he’ll think about it, which in his language is nearly a yes.

    Dig deeper: saying hard things kindly

  6. Mix-ups are normal. Repair is the skill.

    Even close, kind people misunderstand each other constantly, several times a day if you’re counting, and what keeps a relationship steady is noticing it and fixing it rather than never doing it. “That came out wrong.” “Can we start again?” Small repairs made often build trust much faster than big apologies made rarely.

    “Excuse me!” a woman snaps in the supermarket, because your cart is blocking the cans. “I’m moving,” you snap back. A second later you hear yourself add, “Sorry. Long day.” And she sags a bit and says hers has been too, and somehow you end up talking about the price of coffee for five minutes by the chopped tomatoes.

  7. Messages lose their tone on the way.

    A text or email carries the words without your face or your voice, so the reader fills in the tone with however they feel right now. A tired reader hears a tired, irritated message. Short replies, missing greetings and late-night timing all get read as meaning something.

    The period problem In one study with students, short text replies ending in a period were rated less sincere than the same replies without one, while handwritten notes showed no such effect. Later work suggests it fades with longer messages, so this one is early days.

    Gunraj and colleagues (2016) · Early: one study with students

Within four minutes two other parents have privately decided she’s furious and started composing careful messages. She was stirring a pan of soup, typing with one thumb, and genuinely thought it was fine.

    Before you send your next tricky message, read it out loud in the grumpiest voice you can manage. If it sounds harsh, add a warm word. This is my favorite test in the whole book, and it takes four seconds.

  8. Different styles aren’t wrong styles.

    Some people say exactly what they mean. Others hint, and expect you to read between the lines. Some talk fast and overlap, and others need a pause to think. Families and cultures and different kinds of mind all shape this, and a clash between two styles is usually a translation problem, with nobody being difficult on purpose.

    “Is the report ready?” “No. I need two more days.” Her manager heard that as rude, for about a month, until he decided to ask what she meant, over a coffee he didn’t want. She said she’d rather tell him the truth plainly than make him guess, and that where she grew up a straight answer was the polite one. After that he started finding her clarity a relief, and then, slightly to his surprise, a model.

    Words to try “I’m quite direct, so if I sound blunt, it doesn’t mean I’m upset.” “It helps me if you say exactly what you need.”

  9. Small talk has a job, and it can be short.

    Small talk (“Cold today, isn’t it?” “Busy weekend?”) isn’t really about the weather. Its job is to say that you see the other person and that the two of you are okay. In 1923 the anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski called this “phatic communion,” talk whose main purpose is bonding more than information. Small moments like this matter more than people expect. In one small field study, Sandstrom and Dunn (2014) found that people who had a short, friendly exchange with a coffee-shop barista felt more belonging and a better mood than people who kept it purely efficient.

    Small talk is hard work for many people, though, including many autistic people, shy or anxious people and people speaking a second language. The rules are unwritten, and it uses energy that some people would rather keep. A few ready-made lines, sometimes called social scripts, take the guesswork out, and keeping it short is allowed. For what it’s worth, this is the part I had to learn on purpose.

    Phil dreads school pickup. He has three lines ready, a “Morning! How’s your week going?” an “Oh, nice” and a “Right, I’d better get going, see you Friday.” Some mornings he still sits in the car with the radio on until the bell rings. Most days he gets through it. One of the other dads now saves him a spot by the fence, and has never once asked him how his week is going, which Phil appreciates more than he can say.

    You could say “Morning! How’s your week been?” “How do you know Nico?” “Oh, how was that?” “Great to see you. I’d better get going.” And to opt out kindly, “I’m not great at chit-chat, but it’s really nice to see you.”

Two ways to read this

Hinting instead of asking

From outside “It’s a bit cold in here,” and then annoyance when nobody closes the window.

The logic A direct request can sound like an order. A hint offers the idea without pressure. The other person can take it up as if it were their own idea, or let it pass. Nobody has to give an open no.

It’s like Leaving a door open for someone, rather than pushing them through it.

Why it fits Indirect speech lets people ask while both sides keep face.

The other side Hints get missed, and then both people feel blamed.

You could say “When I hint, I’m trying to be gentle.”

One small step Add the ask to the hint. “It’s cold. Could you close the window?”

Both directions For some people, “Fine.” with a period is just fine. For others it reads as a door being shut. Some people say exactly what they mean and hear exactly what’s said. Others wrap a request in a hint and expect it to be found. If you’re literal, the hint is a real request in soft packaging. If you hint, the literal person isn’t ignoring you. They need the ask said out loud.

In everyday life

On your own: how you talk to yourself

You send yourself messages all day, from “Typical, I always mess this up” to “That was hard and I handled it.” The same things apply. Notice the tone, check whether it’s fair, and mend it when it’s harsh. Catch one harsh sentence you say to yourself today and rewrite it the way you’d say it to someone you like.

Going quiet can mean very different things.

When someone stops talking in an argument, it might be a shutdown, a need for space or the silent treatment, and from outside they can look the same. A shutdown happens when a person is flooded or overloaded and words go offline for a while. Autistic young people in Phung and colleagues (2021) described shutdowns as something that happens to them, separate from choosing to withdraw. Needing space is a choice to pause so the talk can go better later.

For something to be the silent treatment, these are usually all present:

  • the silence works as a message or a penalty,
  • it lasts well beyond the argument, often for days,
  • it ends only when the other person gives in or apologizes,
  • there’s no offer of a time to come back, and it’s often aimed at one person while everyone else gets warmth.

Not enough on its own:

  • going quiet when flooded or overloaded,
  • asking for a break, even an abrupt one,
  • needing time to think before speaking,
  • a quieter person’s normal pace.

One pattern is very common and nobody’s villainy. One person pushes to talk and the other pulls back, and each move makes the other stronger. A large review by Schrodt and colleagues (2014) linked this push-and-pull pattern with less satisfaction for both people, whichever role they played. If you’re the quiet one, a short line keeps the door open: “I can’t talk yet. I’m not punishing you. Give me an hour.” If you’re waiting, “Take your time. Can you tell me when?” asks for a time without putting a label on the pause.

When the past joins the conversation

Sometimes the size of a reaction belongs to an older moment. A raised voice can sound like a parent’s, and a closed door can feel like being shut out again. From outside you can’t know someone’s history, and guessing out loud (“This is about your dad”) usually makes things worse. What you can notice is the shape. The reaction is much bigger than the moment, the voice or face changes suddenly, or the person wants to leave or freezes.

You can’t calm someone down by telling them to calm down. You can change what you bring to the moment, and all of these are within your control:

  • Your pace. Slow down and use fewer words.
  • Your tone. Lower your voice and soften the volume.
  • Your body. Sit down if you’re standing, unclench your hands, and breathe out slowly. Slow breathing tends to calm the body (Zaccaro and colleagues, 2018 · Moderate evidence).
  • Space. Give the other person room, and don’t block a doorway or follow them.
  • Your words. Name what you see without blame (“This feels big. Let’s slow down.”). Putting feelings into words can turn them down a notch (Lieberman and colleagues, 2007 · Moderate evidence).
  • Timing. Agree to come back at a set time rather than pushing on.
  • Stepping away and coming back. “I’m going to step out for twenty minutes, and I’ll come back to this at seven.” Then come back at seven.

The same list works when the big reaction is yours.

Common mix-ups

“If they loved me, they’d just know” is the big one. Closeness makes us check less without making us understand any more, so saying it clearly is a kindness, and no test of love. Listening isn’t agreeing, either, because you can understand someone completely and still see it differently, and the understanding comes first. A repair doesn’t mean you’re confessing to being the bad one. It just says that didn’t go the way you wanted, and either person can make one. And texts feel safer for hard things because they’re easier to send. They’re also much easier to misread, so for anything tender, add warmth, or say it out loud.

If you want to help someone else with this

Maybe a friend keeps having the same argument with their partner, or your teenager feels nobody listens. You can’t talk for them. You can be the one person who really hears them, without fixing, which is often the most useful thing anyone does all week. You can help them pull the two layers apart, by asking what it was about and then what it seemed to say about the two of them. You can offer the simple shape (what happened, how they feel, what they’d like) and let them find their own words. And stay out of judging the other person’s character, since you only hear one side and they may make up next week. Notice if it’s more than a mix-up.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

In everyday life

We tell stories about ourselves too, and “I always mess things up” is usually built from a few bright memories that leave a lot out. After years together, partners stop checking and start assuming. And “I know what you meant” can do a lot of quiet damage in a long relationship. Children get labeled early, the difficult one, the easy one, and the label becomes a lens that only sees what fits. With ADHD, attention gets pulled by whatever is new or urgent and can lock on hard, so someone may genuinely not have seen or heard. Get their attention first and put requests in writing. Two memories of one evening, or gaslighting?

Two people often remember the same evening differently. Memory isn’t a recording. Each person noticed and kept different parts, and stress, tiredness and strong feelings blur it further, so most “That’s not what happened!” arguments are two honest memories. Gaslighting describes something less common than the word suggests, and the word does real harm when it’s used for an ordinary disagreement.

For something to be gaslighting, these are usually all present:

  • the same person denies, again and again, things the other person saw, heard or knows happened,
  • the denial goes on even when there is something to check, such as a message, a photo or someone else who was there, or it is always about things nobody else could check,
  • the other person ends up doubting their own memory or judgment in general,
  • it tends to end with the denier winning, avoiding blame or getting their way.

Not enough on its own:

  • two people remembering it differently,
  • someone misremembering, especially when tired, stressed or upset,
  • disagreeing about what something meant,
  • one heated “That never happened!” in the middle of a fight, or someone saying afterward, “I don’t think I meant it like that.”

Ask whether it happens in both directions, and, when there is a message or a calendar, whether the conversation settles when you look at it. Then talk about what each of you remembers rather than the word itself. “I remember you saying Saturday. You remember Sunday. Can we check the message and plan from there?” keeps both memories in the room.

Common mix-ups

Nobody sees things exactly as they are. Everyone sees through a lens, and knowing that is where fairness starts. Memory isn’t a recording, either, since it gets rebuilt each time, so two honest people can remember the same evening differently and both be telling the truth. Giving someone the benefit of the doubt doesn’t mean ignoring what happened. It means checking the story before you decide, and if something hurtful keeps happening, that matters. And a face or a tone is a clue to what someone meant. Faces and tones vary a lot between people and between cultures, so a face is a weaker clue than it feels.

If you want to help someone else with this

Start with how they feel, and make sense of the feeling first, and only then wonder aloud whether there might be another way to see it. Offer information instead of verdicts. “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.” Check your own guesses out loud (“I assumed he was annoyed, I’ll ask”), which shows how it’s done. Be careful with labels for children and colleagues, because they stick and then shape what everyone sees. And believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone that what happened to them didn’t happen.

Words to try “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”

Kai sent his friend a long message at lunchtime. Four paragraphs about the new job, and a question at the end. It says Read 12:47, and there’s nothing after that.

By dinnertime he’s gone from “she’s busy” to “she’s annoyed.” By nine thirty he’s wondering whether anybody really likes him at all. The friend, meanwhile, is at her nephew’s sixth birthday at an indoor play place in Columbus, and her phone is in her coat pocket under nineteen other coats.

The monthly look-back (Book 4, Chapter 5, page 97) looks at the month and keeps both people from feeling blamed, because feeling safe is what lets anyone say what went wrong.

Quickly, then slowly. On the surface, a message that hasn’t been answered. Underneath, an old expectation about whether people come back. Underneath that, a body that has been on its own too much. The roots are state and setting, and feedback loops, since trust grows in small repeated moments. The chapter takes them in order.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That hurt works like hunger, pushing you to reach for someone.

    Friendship takes hours Studies of adults who had recently moved found that more time together, especially relaxed time, went with moving from acquaintance to friend. The hour counts are rough. The message isn’t: friendship is mostly built out of time spent.

    Hall (2019) · Moderate: survey studies; the hour estimates are rough

    Rhea moved to Pittsburgh for work. She’s busy and doing well and likes the job. And by the third month she’s low and teary every Sunday afternoon, when the office is shut and nobody in the whole city knows her well enough to call. She found this embarrassing for a while, and told her mom on the phone that she was “just tired.” Then she joined a running club, which she hated, and a bar trivia team, which she loved.

    Dig deeper: why we need each other

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children we learn, from the people who looked after us, whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder, or stop asking. These lessons are a starting point that later relationships can change. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    Watch a toddler at the park. He runs off toward the ducks, then runs back to check his grandma is still on the bench, then off again, a bit further, and back, and further still, right to the edge of the pond, where he stands and shouts at a goose. He gets braver every time, because he knows exactly where she is. If you’re the one on the bench, “I’m right here, go and look, come back whenever you want” is more or less the whole job.

    Dig deeper: what attachment is

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close and need more room. Many of us feel mostly secure. Most of us sit somewhere in between, and where we sit can shift with the day and the person.

    These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they work as sliding scales.

    When things get tense, Ronan wants to talk it through, now, immediately. His wife Clara needs to go quiet first, usually in the yard, usually with the pruning shears. He reads her quiet as her leaving, and she reads his questions through the back door as pressure. It took them nine years to see it. Now she says “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and he waits, which for about the first month he found almost unbearable.

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust rarely comes from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again, like doing what you said, and being the same person on a hard day as on a good one.

    The new hire said she’d send the figures by Thursday, and she did. The next week she warned on Monday that Friday’s would be late. By the third month her manager trusted her completely, and couldn’t say when that began.

    Dig deeper: how trust grows

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle the nervous system. In one small study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. It works both ways, so we can be that for other people, often without saying much.

    After his panic attack, Kieran’s friend didn’t try to fix anything. He sat down next to him on the cold step outside the apartments. “Take your time. I’m here.” Then nothing else, for about twenty minutes, while a man walked past twice with a very small dog. Eventually Kieran’s breathing slowed, and he noticed how cold the step was. “Thanks,” he said. “Any time,” said his friend, and meant it.

    Notice who helps you feel calmer, and tell them. “I always feel better after seeing you” is a lovely thing to hear, and almost nobody says it.

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Teams, families, clubs and communities can offer either. Being left out of a group hurts even when the group doesn’t matter much to us, and real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    At her new job Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny. She orders a club soda with lime “for the drive,” every Friday, so nobody asks why she doesn’t drink. She goes home wiped out. At her book club, in the back room of the library on alternate Tuesdays, she says exactly what she thought of the book, which last month was “I hated every single character,” and someone said, “Oh, thank God,” and they talked till the custodian turned the lights off.

    You could say (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it okay if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It comes back slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    At Sunday dinner, for a laugh, Jen told the whole table a story her sister had told her in confidence. Nobody else at the table thought it was a big deal, but her sister left before dessert. The apology, two days later, was a start. What actually rebuilt things was the months after, when the story never came up again, and Jen started checking before she told anyone anything. “Can I mention the thing about work?” “No.” “Okay.”

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can seem a long way off. Belonging can start far smaller than that. A chat with a neighbor. A regular class. An online group about something you love. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    We underestimate strangers Commuters asked to talk to a stranger on their train or bus had a better journey than those told to keep to themselves, even though most people predicted the opposite. Misjudging how pleasant it will be is part of what keeps people apart.

    Epley and Schroeder (2014) · Moderate: field and lab experiments, replicated since

    After his divorce, Martin started saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall outside the station. It was the weather, at first. A few weeks later they were talking football every morning, mostly about the Eagles’ defense, which they agree about entirely. It’s a small friendship, and it makes Mondays bearable. Small rituals like that are worth keeping going on purpose, and Keep your little rituals alive helps you notice the ones you already have.

Common mix-ups

“Needing people means I’m needy” is the one people say most quietly. Everyone needs connection, including the people who hide it well. Attachment style isn’t fixed for life, either, and patterns can change, especially through steady, kind relationships and a bit of reflection. And forgiving isn’t the same as trusting again right away. Trust can return slowly, or not at all, and that’s your call.

  • “Jealousy and checking up show how much they love me.” Jealousy is a common feeling, and asking for reassurance (“Are we okay?”) is ordinary, especially for people who feel rejection keenly, which Downey and Feldman (1996) called rejection sensitivity. A worried text is a feeling looking for comfort. Checking someone’s phone without asking, needing to know where they are all day, or deciding who they can see is a different thing, whatever feeling sits underneath it. The question to ask is whether the request leaves the other person free to say no.

If you want to help someone else with this

Be steady, and turn up when you said you would, which matters more than any big gesture. If someone says they feel alone, listen before you remind them how many friends they have. Invite, and keep inviting. People who feel left out may say no a few times, and a warm invitation with no pressure still counts every time. In groups, notice who’s on the edge and ask what they think. And leave the labels out. Telling someone they’re “so avoidant” or “so clingy” has never helped anyone, as far as I can tell. “What would help?” often does.

You could say “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” “We’re having a few people over on Friday. No pressure, but I’d love you to come.”

Eleven years in this house with Charlie. Tonight you’re both on the couch, both on your phones, with a cooking show on in the background that neither of you is watching. Someone on it is crying about a soufflé.

You try to remember the last time the two of you talked about something that wasn’t bills, or the water heater, or whose turn it is to cook. You can’t. You love Charlie, which isn’t in question. You feel oddly on your own, a foot away from them.

This chapter is about closeness in all its forms, far beyond romance. It looks at what helps people feel near to each other, and why closeness fades and how it grows back, and it builds on Chapter 4 (page ), on attachment and trust.

Two people on a sofa, scrolling, in quick strokes. The surface is a quiet evening. Underneath, a closeness that has thinned without anyone deciding it should. Underneath that, months of small bids missed on both sides. The roots are balance and feedback loops. The chapter goes back over them slowly.

  1. Love comes in many forms.

    The ancient Greeks had several words for love, for friends, family, romantic partners and strangers. People love in many ways now too, through lifelong friendships, partners of any gender, more than one partner, chosen family, and among people who are asexual or aromantic and build deep closeness without romance or sex. Some people on their own pour love into friends, family, pets, work and community. A life can be full of love without a partner in it.

    Frances is fifty-six and has never wanted a partner. Ask her about her great love story and she’ll tell you about Denise, her best friend of thirty years. Between them there have been two house moves, one cancer scare, a camping trip in Cape Cod that they still can’t discuss without one of them leaving the room, and more Sunday phone calls than either of them could count. “People feel sorry for me,” she says. “I feel quite sorry for them.”

    Dig deeper: the many forms of love

  2. Closeness grows from being known.

    Intimacy is about much more than touch or romance. At its heart is being known. The psychologists Reis and Shaver described it as a back-and-forth, in which one person shares something real about themselves and the other responds in a way that leaves them feeling understood and cared for. In a diary study by Laurenceau, Barrett and Pietromonaco (1998), feeling that the other person really responded was a big part of what made everyday conversations feel intimate.

    Two brothers who mostly talk about football were driving to their dad’s nursing home. Somewhere on the highway, staring straight ahead, one of them said, “I find these visits really hard.” There was a long pause before the other said, “Yeah. Me too. I always feel guilty on the way home.” It was the longest conversation they’d had in years. It lasted all the way to the parking lot, where they sat with the engine off for another ten minutes. Then they went in and talked about football.

    Dig deeper: intimacy as being known

  3. Closeness is built a step at a time.

    People usually get close by sharing a little more, then a little more, when it feels safe. In a well-known study by Aron and colleagues (1997), pairs of strangers who took turns asking gradually more personal questions, now known as the “36 questions,” felt closer after forty-five minutes than pairs who made small talk. The same idea works in friendships and long relationships, where closeness grows when both people take small, matched steps.

    Two new neighbors start with the weather, and the parking, which on their street is a serious subject. A few months later one asks how the other’s mom is getting on. Some time after that, over the fence, with a garden hose running, one of them says, “Can I tell you what’s actually been going on?”

    Dig deeper: building closeness step by step

  4. Feeling understood matters most.

    What makes someone feel loved is often less about grand gestures and more about feeling understood. Researchers call this responsiveness, meaning noticing, understanding and caring about what matters to the other person, and it shows in small moments such as how you react to good news. Gable and colleagues (2004) found that people whose partners usually met their good news with real interest and warmth felt closer and more satisfied together.

    “I got into the band.” She’s fourteen, coat still on, trying to sound casual about it. Her grandpa put the paper down, properly down, folded, and said, “Which instrument? When’s the first concert?” She talked for twenty minutes, mostly about the trombone section and a boy called Luca who can’t count. Next time someone shares good news with you, put your phone face down and ask two follow-up questions.

    Dig deeper: responsiveness

  5. Old maps show up in love.

    The patterns from Chapter 4 (page ) often come out strongest with the people we love most. Hazan and Shaver (1987) suggested that adult love uses the same attachment system as childhood bonds. When closeness feels shaky, some people reach harder for reassurance and others pull back to feel safe. Both are ways of protecting something precious.

    When his partner goes quiet, Leo texts, and once it was five times in an hour. His partner goes quiet to calm down, and five texts feels like being chased around the house. What they agreed, eventually, was that his partner would say, “I need an hour, and I’ll message you at eight.” Leo found, somewhat to his surprise, that he could wait quite happily for a known time, and that the waiting had never really been the problem.

  6. Love changes over time.

    Early love is often intense, with racing thoughts and a lot of longing, and over the years it usually settles into something calmer and deeper. A review by Acevedo and Aron (2009) found that some people in long relationships still report strong romantic love, without the anxious, all-consuming edge of early love, and that this was linked to being happier together.

    Thank-yous as a booster In a daily diary study of couples living together, feeling grateful for a partner’s thoughtful act predicted feeling more connected and more satisfied the next day, for both of them. Correlational, but cheap to test at home.

    Algoe, Gable and Maisel (2010) · Moderate: a daily diary study; correlational

    In experiments by Aron and colleagues (2000), couples who tried a new, mildly challenging activity together felt better about their relationship afterward than couples given a dull task. After twenty-five years together, Howard and Lynne signed up for a pottery class at the community center. They were both dreadful. Lynne’s bowl collapsed twice and Howard got clay in his ear. They laughed most of the evening and brought home a bowl that won’t stand up, and on the walk back Howard said, “That felt like a date.” Something new matters more than something big. A new walk. A new recipe. A café in the next town.

  7. Love at every age.

    Love looks different across a life. Teenagers’ first loves can be huge and real. Adults juggle love with work and children and, often, aging parents. Many older people choose to spend their time with fewer, closer people, which Carstensen and colleagues (1999) linked to feeling that time is more limited, so that the most meaningful relationships matter more. People find new love at seventy and eighty, and people lose great loves and carry them always.

    Arthur is seventy-eight, and he met Irene at his walking group, in the drizzle, arguing about a trail map.   8. Love is something you do.

    Feelings of love come and go. The actions of love can be steady. Turning toward someone when they speak. Keeping small promises. Saying thank you, and sorry. Making room for who they are. Physical closeness, like a hug or holding hands, matters a lot to some people and less to others, and sex is part of closeness in some relationships and not in others. What matters is that both people feel comfortable and free to say yes or no.

Common mix-ups

The spark fading doesn’t mean love is over. Early intensity usually settles, and many long relationships stay warm and close and lively for decades. Nobody reads minds, either, however much they love you, so saying what helps is a gift rather than a failure of romance.

  • “A boundary is just an ultimatum with a nicer name.” A boundary says what you will do. “If the shouting starts, I’ll leave the room and we can try again later.” An ultimatum tries to decide what the other person does, with a threat attached. “Stop seeing your friends or I’m leaving.” Both can be said calmly and both can be said in anger. Ask whether it’s about your own actions or theirs, and whether you’d accept the same rule turned around.
  • “Jealousy proves love.” Feeling jealous is human, and saying so (“I felt left out when you went without me”) is honest. Jealousy turns into something else when it becomes checking up, rules about who someone can see, or coldness as a penalty for time spent apart.

The family group chat is going. Uncle Trevor has posted a joke about your cousin’s new partner, and the laughing faces are stacking up underneath it. Six, then seven, and your mom has done one.

You don’t find it funny. You also don’t want to be the one who can’t take a joke. Your thumb is already over the little laughing face.

We all belong to groups, and we need them. Chapter 4 (page ) looks at belonging, at why we need each other and how trust grows. This chapter looks at the other side, at how groups pull on us, why we go along, and how to stay true to yourself while staying connected.

A joke in the family chat, quickly. On the surface, a picture and a laughing face. Underneath, the pull not to be the one who spoils it. Underneath that, a norm nobody wrote down. The roots are quiet incentives and ownership and structure. Then slowly, one pull at a time.

  1. The pull to fit in is old and strong.

    For most of human history, being in a group meant surviving, and being left out still feels alarming. In studies by Williams and colleagues (2000), people who were simply left out of an online ball-throwing game, by players they believed were strangers, felt worse and less as if they belonged. So we bend to stay in. We laugh at the jokes, keep quiet, go along.

    At the warehouse, the new guy gets called “Prof” in his first week, because he wears glasses and once, fatally, used the word “ostensibly.” He hates it. He laughs anyway, every time, because more than anything that first month he wants to be one of them. By Christmas he’s down as Prof on the schedule, and has mostly stopped minding.

    Dig deeper: why being left out hurts

  2. We go along more than we think.

    In Solomon Asch’s famous experiments (Asch, 1956), people judged which of three lines matched another line. When the actors around them all gave the same wrong answer, people went along with the group on about a third of the answers, even though the right answer was obvious.

    Many people never went along at all. Later research, such as a review by Bond and Smith (1996), found the effect is real but varies, smaller in more recent studies and larger in cultures that put the group first.

    Groupthink Irving Janis named the moment a close group’s wish to agree crowds out a realistic look at the options. His evidence was case studies of big policy decisions, so it’s an idea more than a measurement, but two of his remedies travel well: the person in charge holds back their view at first, and someone is asked to argue the other side.

    Janis (1972) · Mixed: an influential idea built on case studies

    Everyone in the meeting loved the new schedule. Bukola could see a hole the size of a bus in the Friday cover, and said nothing, because everyone loved the new schedule. Afterward, waiting for the elevator, two colleagues said quietly that they’d had doubts too. All three of them went back to their desks. The schedule started on the Monday and the Friday cover fell over on the first Friday. Writing your own view down before a group decides, and only then listening, makes it a good deal easier to say it.

    Dig deeper: Asch and what came after

  3. Authority can lead people a long way.

    In Stanley Milgram’s experiments (Milgram, 1963), many ordinary people kept giving what they believed were painful electric shocks to a stranger because a man in a lab coat told them to, although no shocks were real. Burger (2009) repeated part of the study safely and found similar results. The studies are also debated. Later researchers, such as Haslam and Reicher (2012), argue that people went along when they believed in the cause, and many people refused.

    Safe to speak up In 51 teams at one manufacturing company, Amy Edmondson found that a shared sense that it was safe to take interpersonal risks went with more learning, and learning went with better performance. Most later studies are correlational, but the idea has held up widely.

    Edmondson (1999) · Strong: a highly replicated idea, though mostly correlational

    On a night shift, a senior doctor tells a staff nurse to give a dose that looks wrong to her. It’s three in the morning, and he is very senior and very tired. She checks anyway, and then says so, and she’s right. Checking was the loyal thing to do. He said thank you, eventually, and sounded as if he meant it.

    Dig deeper: Milgram and its critics

  4. “Us and them” forms fast.

    Part of how we see ourselves comes from our groups, whether that’s a family, a town, a team, a faith or a job. Tajfel and Turner (1979) called this social identity. It brings pride and loyalty. It also makes us quick to favor “us” and to see “them” as all the same, even when the groups are formed on almost nothing.

    Two branches of one family have always sat apart at weddings, and nobody can quite remember why. Something about a baptism in 1994. At the most recent one a cousin quietly rearranged the seating chart, and the two sides discovered, over the cheese course, a shared and passionate weakness for terrible music. They were the last ones on the dance floor. They still sit apart at funerals, but it’s a start.

    Dig deeper: social identity

  5. Norms are the rules nobody wrote down.

    Every group has norms about what you wear, how you speak, what you laugh at and what you never mention, and we often guess them wrong. In studies at one university, Prentice and Miller (1993) found that most students were privately less comfortable with heavy drinking than they thought everyone else was. Each person thought they were the odd one out, so everyone kept going along.

    In one office everybody answered emails at midnight, because everybody thought it was expected. Then a manager said, quite casually, in a meeting about something else, that she didn’t expect replies after six. You could almost hear the room breathe out. “We have always done it this way” usually means a rule nobody has checked in years, so it’s worth asking when anyone last did.

  6. Cultures differ, and so do people inside them.

    Cultures differ in how much they put the individual or the group first, which researchers call individualism and collectivism (Hofstede, 1980). In some, standing out is admired, and in others fitting in and keeping harmony are signs of maturity. Labels need care, though. A large review by Oyserman and colleagues (2002) found the differences were smaller and less tidy than often claimed, and individuals vary hugely within every culture.

    Anjali calls her mom every day, sometimes twice. Her partner Sean, who was raised to “do his own thing” and calls his parents on birthdays, thought this was clingy. She thought he was cold, and said so, once, in the car. Each of them was following rules they’d learned before they could tie their shoes. It came out properly over a Sunday dinner at her mom’s, when her mom asked Sean when he’d last called his own mother, and he genuinely couldn’t remember.

  7. Every family is a small culture.

    Families have their own norms about how loud people are and how feelings are shown. Some things get talked about and some never do, and there are rules about how you say sorry, if you do. Usually we only notice our family’s rules when we live with someone from a different family. Where your lens came from (Book 1, Chapter 1, page 7) looks at the rules you grew up with.

  9. You can belong and still be yourself.

    Belonging means being accepted as you are, whereas fitting in means changing in order to be accepted (Chapter 4, page , says more). Good groups have room for difference. It helps to know your own values, to have more than one group, and to have at least one ally. In Asch’s experiments, when just one other person gave the right answer, going along with the group dropped sharply.

He gets teased, and he’s also the state junior runner-up, which he mentions more often than strictly necessary.

    Disagree kindly, and early.

If you’re a teenager

Influence, pressure and manipulation.

Groups and people shape each other all the time, and most of it is ordinary. You pick up a team’s jokes, a family’s habits and a friend group’s taste in music, and people who care about you will sometimes try to change your mind. That’s influence, and it happens in the open. “Manipulative” is a much stronger word.

For something to be manipulation, these are usually all present:

  • a request is hidden behind something else, or the information you are given turns out to be false,
  • your feelings, such as guilt, fear or love, are what gets used to get it,
  • it repeats,
  • saying no costs you something, such as your place in the group or warmth from them.

Not enough on its own:

  • persuading you openly, even hard,
  • asking more than once,
  • being disappointed or sad when you say no,
  • hinting instead of asking, which is a normal style in many families and cultures.

A good test is whether you could say “I see it differently” and still be welcome next week. If something feels off, describe the moment rather than the motive: “When you said the others would be let down if I didn’t come, I felt pushed. Can you just ask me straight?”

In everyday life

Your friend is telling you about her awful week. Before she’s finished her first sentence you have five good ideas. You give her all five, in order, with reasons.

You both hang up feeling slightly worse, and neither of you could say why.

Most of us want to help the people around us, and helping well is a skill, because good intentions don’t always land.   1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in kinds. Some people want ideas. Some want someone to listen. Some want a practical hand, a meal or a ride, and some just want company on the couch, and giving the wrong kind, however kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard.

    That phone call could have gone differently. “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to have a rant?” It feels a bit formal the first few times you say it, like reading off a card, but people tend to love being asked.

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being properly heard helps people calm down and think, and quite often they find their own answer. Good listening means paying attention, letting them finish, checking you understood, and showing that their feelings make sense even if you’d feel differently.

    Matching the help to the problem Cutrona and Russell proposed that support works best when it fits the problem: practical help for things that can be changed, comfort for things that can’t. Later studies back this in part.

    Cutrona and Russell (1990) · Moderate: widely cited; later studies support it in part

    In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice, then sum up what you heard. Two minutes is longer than you think.

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone we want the problem gone. But doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable, so helping well often means helping someone do it themselves. It’s their life, and their choice.

    Rosalind’s son is twenty-four and struggling with a job application for the city, and she is itching to rewrite it. She can see three things wrong with the first paragraph alone. She sits on her hands. “Would you like me to read it and say what stands out?” He makes the changes himself, some of them, not the ones she’d have made. When he gets the interview he’s quietly, enormously proud, and so is she, and she doesn’t mention the paragraph.

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were yours can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion, which is warm concern from a steadier place, still caring deeply without sinking alongside the other person. A steady helper is more use than a second person drowning.

    When Abi’s roommate went through a breakup, Abi cried with her every night, stopped sleeping, and was a wreck by the end of the first week. So she changed tack. Still kind, still there. But a run in the mornings, and Friday evening with her own friends, and a quiet reminder to herself, roughly hourly, that this was her roommate’s pain to feel and hers to keep company with.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything leads, eventually, to resentment, exhaustion and not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can actually give, so your yes means something. And a kind no that comes with an offer of what you can do is still help.

    Kojo’s sister keeps asking to borrow money, fifty here and eighty there, and none of it comes back. He loves her and he’s running out, of money and of something else. On the phone one evening, with the TV on mute, he says it. “I can’t lend money anymore. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you want.” There’s a silence long enough for him to hear her kettle click off. “Fine,” she says, not fine. On Sunday she turns up with a grocery bag full of envelopes.

    Ways to say it “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    If you need words for a “not this time,” Say no kindly helps you make your own, with something to offer instead.

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot can burn out: caregivers, parents, nurses, teachers, the friend everyone turns to. The signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, or numb and cynical about the people you help, or dreading the next call, and any of them means you need a hand too.

    Gwen has looked after her husband since his stroke two years ago. Last month she snapped at him over the remote control, really snapped, and felt terrible for the rest of the day. That night she told her sister, at the kitchen table, “I think I’m running on empty.” Her sister took Tuesday mornings from then on, and Gwen walked to the end of the road and back, alone, on purpose. She has, she says, about a third of herself back.

    Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love.

  7. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something going wrong, each one can assume someone else will deal with it. Psychologists call this the bystander effect, and knowing about it helps you break it. Small, safe actions count. “Are you okay?” Getting a staff member.

    On a train out of Philadelphia, a man was making a young woman uncomfortable, leaning in and asking where she was getting off. Yvonne didn’t confront him. She sat down in the seat next to the woman and said brightly, “Hi! Is this the train to Chicago?” The woman grabbed the conversation with both hands. They talked about Chicago, which neither of them knew much about, until the next stop, when the man got up and moved down the car.

    It helps to decide in advance what your safe move would be, distracting or getting help or checking in afterward. A plan makes it easier to act, and people without one are more likely to freeze.

    Sometimes the person in trouble is someone you know well. If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s okay to ask them plainly.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  8. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways, and people who are good at giving help are often hopeless at receiving it. Letting someone help is a gift to them as well, because most people like being useful to someone they care about. And relationships where only one person ever helps start to feel lopsided.

    People say yes more than we think In a set of experiments and field studies, people asking for help underestimated, by as much as half, how many others would agree, mainly because they forgot how awkward it is to say no. Later work finds the effect varies with how you ask (in person or in writing).

    Flynn and Lake (2008) · Moderate: several experiments and field studies; the size of the effect varies with how you ask

    After the baby came, Nkechi said “We’re fine, thanks” to every single offer, including several when she very much wasn’t. When she finally said yes to the woman next door, who had offered a lasagna four times, the neighbor was so pleased she nearly dropped it. They walk their strollers around the park together on Thursday mornings now. Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage.

    Words you could use “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

Two ways to read this

Help, two ways

Both directions Some people want ideas the moment they share a problem. Others want to be heard and nothing more, and an idea offered too soon feels like being corrected. If you fix, the listener isn’t ungrateful. They wanted company first. If you need to be heard, the fixer isn’t dismissing you. Ideas are how they show they care. Asking “Ideas, or just listening?” saves both of you an evening.

Where the fields meet Business explains the bystander moment exactly: work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, so one person has to own it. Holistic therapies add the other half, which is to start with the body, because a helper who never rests stops being able to help. Some of these practices are well studied and some are debated.

In everyday life

Helping yourself counts too, and you can ask yourself the same question, ideas or rest. Caring for a parent, or a child with extra needs, is long-haul help, so share the load early (see Caring for someone you love). The friend everyone leans on needs someone to lean on too. Covering for a struggling coworker is kind for a week, and after that it needs a real conversation with the team. In an emergency, point to one person (“You in the blue coat, please get help”), because a crowd tends to wait. If you have ADHD, helping can feel interesting and urgent, so a fast yes comes easily, and “I’d like to help. Let me check my week and come back to you tonight” buys you time. Caring a lot, or losing yourself?

People sometimes call a devoted helper “codependent.” Codependency is not a diagnosis, and researchers debate how to define it, so it’s worth being careful with it.

For something to be caring that has tipped into losing yourself, these are usually all present:

  • your mood rises and falls with the other person’s, most days,
  • you can’t say no to them, even when you want to,
  • you keep covering for choices that hurt them or you,
  • your own needs, friends and rest have quietly disappeared for months.

Not enough on its own:

  • caring a lot about someone,
  • helping a partner or parent through a hard year,
  • being the person who organizes the family,
  • a culture or family where people come first and look after each other.

If you recognize yourself in the first list, the aim is to take some of yourself back, not to care less. One protected evening a week is a start.

Common mix-ups

People think helping means solving the problem. Often it means listening, or being there, or helping someone find their own answer. People think good helpers never say no, when it’s knowing their limits that lets good helpers last. And “I’ll make it worse if I step in” stops a lot of people. There are small, safe ways to help, and doing nothing often feels worse to the person in trouble.

If you want to help someone else with this

The helpers around you need looking after too. Notice them, the caregiver, the friend everyone turns to, the coworker who always covers, and ask how they are as if you mean it. Offer something specific, because “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is far easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.” Give them permission to rest (“You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this”). And if a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love. Sometimes it’s as simple as asking them, “You look after everyone. Who looks after you?”

Words for hard moments

Some ways to say the hard parts, collected in one place. Change the words until they sound like you.

To begin

  • “Is now a good time to talk about Saturday, or would tomorrow be better?”
  • “Something has been on my mind, and I’d rather tell you than let it grow.”
  • “I’d like to understand how it looked from your side.”

To check what they meant

  • “You said you were fine. I’d like to check, because I’m not sure.”
  • “Can I check I’ve got this right?”
  • “I want to be sure I said that clearly. What did you take from it?”

To pause

  • “I need twenty minutes. I’ll come back after that.”
  • “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet.”

To put it right

  • “That came out wrong. Can I try again?”
  • “I think we both meant well, and it still went sideways.”
  • “I’m sorry I was sharp with you earlier.”

When someone is struggling

  • “Do you want ideas, or would you like me to just listen?”
  • “That sounds hard. Tell me more.”
  • “I can do Tuesday evenings. Who else could we ask?”

My family remember things completely differently from me. Who is right?

Often both of you, a little. Memory keeps what mattered to each person and quietly fills in the rest. How we see each other (Chapter 3, page ) explains why, and suggests asking “What do you remember about that day?” before you share your own version.

How do I help someone without wearing myself out?

Ask what kind of help they want, do the part you can keep doing, and say plainly where your limit is. Help that lasts usually comes from more than one person. Helping others well (Chapter 7, page ) has a part on noticing when you are running on empty.

I feel like I don’t quite belong anywhere. Is something wrong with me?

No. Feeling on the edge of a group is common, especially after a move, a breakup or a change of job. Belonging tends to grow from small, repeated contact with the same few people. Words used in this book

The book’s own words, each in one plain sentence. All of them are at spreadloveandacceptance.com/glossary.

Check-in
A check-in is a short, planned talk that keeps small things small. The daily one takes about ninety seconds, with no debating.
Complacency
Complacency is what happens when something has worked for a long time and you stop checking on it. It comes from a setup nobody has looked at lately, and it says nothing about laziness or love.
Drift
Drift is how two people slowly fall out of step when nobody checks in, even when nothing is wrong.
Flooded
Flooded means being so wound up that listening stops working for a while. The kind thing is a short pause with a time to come back.
Frequency
Your frequency is your natural pace: how fast you decide, talk, reply and recover.
Groupthink
When a close group wants agreement so much that doubts go unsaid and a poor decision goes ahead. Irving Janis named it in 1972.
A kind no
A kind no says what you can and can’t do right now, without blaming anyone.
Kinship care
When a grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend raises a child whose parents can’t.
The load
The load is all the work of a shared life, including the thinking, planning and remembering that nobody sees.
My weather today
My weather today is a one-minute check on your own conditions today, like sleep, stress and energy: Clear means calm and connected, Gusty means revved up, and Fogged in means running on empty.
Owner (one owner per job)
The owner of a job is the one person who makes sure it gets done from start to finish, so nobody has to keep asking.
Pressure
Pressure, in My weather today, is how much you were already carrying today: light, building or heavy. Behind the forecast sits a rough tally from 0 to 100; under 45 leaves room in the day, 65 or more means go gently. It is a reading of what you told it, not a score or a grade.
Repair
A repair is anything that stops a tense moment from getting worse, like a joke, a softer tone or “Can we start over?”
Retune
To retune is to notice you’ve fallen out of step with someone and adjust together: slower, softer, or at a better time.
Static
Static is the crackle between two people when the words are fine but the tone, pace or timing doesn’t match.
Stress tank
How much stress you are already carrying, from How much are you carrying?, scored from 0 (empty) to 1 (full). Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 to 0.59 medium, 0.60 and up high. A fuller tank means less room to take more on. (Your battery is the other side of it: energy left.)
Turning toward
Turning toward means answering someone’s small bids for attention, even briefly, instead of missing or brushing them off.
Wiring
Your wiring is the way your brain and senses take in words, sound, change and people. It stays the same from day to day.
Workpaper
A page you fill in, on your own or with someone, about your own week or your own side. Every workpaper is free to print or to fill out on the website.
Your lens
Your lens is the set of understandings about yourself, other people and what they owe you that you picked up growing up and still use without noticing.
