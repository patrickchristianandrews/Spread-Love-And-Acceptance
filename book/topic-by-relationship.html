The book by relationship

The ideas in the book are the same for everyone, but which parts matter most depends on who you share your life with. This page picks the three to five best sections of the book for each kind of relationship, with one line on why. Each link goes straight to that part of a chapter’s expanded version.

Just you

For working on yourself, whether you live alone, are between relationships, or simply want to start before asking anyone else. Nothing here needs another person.

  • Part One, Then: the rulebook from home: write down the rules you grew up with, and choose which ones to keep.
  • Part One, Now: where is this feeling coming from?: a four-step check for when a reaction is bigger than the moment.
  • Part One, Now: plan what settles you: decide on a calm day what helps, so you don’t have to invent it in a hard moment.
  • Preface: your own unseen work: count the noticing, remembering and planning you carry, even when you live alone.
  • Chapter V: a monthly look at yourself: ten kind minutes a month: what filled you, what drained you, one thing to change.
  • Part Three, Chapter 7: a kinder inner voice: own a mistake without shame, and speak to yourself the way you would to a friend.
  • Part Three, Chapter 6: why small steps work: change that lasts starts tiny, and a slip is information, not failure.

Couples, of every kind

For two partners, married or not, same-sex or not, living together or apart. The examples in the book often use a couple, so most of it fits. These are the best places to start.

  • Preface: why the other person doesn’t see it: the work nobody sees is usually missed, not ignored.
  • Chapter I: retuning, step by step: name it, pause with a return time, slow down, then go back with one clear sentence.
  • Chapter III: seven angles on the same moment: tiredness, history and time pressure can make you both right about different things.
  • Chapter IV: the two kinds of fair: “did it all get done?” versus “did we each do what we said?” and how to agree a mix.
  • Chapter V: how to talk about a pattern: one topic, a good moment, and hearing each other back before answering.
  • Part Three, Chapter 4: how repair works: small, early repairs keep a mix-up from becoming a fight.
  • Part Three, Chapter 5: anxious, avoidant and secure: why one of you reaches out and the other pulls back, and how both can feel safer.

More for couples: where to start in your relationship · how to stop fighting · money together · gaming and time together · long distance

More than two partners

For triads, polycules and anyone sharing a home with more than one partner. More people means more to coordinate, so clear ownership matters even more.

  • Preface: your side, their side: each person logs only their own week; nobody fills in anything for anyone else.
  • Chapter II: workload balance: with three or more, compare each share with an even share, or with the split you agreed.
  • Chapter IV: look to the owner: one owner and one backstop per job ends “I thought one of you had it.”
  • Chapter III: two true views of the same thing: three people can see one evening three ways, and all be right.
  • Chapter V: sort each repeat: a monthly look for jobs that keep slipping, without a review of anyone.

Friends

For close friends, old friends and friendships that have drifted. There’s no shared home here, but effort, timing and tone still matter.

  • Part One, Now: your circle, and matching energy: give warmth where it comes back, and notice when you’re always the one reaching out.
  • Chapter I: why close can be noisy: we check our meaning less with people we know well.
  • Chapter III: leftover stress: a short reply may be a full tank, not a cooling friendship.
  • Part One, Next: ask for support, as information: “Would you ask me how it went?” is a gift to a friend, not a demand.
  • Part Three, Chapter 5: building connection: how friendships grow from small, repeated moments.
  • Part Three, Chapter 3: the stories we tell: “they’ve gone quiet, they must be upset” is a guess worth checking.

Family across generations

For parents and children, teens, grandparents, and grown-up children with their parents. Each generation learned different rules, and each stage of life asks something different.

  • Part One, Then: each stage of growing up asks its own question: why a teen, a new parent and a grandparent can want such different things.
  • Part One, Then: an old rule can read other people for you: “in my day we just…” is a family rule, not a fact.
  • Chapter IV: choosing your kind of fair: agree house rules and childcare help on a calm day, not in the middle of a visit.
  • Chapter IV: letting a comment land before you react: advice can sound like criticism; check what was meant first.
  • Part One, Now: explain yourself without over-sharing: one sentence on what helps you, without owing anyone your history.
  • Part Three, Chapter 10: when values clash: two generations can want good things that pull in different directions.
  • Part Three, Chapter 9: adjustments that help everyone: for families with a child or grandparent whose mind works differently.

Siblings and family rifts

For adult brothers and sisters, and for anyone in a family that has gone quiet after a loss, a will, or years of hurt. You’re allowed to reach out, and you’re allowed to decide you’re not ready.

  • Part One, Then: when something happened matters: siblings raised in the same house can grow up in quite different families.
  • Part One, Then: you get to choose which rules to keep: “the eldest holds it together” can be put down.
  • Chapter III: seven angles on the same moment: the same funeral or will can look completely different from each side.
  • Chapter I: going back in, say it so it lands: a first message after a long silence: short, warm, no demands.
  • Part One, Now: your circle of control: you can offer contact; you can’t make it be accepted.
  • Part Three, Chapter 11: what forgiveness is, and isn’t: it isn’t forgetting, excusing or having to reconnect.
  • Part Three, Chapter 8: grieving styles and families: why siblings can grieve the same parent so differently.

More: adult siblings and family rifts · grief and later life

Separated co-parents

For parents raising children across two homes. The relationship has changed, but the shared load hasn’t gone away. Keep it about the children, and keep it practical.

  • Chapter I: nobody has to be wrong: arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it.
  • Chapter IV: look to the owner: one owner per job across two homes: forms, kit, appointments.
  • Chapter IV: keep the two questions apart: “was it done?” is a fact; “did that message hurt?” is for another time.
  • Chapter III: what a high reading asks of you: a handover when either tank is full is a time for logistics only.
  • Chapter V: what the look-back is for: fixing the setup, never reviewing the other parent.
  • Part Three, Chapter 4: texts, email and tone: why short messages get read in the worst light, and how to write ones that land.

More: co-parenting · on my own, and part-time parenting

Foster, adoptive and kinship families

For foster caregivers, adoptive parents, and grandparents or relatives raising a child. Children often arrive with rules learned somewhere hard, and caregivers often run on a full tank for a long time.

  • Part One, Then: old answers learned without all the facts: a child’s “adults leave” makes sense once you see where it came from.
  • Part One, Now: being wired differently is not being wrong: behavior is information about what a child needs.
  • Chapter IV: covering without resentment: two caregivers say covering out loud, so neither quietly burns out.
  • Chapter III: when “full” has become normal: long-term caring can make a high tank feel ordinary.
  • Chapter II: leftover stress: when a caregiver’s stress is high, treat it as the headline.
  • Part Three, Chapter 5: what attachment is: how early bonds shape what a child expects, and how safety is built again.

More: foster and kinship families · for grandparents · when a child has ADHD

Roommates and roommates

For shared apartments, house shares and rooms in the dorms. You may not be close, and that’s fine. Clear jobs and a short monthly meeting keep things friendly.

  • Preface: the kinds of unseen work: someone always notices the trash can and the bills; name it early.
  • Chapter IV: choosing your kind of fair: owned jobs by promises, one-offs by whoever can.
  • Chapter IV: the trash, three weeks in a row: what to do when one job keeps slipping.
  • Chapter II: ownership clarity: the jobs nobody owns matter most.
  • Chapter V: getting ready: four weeks of notes and twenty minutes is enough for a house meeting.
  • Part Three, Chapter 3: actions and reasons: the dishes left out are about a busy week more often than about you.

Work and business partners

For coworkers, managers, teams and people who run a business together. The book’s ideas came from looking at how systems work, so they fit a workplace well.

  • Chapter II: what the check is for: is the way you share work able to last? It judges the setup, never a person.
  • Chapter IV: judging by promises: clear owners and agreed deadlines keep a team fair.
  • Chapter I: what sets the frequency: pace and urgency differ: what “soon” means to you may not be what it means to them.
  • Chapter IV: the flat thank-you: checking the meaning of a short message before reacting to it.
  • Chapter V: close the month and keep count: fewer unowned jobs over time means the setup is working.
  • Part Three, Chapter 4: styles, cultures and minds: direct and indirect ways of talking, and how to meet in the middle.
  • Part Three, Chapter 12: boundaries in helping: supporting a colleague without carrying their whole load.

More: at work · appreciation at work

Life changes

For any relationship going through a big change: a new baby, an illness or disability, caring for someone, coming home after time apart, different shifts, retirement, children leaving home, a breakup, coming out, or blending two faiths. The old split may not fit anymore.

  • Chapter II: a month of readings is a trend: check the setup again when life changes, even if it worked before.
  • Part One, Next: plan inside your circle, and budget your energy: share by energy, not hours, when one of you has less to give.
  • Chapter III: when “full” has become normal: a long hard season can make a full tank feel ordinary.
  • Chapter V: the quiet months: once things settle, keep looking; that’s when drift creeps in.
  • Part Three, Chapter 8: endings, the in-between and beginnings: big changes are losses too, even good ones.
  • Part Three, Chapter 6: change with other people: making a change when the people around you haven’t chosen it.
