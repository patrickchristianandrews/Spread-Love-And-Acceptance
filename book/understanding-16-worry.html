Worry, low mood and trauma

Simple versionRead the full chapter

How worry and anxiety work, the difference between a low patch and depression, what trauma is and isn’t, what helps day to day, how to support someone, and when and where to get more help. For anyone, in any kind of relationship, or on your own.

In short

  • Worry, low mood and the after-effects of trauma are common, human and treatable. They are not weakness, and they are not your fault.
  • They all run through the stress system from Chapter 2. Small, steady things that settle the body and reconnect you with life help more than big pushes.
  • Avoiding and withdrawing feel safer but keep things going. Small steps back in, a little at a time, help most.
  • Talking therapies work for many people, and so can medicines. If it has lasted weeks, is getting in the way of life, or you feel unsafe, get help. If you might act on thoughts of ending your life, call or text 988 (US) or call Samaritans on 116 123 (UK and Ireland) now.

Sunday, 10:50pm

The house is quiet. You’ve checked the front door twice and the back door once, and you’re thinking about the front door again. Tomorrow is the hospital, ten o’clock. Round and round. What if it’s bad. What if I can’t cope. Who would I even tell.

And if you’re honest, it isn’t only tonight. You haven’t really wanted to see anyone for a few weeks. Even replying to a text has started to feel like lifting something.

Almost everyone worries, feels low or goes through something frightening at some point. For some people it settles by itself. For others it sticks, and grows, until it shapes every day. This chapter is about how these things work, what tends to help, and when it’s time to ask for more. It can’t diagnose anyone. If any of this is about you right now, you aren’t alone in it, and it can get better.

Some tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. The daily check-in’s “low, medium or high” (Part Two, Chapter I) lets people adjust when worry or low mood has shrunk the room you have. “A full tank means later, not never” (Part Two, Chapter III) names a time to come back, because avoiding a thing keeps fear going. And picking one small goal (Part One, Next) suits low mood, since doing very often comes before feeling better.

  1. Worry and anxiety are the body’s alarm.

    Fear is the body’s alarm for danger that’s here now. Anxiety is the same alarm ringing about something that might happen. Worry is the thinking part, the “What if…?” going round. All three run through the stress system from Chapter 2. Racing heart, tight chest, shallow breath, churning stomach, bad sleep. The alarm is trying to protect you. The trouble is it can be set far too sensitive, and it can’t tell a tiger from an email.

    Before every presentation in class, Leah’s heart pounds so hard her hands shake her notes. She is fifteen. Nothing bad has ever happened in a presentation. Her alarm still treats a room full of people she’s known since Year 2, one of whom once ate a worm for 50p, as somewhere dangerous.

    When it rises, try breathing out slowly five times, each breath out longer than the breath in. Then name three things you can see. It tells the body the danger isn’t here right now. It won’t switch the alarm off. It can turn it down a notch.

    Dig deeper: how anxiety works

  2. Avoiding keeps fear going.

    When something makes us anxious, stepping away brings quick relief, and the relief teaches the brain that escaping was clever. So next time the fear is a little bigger and the world a little smaller. What helps is going back towards the feared thing in small, chosen steps, and staying long enough to find out you can cope. Therapists call this exposure. Craske and colleagues (2014) describe how it works best when each step tests what you expect to happen.

    After a panic attack on the 42, Graham started walking everywhere. In the rain. Three miles to work. His sister didn’t argue. One Saturday she just stood at the bus stop with him and they got on, and off again, after one stop. A week later, two stops. It took most of the spring before he did the whole run into town, sitting by the doors, feeling sick, reading the same advert for a solicitor over and over. But he did it. His brain had slowly learned that buses are survivable. He still sits by the doors.

    Things to tell yourself “What do I think will happen? Let’s see what actually happens.” “Uncomfortable isn’t the same as dangerous.”

    Dig deeper: avoidance and small steps

  3. Give worry a time and a place.

    Trying hard not to worry rarely works. What helps a lot of people is postponing it. You notice a worry, write it down, and save it for a set “worry time” later in the day. In an early study by Borkovec and colleagues (1983), people who practised this for four weeks reported worrying less each day. In worry time, go through the list and ask of each one whether there’s anything you can do about it. If there is, plan one step. If there isn’t, practise letting it go, which is easier said than done, I know.

    Desmond looks after his dad and keeps a small red notebook by the kettle. Worries go in it all day. At six, with a cup of tea, he gives the notebook fifteen minutes. By then half the morning’s worries look smaller, and a couple have quietly sorted themselves out. The one about the boiler is still there. It has been there since August.

    Thoughts you could try “Noted. I’ll think about that at six.” “Can I do anything about this today? If not, it can wait.”

  4. Low mood is common. Depression is more than a bad week.

    Everyone has low days, and sadness after a loss or a let-down is normal. Depression is different. It means low mood, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy, for most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, often with poor sleep, tiredness, appetite changes, feeling worthless or guilty, and finding it hard to think. It can look like sadness. It can also look like irritability, or just running on empty. It is a health problem, and nobody gets it by being weak.

    Lionel taught geography for thirty-one years. Lately he has stopped going to the Thursday walking group. He isn’t reading. He wakes at four every morning and lies there. Getting old, he tells himself. It was his daughter, up for the weekend, who said it, carefully, while they were washing up. “Dad, I think this might be depression. And that can be treated.” He was quite cross with her for about a day.

    You could say “I’ve not been myself for a few weeks, and I don’t think it’s just tiredness.” “I don’t enjoy things I used to. Can I talk to you about it?”

    Dig deeper: low mood and depression

  5. Doing often comes before feeling better.

    Low mood tells you to wait until you feel like it. But withdrawing makes life emptier, and that deepens the low. A well-tested approach called behavioural activation turns this round. You plan small, doable things that bring a bit of pleasure or a sense of having done something, and you do them whether you feel like it or not. A review of 26 trials by Ekers and colleagues (2014) found it helped with depression, though many of the studies were small.

    After her divorce, living on her own, Lorraine planned three small things a day. A ten-minute walk to the postbox and back. One call or message. One job she could finish, like wiping the hob. Some days they felt pointless. Most days, honestly. She did them anyway, and over a few weeks the days started to feel less flat, though she couldn’t tell you which day it changed.

    Things to tell yourself “I don’t have to feel like it to do it.” “Small counts.”

    Dig deeper: behavioural activation

  6. Sleep, moving and people do a lot of the work.

    These sound too simple to matter. The evidence is solid all the same. Improving sleep can lift mood: in a large trial with university students, Freeman and colleagues (2017) found that an online sleep course reduced insomnia, and with it some other mental health difficulties.

    Exercise helps depression too. A review of 218 trials by Noetel and colleagues (2024) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all helped. Belonging to groups protects as well. Cruwys and colleagues (2013) found that people with depression who joined groups were less likely to relapse.

    A new dad, exhausted and flat, sat down with his partner and a calendar. One protected long sleep each at the weekend. A pram walk every morning, round the park, whatever the weather. And his old Wednesday five-a-side, which he’d dropped when the baby came and missed more than he’d admitted. He was terrible at five-a-side the first week. He went back. Calm sounds help some people wind down at night, too, and Cool-down corner has some.

    Words you could use “I need help protecting my sleep for a while.” “Would you come for a short walk with me? I find it hard to go on my own.”

    Dig deeper: sleep, exercise and connection

  7. Trauma is about what happened, and what it left behind.

    Trauma usually means going through, or seeing, something that threatened life or safety, such as violence, abuse, a serious accident, war, or the sudden death of someone close. It is very common. In surveys across 24 countries, Kessler and colleagues (2017) found about 7 in 10 adults had been through at least one.

    Most people recover over time. A smaller number develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with memories that come back vividly, avoiding reminders, feeling on high alert, and sleep problems. Ordinary hard times and stress hurt too, even when they aren’t trauma in this sense.

    Months after a crash on a wet roundabout, Shirin still jumps at the sound of brakes and won’t drive after dark. Her partner has started saying “It’s fine, it’s fine” before she’s even flinched, which she finds both sweet and maddening. Her alarm learned that cars can mean danger and hasn’t unlearned it yet. That is a long way from overreacting. If something frightening happened more than a month ago and it still shapes your days, talk to a doctor. Trauma-focused talking therapies help many people.

    Things to tell yourself “My reactions make sense after what happened.” “It’s in the past, even if my body doesn’t feel that yet.”

    Dig deeper: what trauma is and isn’t

  8. After something frightening: safety, choice and no pressure.

    After a frightening event, people need safety, practical help, warmth and choice. They don’t need to be made to talk. A review of single-session “debriefing” after trauma by Rose and colleagues (2002) found it did not prevent PTSD, and might make things worse for some. Services that are “trauma-informed” (SAMHSA, 2014) put safety, trust, choice and working together first, and the same ideas help in families, friendships and workplaces.

    Someone had been in Nan’s house while she was at the shops. They took her purse from the drawer and her late husband’s watch. She didn’t want to talk about it, and said so, twice. Her grandson didn’t push. He fixed the lock on the back door, which took him most of the afternoon and a trip to Screwfix, and slept on her sofa. “If you ever want to talk about it, I’m here,” he said in the morning, and went to work. A week later she rang him at nine at night and talked for an hour, mostly about the watch.

    Ways to say it “You don’t have to tell me anything. What would help right now?” “You’re safe now. I’m staying.”

    Dig deeper: trauma-informed care

  9. Talking therapies help many people, and medicines can too.

    Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) teaches practical ways to notice and change patterns of thinking and doing. It’s one of the most studied talking therapies, and a review of over a hundred meta-analyses by Hofmann and colleagues (2012) found the strongest support for anxiety problems and stress, and support for many other problems too.

    Guidelines such as NICE (2022) also recommend guided self-help, behavioural activation, exercise groups and, for some people, antidepressants. Questions about medicine, including starting, changing or stopping it, are for a doctor or prescriber. Please don’t stop a medicine suddenly without advice.

    Zack was too embarrassed to ask anyone about the panic attacks. Then one stopped him getting on the 7.40 for the second week running, and he stood on the platform watching it go. His GP referred him for a short course of CBT. Eight sessions. He takes the train to work again now, with a book and a bottle of water for the bad days, and there are still some bad days. Before an appointment, it helps to write three lines. What you’ve noticed. How long. What it’s stopping you doing.

    Words to try “I’d like to talk about my mood. It’s been going on for a while.” “What are my options, and what do you recommend for someone like me?”

    Dig deeper: what treatment involves

Your part, and only your part. You can learn how your alarm works, keep the basics going, take small steps back towards life, and ask for help when it’s more than you can carry. You can’t think your way out of depression by trying harder, and you can’t fix someone else’s. Being kind to yourself, and getting the right help, are part of the work. If you feel unsafe, reaching out today matters more than anything on this page.

Monday, 7:30am

Last night you wrote the three worries on the back of an envelope from the gas board and told them, out loud, feeling ridiculous, that they could wait till morning. You slept a bit. Now you breathe out long at the kitchen window and text your neighbour. Hospital at ten. Bit scared. Cuppa after?

Three hearts and “course”. The appointment is still the appointment. You look at the envelope again, and the weeks of not wanting to see anyone, and add a fourth line at the bottom, smaller than the others. Ask about my mood.

If you’re a teenager

Exams, friends, group chats and the future can all land at once, usually at about one in the morning. Tolu is seventeen and can’t sleep the night before her chemistry exam. She writes the worry down for tomorrow’s ten-minute worry time, plans one small step (ask Mr Okafor what kind of questions come up) and puts her phone out on the landing, which she hates. The next day she tells her mum, who says, “Why didn’t you wake me?” and it helps more than Tolu expected.

If worry or low mood is stopping you sleeping or going to school most days for a few weeks, tell someone you trust or your doctor. Under 19 in the UK, you can call Childline free on 0800 1111.

You could say “I keep worrying at night. Can I talk it through?” “I don’t need you to fix it. I just need to say it.”

In everyday life

Living on your own can make worry louder at night and low mood quieter and easier to miss, so one small contact a day is worth protecting, even a two-minute call (see On your own: when it’s lonely). With a partner, low mood can look like not caring and anxiety can look like nagging, so name it plainly. “I’m low today, and I’m not cross with you.” Children and teenagers get anxious and low too, and calm, routine and listening help them more than reassurance on a loop. A friend who keeps cancelling may be struggling. Keep inviting them, with no pressure to say yes.

Grief and depression overlap without being the same thing, and Chapter 8 looks at grief on its own terms. At work, a short honest line to a manager often opens up more support than people expect. If you have ADHD, avoidance often looks like putting things off, so make the first step almost silly (“open the envelope”), set a ten-minute timer, or do it with someone else in the room. Common mix-ups

Anxiety doesn’t mean something is wrong with you. It’s a normal alarm. It only becomes a problem when it rings too loud or too often, or starts shrinking your life. Depression isn’t just sadness, and nobody snaps out of it. It affects sleep, energy, thinking and the body as well as mood, and many people recover with the right help. Not everything bad that happens is trauma in the clinical sense, which usually means a threat to life or safety. Other hard things still hurt and still deserve care, and the word doesn’t need to fit for help to be fair. Talking about trauma straight away doesn’t help everyone either. Some people want to and some don’t, forcing it can make things worse, and safety and choice come first.

And asking someone about suicide does not put the idea in their head. A review by Dazzi and colleagues (2014) found no evidence that asking increases suicidal thoughts, and it may help.

If you want to help someone else with this

Notice, and ask. “You’ve seemed low lately. How are you doing, really?” opens the door, and after that it helps to listen more than you talk. Don’t argue with the feeling. “Cheer up” and “It could be worse” push people away. “That sounds really hard” keeps them close. Offer something specific, a walk or a meal or a lift to the doctor, because a small practical offer is much easier to accept than a big vague one.

Ask directly about safety if you’re worried. “Are you having thoughts of ending your life?” If yes, stay with them, and get help together today. And look after yourself too, because supporting someone who is struggling is tiring (see Chapter 12).

Words you could use “I’m not trying to fix it. I just want you to know I’m here.” “Would it help if I came with you to the doctor?”

When to get more help

Talk to a doctor, a counsellor or a mental health service if:

  • you’ve felt low, empty or hopeless, or lost interest in things, most days for two weeks or more;
  • worry or panic is stopping you doing things, or you’re avoiding more and more;
  • something frightening happened over a month ago and still shapes your days and nights;
  • you’re using alcohol, drugs, food or anything else to cope;
  • you’re not sleeping, eating or looking after yourself;
  • you have thoughts of harming yourself.

If you might act on thoughts of ending your life, or you are in danger, get help now. Call your local emergency number (911 in the US and Canada, 999 in the UK, 112 in most of Europe). In the US, call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or text HOME to 741741. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123, or text SHOUT to 85258.

In Canada, call or text 988. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Under 19 in the UK? Childline is free on 0800 1111. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines in your country. More are on Someone to talk to, free.

In England you can usually refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies without seeing a GP first. Find a counsellor or therapist has more places to start. If someone at home is hurting or frightening you, Not safe at home? lists free, private help.

This chapter is general education. It isn’t a diagnosis or a substitute for care from a professional.
