Worry, low mood and trauma

Simple versionRead the full chapter

How worry and anxiety work, the difference between a low patch and depression, what trauma is and isn’t, what helps day to day, and how to support someone. For anyone, in any kind of relationship, or on your own.

At a glance

  • Worry, low mood and the after-effects of trauma are common, human and treatable. They are not weakness, and they are not your fault.
  • They all run through the stress system from Chapter 2. Small, steady things that settle the body and reconnect you with life help more than big pushes.
  • Avoiding and withdrawing feel safer but keep things going. Small steps back in, a little at a time, help most.
  • Therapy works for many people, and so can medicines.

Sunday, 10:50pm

The house is quiet. You’ve checked the front door twice and the back door once, and you’re thinking about the front door again. Tomorrow is the hospital, ten o’clock, and it goes round and round. What if it’s bad. What if I can’t cope. Who would I even tell.

And if you’re honest, it isn’t only tonight. You haven’t really wanted to see anyone for a few weeks. Even replying to a text has started to feel like lifting something.

Almost everyone worries, feels low or goes through something frightening at some point. For some people it settles by itself, and for others it sticks, and grows, until it shapes every day. This chapter is about how these things work, what tends to help, and when it’s time to ask for more. It can’t diagnose anyone. If any of this is about you right now, you aren’t alone in it, and it can get better.

Some tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. The daily check-in’s “low, medium or high” (Part Two, Chapter I) lets people adjust when worry or low mood has shrunk the room you have. “A full tank means later, not never” (Part Two, Chapter III) names a time to come back, because avoiding a thing keeps fear going. And picking one small goal (Part One, Next) suits low mood, since doing very often comes before feeling better.

Quickly, then slowly. On the surface, checking the door twice. Underneath, an alarm that keeps going off with nothing to point at. Underneath that, sleep, people and days that have narrowed. The roots are state and setting, and feedback loops, since avoiding keeps the alarm going. The rest of the chapter takes each part in turn.

  1. Worry and anxiety are the body’s alarm.

    Fear is the body’s alarm for danger that’s here now. Anxiety is the same alarm ringing about something that might happen. Worry is the thinking part, the “What if…?” going round. All three run through the stress system from Chapter 2, which is why they come with a racing heart and a tight chest and a churning stomach and nights of bad sleep. The alarm is trying to protect you. The trouble is it can be set far too sensitive, and it can’t tell a tiger from an email.

    Fear of not knowing One review argues that fear of the unknown, the not-knowing itself, may sit underneath a lot of anxiety. It’s a proposal rather than a settled finding, but it fits the night before a hospital appointment better than most explanations do.

    Carleton (2016) · Theory: an influential review; the central claim is a proposal

    Before every presentation in class, Leah’s heart hammers so hard her hands shake her notes. She is fifteen, and nothing bad has ever happened to her in a presentation. Her alarm still treats a room full of people she’s known since first grade, one of whom once ate a worm for 50 cents, as somewhere dangerous.

    When it rises, try breathing out slowly five times, each breath out longer than the breath in. Then name three things you can see. It tells the body the danger isn’t here right now, and while it won’t switch the alarm off, it can usually turn it down a notch.

    Dig deeper: how anxiety works

  2. Avoiding keeps fear going.

    When something makes us anxious, stepping away brings quick relief, and the relief teaches the brain that escaping was clever. So next time the fear is a little bigger and the world a little smaller. What helps is going back toward the feared thing in small, chosen steps, and staying long enough to find out you can cope. Psychologists call this exposure. Craske and colleagues (2014) describe how it works best when each step tests what you expect to happen.

    After a panic attack on the 42, Graham started walking everywhere, in the rain, three miles to work and three miles back. His sister didn’t argue. One Saturday she just stood at the bus stop with him and they got on, and off again, after one stop. A week later, two stops. It took most of the spring before he did the whole run into town, sitting by the doors, feeling sick, reading the same ad for a lawyer over and over. But he did it, and his brain had slowly learned that buses are survivable.

    Dig deeper: avoidance and small steps

  3. Give worry a time and a place.

    Trying hard not to worry rarely works. What helps a lot of people is postponing it. You notice a worry, write it down, and save it for a set “worry time” later in the day. In an early study by Borkovec and colleagues (1983), people who practiced this for four weeks reported worrying less each day. In worry time, go through the list and ask of each one whether there’s anything you can do about it. If there is, plan one step. If there isn’t, practice letting it go, which is easier said than done, I know.

    Desmond looks after his dad and keeps a small red notebook by the kettle. Worries go in it all day. At six, with a cup of tea, he gives the notebook fifteen minutes. By then half the morning’s worries look smaller, and a couple have quietly worked themselves out. The one about the water heater has been in there since August, and he has decided to let it stay. When one turns up at the wrong time of day, the trick is to tell it, more or less, “Noted. I’ll think about that at six.”

  4. Low mood is common. Depression is more than a bad week.

    Everyone has low days, and sadness after a loss or a let-down is normal. Depression is different. It means low mood, or losing interest in things you used to enjoy, for most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more, often with poor sleep, tiredness, appetite changes, feeling worthless or guilty, and finding it hard to think. It can look like sadness. It can also look like irritability, or just running on empty. It is a health problem, and nobody gets it by being weak.

    Lionel taught geography for thirty-one years. Lately he has stopped going to the Thursday walking group. He isn’t reading. He wakes at four every morning and lies there listening to the water heater come on. Getting old, he tells himself. It was his daughter, up for the weekend, who said it, carefully, while they were doing the dishes. “Dad, I think this might be depression. And that can be treated.” He was pretty annoyed with her for about a day.

    You could say “I’ve not been myself for a few weeks, and I don’t think it’s just tiredness.” “I don’t enjoy things I used to. Can I talk to you about it?”

    Dig deeper: low mood and depression

  5. Doing often comes before feeling better.

    Low mood tells you to wait until you feel like it. But withdrawing makes life emptier, and that deepens the low. A well-tested approach called behavioral activation turns this around. You plan small, doable things that bring a bit of pleasure or a sense of having done something, and you do them whether you feel like it or not. A review of 26 trials by Ekers and colleagues (2014) found it helped with depression, though many of the studies were small.

    After her divorce, living on her own, Lorraine planned three small things a day, a ten-minute walk to the postbox and back, one call or message, and one job she could finish, like wiping the stove. Most days, honestly, they felt pointless. She did them anyway, and over a few weeks the days started to feel less dull, though she couldn’t tell you which day it changed.

    Dig deeper: behavioral activation

  6. Sleep, moving and people do a lot of the work.

    These sound too simple to matter. The evidence is solid all the same. Improving sleep can lift mood: in a large trial with university students, Freeman and colleagues (2017) found that an online sleep course reduced insomnia, and with it some other mental health difficulties.

    Exercise helps depression too. A review of 218 trials by Noetel and colleagues (2024) found walking or jogging, yoga and strength training all helped. Belonging to groups protects as well. Cruwys and colleagues (2013) found that people with depression who joined groups were less likely to relapse.

    People are not an optional extra A meta-analysis of 148 studies, with over 300,000 people, found that people with stronger social ties had about 50% better odds of survival over the years they were followed. The studies observe rather than prove cause, but it’s hard to read that and call calling a friend a luxury.

    Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010) · Strong: a large meta-analysis of observational studies; cause not established

    A new dad, exhausted and low, sat down with his partner and a calendar and between them they blocked out one protected long sleep each on the weekend, a stroller walk every morning around the park whatever the weather, and his old Wednesday five-a-side, which he’d dropped when the baby came and missed more than he’d admitted. He was terrible at five-a-side the first week, and went back anyway. Calm sounds help some people wind down at night, too, and Cool-down corner has some.

    Words you could use “I need help protecting my sleep for a while.” “Would you come for a short walk with me? I find it hard to go on my own.”

  7. Trauma is about what happened, and what it left behind.

    Trauma usually means going through, or seeing, something that threatened life or safety, such as violence, abuse, a serious accident, war, or the sudden death of someone close. It is very common. In surveys across 24 countries, Kessler and colleagues (2017) found about 7 in 10 adults had been through at least one.

    Most people recover over time. A smaller number develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with memories that come back vividly, avoiding reminders, feeling on high alert, and sleep problems. Ordinary hard times and stress hurt too, even when they aren’t trauma in this sense.

    Months after a crash at a wet intersection, Shirin still jumps at the sound of brakes and won’t drive after dark. Her partner has started saying “It’s fine, it’s fine” before she’s even flinched, which she finds both sweet and maddening. Her alarm learned that cars can mean danger and hasn’t unlearned it yet. That is a long way from overreacting. Trauma-focused therapies help many people.

    Dig deeper: what trauma is and isn’t

  8. After something frightening: safety, choice and no pressure.

    After a frightening event, people need safety, practical help, warmth and choice. They don’t need to be made to talk. A review of single-session “debriefing” after trauma by Rose and colleagues (2002) found it did not prevent PTSD, and might make things worse for some. Services that are “trauma-informed” (SAMHSA, 2014) put safety, trust, choice and working together first, and the same ideas help in families, friendships and workplaces.

    Someone had been in Grandma’s house while she was at the store. They took her purse from the drawer and her late husband’s watch. She didn’t want to talk about it, and said so, twice. Her grandson didn’t push. He fixed the lock on the back door, which took him most of the afternoon and two trips to the hardware shop, and slept on her sofa. “If you ever want to talk about it, I’m here,” he said in the morning, and went to work. A week later she called him at nine at night and talked for an hour, mostly about the watch.

    Dig deeper: trauma-informed care

  9. Therapy helps many people, and medicines can too.

    Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) teaches practical ways to notice and change patterns of thinking and doing. It’s one of the most studied talk therapies, and a review of over a hundred meta-analyses by Hofmann and colleagues (2012) found the strongest support for anxiety problems and stress, and support for many other problems too.

    Guidelines such as NICE (2022) also recommend guided self-help, behavioral activation, exercise groups and, for some people, antidepressants. Medicines work differently for different people, and stopping one suddenly can cause problems of its own.

    Zack was too embarrassed to ask anyone about the panic attacks. Then one stopped him getting on the 7:40 for the second week running, and he stood on the platform watching it go. His doctor referred him for a short course of CBT, eight sessions. He takes the train to work again now, with a book and a bottle of water for the bad days, and there are still some bad days. Before an appointment, it helps to write three lines, on what you’ve noticed, how long it has gone on, and what it’s stopping you doing.

    Words to try “I’d like to talk about my mood. It’s been going on for a while.” “What are my options, and what do you recommend for someone like me?”

    Dig deeper: what treatment involves

Your part, and only your part. You can learn how your alarm works, keep the basics going, take small steps back toward life, and ask for help when it’s more than you can carry. You can’t think your way out of depression by trying harder, and you can’t fix someone else’s. Being kind to yourself, and getting the right help, are part of the work.

Monday, 7:30am

Last night you wrote the three worries on the back of an envelope from the gas board and told them, out loud, feeling ridiculous, that they could wait till morning. You slept a bit. Now you breathe out long at the kitchen window and text your neighbor. Hospital at ten and I’m a bit scared, cup of coffee after?

Three hearts and “course.” The appointment is still the appointment. You look at the envelope again, and the weeks of not wanting to see anyone, and add a fourth line at the bottom, smaller than the others. Ask about my mood.

If you’re a teenager

Exams, friends, group chats and the future can all land at once, usually at about one in the morning. Tolu is seventeen and can’t sleep the night before her biology exam. She writes the worry down for tomorrow’s ten-minute worry time, plans one small step (ask Mr. Okafor what kind of questions come up) and puts her phone out on the landing, which she hates. The next day she tells her mom, who says, “Why didn’t you wake me?” and it helps more than Tolu expected.

If worry or low mood is stopping you sleeping or going to school most days for a few weeks, tell someone you trust.

You could say “I keep worrying at night. Can I talk it through?” “I don’t need you to fix it. I just need to say it.”

Two ways to read this

Worry, from both sides

Some people worry out loud, talking it round until it shrinks. Others keep it in, and talking about it too soon makes it grow. If you talk it out, the quiet worrier may need a walk side by side more than a conversation. If you keep it in, the talker isn’t making a fuss. Saying it is how they get it out of their head.

Where the fields meet. Holistic therapies start with the body, which is where worry lives first: slow breathing out, a walk, a regular bedtime. Some of these practices are well studied and some are debated. Aromatherapy adds that the setting matters, a quieter room or softer light. The evidence for particular oils is weak, so the help here is the room, not the bottle.

In everyday life

Living on your own can make worry louder at night and low mood quieter and easier to miss, so one small contact a day is worth protecting, even a two-minute call (see On your own: when it’s lonely). With a partner, low mood can look like not caring, and anxiety can look like nagging when it’s really a worried mind asking again for reassurance. Describe what’s going on instead of leaving the other person to guess: “I’m low today, and I’m not mad at you.” Children and teenagers get anxious and low too, and calm, routine and listening help them more than reassurance on a loop. A friend who keeps canceling may be struggling. Keep inviting them, with no pressure to say yes.

Grief and depression overlap without being the same thing, and Chapter 8 looks at grief on its own terms. At work, a short honest line to a manager often opens up more support than people expect. If you have ADHD, avoidance often looks like putting things off, so make the first step almost silly (“open the envelope”), set a ten-minute timer, or do it with someone else in the room. Common mix-ups

Anxiety doesn’t mean something is wrong with you. It’s a normal alarm. It only becomes a problem when it rings too loud or too often, or starts shrinking your life. Depression isn’t just sadness, and nobody snaps out of it. It affects sleep, energy, thinking and the body as well as mood, and many people recover with the right help. Not everything bad that happens is trauma in the clinical sense, which usually means a threat to life or safety. Other hard things still hurt and still deserve care, and the word doesn’t need to fit for help to be fair. Talking about trauma right away doesn’t help everyone either. Some people want to and some don’t, forcing it can make things worse, and safety and choice come first.

If you want to help someone else with this

Notice, and ask. “You’ve seemed a bit low lately. How are you doing, really?” opens the door, and after that it helps to listen more than you talk. Don’t argue with the feeling. “Cheer up” and “It could be worse” push people away. “That sounds really hard” keeps them close. Offer something specific, a walk or a meal or a ride somewhere, because a small practical offer is much easier to accept than a big vague one.

If you’re worried someone may be thinking of ending their life, it’s okay to ask them plainly, in your own words. Asking doesn’t put the idea in their head (a review by Dazzi and colleagues, 2014, found no sign that it does). If they say yes, stay with them, listen, and help them tell someone else they trust. And look after yourself too, because supporting someone who is struggling is tiring (see Chapter 12).

Words you could use “I’m not trying to fix it. I just want you to know I’m here.” “Would it help if I came with you?”
