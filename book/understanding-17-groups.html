Fitting in: groups, influence and culture

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why fitting in pulls so hard, how groups shape what we say and do, how culture and family set the unwritten rules, and how to stay yourself, and disagree kindly, without losing your place, whether the group is a family, a friendship, a team, a faith, a neighborhood or a group chat.

Thursday, 9:20pm

The family group chat is going. Uncle Trevor has posted a joke about your cousin’s new partner, and the laughing faces are stacking up underneath it. Six, then seven, and your mom has done one.

You don’t find it funny. You also don’t want to be the one who can’t take a joke. Your thumb is already over the little laughing face.

We all belong to groups, and we need them. Chapter 5 looks at belonging, at why we need each other and how trust grows. This chapter looks at the other side, at how groups pull on us, why we go along, and how to stay true to yourself while staying connected.

A joke in the family chat, quickly. On the surface, a picture and a laughing face. Underneath, the pull not to be the one who spoils it. Underneath that, a norm nobody wrote down. The roots are quiet incentives and ownership and structure. Then slowly, one pull at a time.

  1. The pull to fit in is old and strong.

    For most of human history, being in a group meant surviving, and being left out still feels alarming. In studies by Williams and colleagues (2000), people who were simply left out of an online ball-throwing game, by strangers they would never meet, felt worse and less as if they belonged. So we bend to stay in. We laugh at the jokes, keep quiet, go along.

    At the warehouse, the new guy gets called “Prof” in his first week, because he wears glasses and once, fatally, used the word “ostensibly.” He hates it. He laughs anyway, every time, because more than anything that first month he wants to be one of them. By Christmas he’s down as Prof on the schedule, and has mostly stopped minding.

    Dig deeper: why being left out hurts

  2. We go along more than we think.

    In Solomon Asch’s famous experiments (Asch, 1956), people judged which of three lines matched another line. When the actors around them all gave the same wrong answer, people went along with the group on about a third of the answers, even though the right answer was obvious.

    Many people never went along at all. Later research, such as a review by Bond and Smith (1996), found the effect is real but varies, smaller in more recent studies and larger in cultures that put the group first.

    Groupthink Irving Janis named the moment a close group’s wish to agree crowds out a realistic look at the options. His evidence was case studies of big policy decisions, so it’s an idea more than a measurement, but two of his remedies travel well: the person in charge holds back their view at first, and someone is asked to argue the other side.

    Janis (1972) · Mixed: an influential idea built on case studies

    Everyone in the meeting loved the new schedule. Bukola could see a hole the size of a bus in the Friday cover, and said nothing, because everyone loved the new schedule. Afterward, waiting for the elevator, two colleagues said quietly that they’d had doubts too. All three of them went back to their desks. The schedule started on the Monday and the Friday cover fell over on the first Friday. Writing your own view down before a group decides, and only then listening, makes it a good deal easier to say it.

    Dig deeper: Asch and what came after

  3. Authority can lead people a long way.

    In Stanley Milgram’s experiments (Milgram, 1963), many ordinary people kept giving what they believed were painful electric shocks to a stranger because a man in a lab coat told them to, although no shocks were real. Burger (2009) repeated part of the study safely and found similar results. The studies are also debated. Later researchers, such as Haslam and Reicher (2012), argue that people went along when they believed in the cause, and many people refused.

    Safe to speak up In 51 teams at one manufacturing company, Amy Edmondson found that a shared sense that it was safe to take interpersonal risks went with more learning, and learning went with better performance. Most later studies are correlational, but the idea has held up widely.

    Edmondson (1999) · Strong: a highly replicated idea, though mostly correlational

    On a night shift, a senior doctor tells a staff nurse to give a dose that looks wrong to her. It’s three in the morning, and he is very senior and very tired. She checks anyway, and then says so, and she’s right. Checking was the loyal thing to do. He said thank you, eventually, and sounded as if he meant it.

    Dig deeper: Milgram and its critics

  4. “Us and them” forms fast.

    Part of how we see ourselves comes from our groups, whether that’s a family, a town, a team, a faith or a job. Tajfel and Turner (1979) called this social identity. It brings pride and loyalty. It also makes us quick to favor “us” and to see “them” as all the same, even when the groups are formed on almost nothing.

    Two branches of one family have always sat apart at weddings, and nobody can quite remember why. Something about a christening in 1994. At the most recent one a cousin quietly rearranged the seating plan, and the two sides discovered, over the cheese course, a shared and passionate weakness for terrible music. They were the last ones on the dance floor. They still sit apart at funerals, but it’s a start.

    Dig deeper: social identity

  5. Norms are the rules nobody wrote down.

    Every group has norms about what you wear, how you speak, what you laugh at and what you never mention, and we often guess them wrong. In studies at one university, Prentice and Miller (1993) found that most students were privately less comfortable with heavy drinking than they thought everyone else was. Each person thought they were the odd one out, so everyone kept going along.

    In one office everybody answered emails at midnight, because everybody thought it was expected. Then a manager said, quite casually, in a meeting about something else, that she didn’t expect replies after six. You could almost hear the room breathe out. “We have always done it this way” usually means a rule nobody has checked in years, so it’s worth asking when anyone last did.

    Dig deeper: norms and wrong guesses

  6. Cultures differ, and so do people inside them.

    Cultures differ in how much they put the individual or the group first, which researchers call individualism and collectivism (Hofstede, 1980). In some, standing out is admired, and in others fitting in and keeping harmony are signs of maturity. Labels need care, though. A large review by Oyserman and colleagues (2002) found the differences were smaller and less tidy than often claimed, and individuals vary hugely within every culture.

    Anjali calls her mom every day, sometimes twice. Her partner Sean, who was raised to “do his own thing” and calls his parents on birthdays, thought this was clingy. She thought he was cold, and said so, once, in the car. Each of them was following rules they’d learned before they could tie their shoes. It came out properly over a Sunday dinner at her mom’s, when her mom asked Sean when he’d last called his own mother, and he genuinely couldn’t remember.

    Dig deeper: culture, with care

  7. Every family is a small culture.

    Families have their own norms about how loud people are and how feelings are shown. Some things get talked about and some never do, and there are rules about how you say sorry, if you do. Usually we only notice our family’s rules when we live with someone from a different family. Where your lens came from looks at the rules you grew up with.

    Two roommates clashed over the kitchen for most of a semester. In one family you wash up straight after eating. In the other you leave it to soak and do it in the morning. Each thought the other was lazy, or fussy, depending. Neither was. Each was obeying a rule from a kitchen two hundred miles away, and once they’d worked that out they made a third rule, which neither of them likes and both of them keep.

    Dig deeper: family cultures

  8. Online groups pull harder and faster.

    Group chats, forums and social media make group pressure quicker and more public, because likes and replies show the norm instantly. In a study of over half a million posts, Brady and colleagues (2017) found that messages with more moral and emotional words spread further, mostly among people who already agreed. Outrage travels fast. Quiet doubts stay put.

    A parents’ group chat turned against a teacher after one angry post about a detention, forty messages in an hour. One dad, who knew her a little from the PTA, sent something calm. He said he’d want to hear her side before anyone decided anything. Two other parents agreed with him, and the thread went quiet and stayed quiet. Before you react to a heated post, wait ten minutes.   9. You can belong and still be yourself.

    Belonging means being accepted as you are, whereas fitting in means changing in order to be accepted (Chapter 5 says more). Good groups have room for difference. It helps to know your own values, to have more than one group, and to have at least one ally. In Asch’s experiments, when just one other person gave the right answer, going along with the group dropped sharply.

    Rory loves chess and his friends tease him about it, relentlessly. He didn’t drop chess and he didn’t drop his friends. He joined the club that meets in the library on Wednesdays as well, so now he has two groups and neither of them gets to decide who he is. He gets teased, and he’s also county under-16 runner-up, which he mentions more often than strictly necessary.

    Dig deeper: staying yourself

  10. Disagree kindly, and early.

    You don’t have to choose between going along and starting a war. A kind disagreement is short and calm, it’s about the issue, and it leaves room for other people. Speaking early is easier than speaking late, and a private word often works better than speaking up in front of everyone.

    Some people keep a sentence ready, for exactly this. “I see it a bit differently. Can I say why?” “I’m not comfortable with that joke.” “Can we hear from people who haven’t spoken yet?” Pick the one that sounds most like you and say it out loud a few times in the car, so it’s there when you need it. Say it so it lands helps with the words.

Your part, and only your part. You can notice the pull to fit in, check what you actually think, choose your groups, and say “I see it differently” kindly.

You can’t control what a group does, and you don’t have to stay in a group that keeps asking you to hide who you are or go against what’s right.

You don’t press it, and you message your aunt instead, privately. I know it was meant to be funny but it might hurt if they see it. Could we leave it?

Four minutes later the joke vanishes, and then a message comes through to you. It was only a bit of fun. People are so sensitive these days. At ten thirty your cousin messages to say thank you, because she saw it before it went. At Sunday lunch your aunt passes you the gravy without looking at you, and you don’t yet know whether that’ll pass.

If you’re a teenager

Group chats are where a lot of friendship happens, and a lot of hurt. Mason is fifteen when he finds a photo of himself from gym class being passed around his class chat with comments under it. He doesn’t answer back.

He screenshots it and mutes the chat. Then he tells his sister, and together they tell his homeroom teacher. Then a girl in his class messages him privately to say it wasn’t funny and that she’s sorry she didn’t say anything. Most people in a group chat don’t like what’s happening, and each of them thinks everyone else does.

You could say “I’m not okay with this. Please delete it.” To a friend, “That wasn’t okay. Are you alright?” To an adult, “Something’s happening online and I need help with it.”

Influence, pressure and manipulation.

Groups and people shape each other all the time, and most of it is ordinary. You pick up a team’s jokes, a family’s habits and a friend group’s taste in music, and people who care about you will sometimes try to change your mind. That’s influence, and it happens in the open. “Manipulative” is a much stronger word.

For something to be manipulation, these are usually all present:

  • the person wants something they aren’t saying, or gives you information they know is false,
  • they use your feelings, such as guilt, fear or love, to get it,
  • it’s deliberate and it repeats,
  • saying no costs you something, such as your place in the group or warmth from them.

Not enough on its own:

  • persuading you openly, even hard,
  • asking more than once,
  • being disappointed or sad when you say no,
  • hinting instead of asking, which is a normal style in many families and cultures.

A good test is whether you could say “I see it differently” and still be welcome next week. If something feels off, describe the moment rather than the motive: “When you said the others would be let down if I didn’t come, I felt pushed. Can you just ask me straight?”

Two ways to read this

A group, two ways

Some people pick up a group’s unwritten rules in a week without anyone saying them. Others only find a rule out by breaking it. If the rules reach you easily, say them out loud for the people they don’t reach. If they don’t reach you, asking “What’s the rule here?” is a fair question, not a weakness.

Where the fields meet. Politics says every group needs a fair way to decide and a say for everyone. Behavioral science adds that norms are habits the whole group keeps without noticing. The root is quiet incentives, because a group drifts toward whatever it rewards.

In everyday life

Having no group can be as hard as having a pushy one, and groups built round something you enjoy bring belonging along with the activity. Partners bring two families and two sets of rules, which are worth talking about openly before you make your own. Children and teenagers feel peer pressure strongly, and practicing a “no” that saves face helps them. Old family roles, the peacekeeper or the black sheep, can be hard to step out of. At work, when everyone agrees too fast, it helps if whoever leads shares their view last and asks each person to name one risk before deciding (more in the full chapter). And online, you’re allowed to mute, leave, or take a break from groups that leave you feeling worse.

If you want to help someone else with this

If someone speaks up, back them up, even quietly (“I think that’s a fair point”), because one ally makes a huge difference. In any group you lead, invite the quieter people and the different views by asking what nobody has thought of yet. If someone seems to be going along against their wishes, ask gently, away from the group. And keep an eye on who gets left out of the chats and the plans, so you can bring them in.

Words you could use “I noticed you went quiet when they said that. Are you okay?” “I’d like to hear what you think, even if it’s different.”
