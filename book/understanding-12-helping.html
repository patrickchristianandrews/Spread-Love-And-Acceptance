Part Three · Chapter 12

Helping others well: support without fixing, boundaries, and not burning out

Simple versionRead the full chapter

How to help someone without taking over, by asking what they need, listening first, caring without drowning and keeping kind limits, with what to do in a crisis and how to keep going without burning out.

In short

  • Ask what kind of help they want. “Ideas, or just someone to listen?” saves a lot of misfires.
  • Listening is help. Fixing a problem for someone can take away their power.
  • Your limits make your help last, and caring from a steady place helps more than drowning in their feelings.
  • In a crisis, stay and bring in help. It’s okay to ask directly about suicide, and to call for help.

Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend is telling you about her awful week. Before she’s finished her first sentence you have five good ideas. You give her all five. In order. With reasons.

“Yeah,” she says. “Maybe.”

You both hang up feeling slightly worse, and neither of you could tell you why.

Most of us want to help the people around us, and helping well is a skill, because good intentions don’t always land. This chapter looks at how support works, why fixing can backfire, and how to care for people in a way you can keep up. Two tools elsewhere grew out of it. One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II), because carers burn out when everything counts as “just helping” and nobody else owns a job. And How much are you carrying? (Part Two, Chapter III), which helps you notice you’re running on empty before you snap or stop.

  1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in kinds. Some people want ideas. Some want someone to listen. Some want a practical hand, a meal or a lift, and some just want company on the sofa. Giving the wrong kind, however kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard. The simplest fix is to ask.

    That phone call could have gone differently. “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to have a rant?” It feels a bit formal the first few times you say it. People tend to love being asked.

    You could say “Would ideas help, or would you rather I just listened?” “What would help most right now?”

    Dig deeper: kinds of support

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being properly heard helps people calm down and think, and quite often they find their own answer. Good listening means paying attention, letting them finish, checking you understood, and showing that their feelings make sense even if you’d feel differently.

    Ravi came home furious about his boss. His partner had “Just quit” ready on her tongue and kept it there. “So he had a go at you in front of everyone,” she said, “after you’d stayed late all week. No wonder you’re livid.” Ravi’s shoulders came down about two inches. Ten minutes later he was sketching out a plan on the back of a takeaway menu, and it was a better plan than “just quit”, and he’d have hated being told to quit.

    In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice, then sum up what you heard. Two minutes is longer than you think.

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone we want the problem gone. But doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable, so helping well often means helping someone do it themselves. It’s their life, and their choice.

    Rosalind’s son is twenty-four and struggling with a job application for the council, and she is itching to rewrite it. She can see three things wrong with the first paragraph alone. She sits on her hands. “Would you like me to read it and say what stands out?” He makes the changes himself, some of them, not the ones she’d have made. When he gets the interview he’s quietly, enormously proud, and so is she, and she doesn’t mention the paragraph.

    Things to tell yourself “Is this mine to fix, or theirs to solve with my support?” “What do they want to do here?”

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were yours can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion, which is warm concern from a steadier place, still caring deeply without sinking alongside the other person. A steady helper is more use than a second person drowning.

    When Abi’s housemate went through a breakup, Abi cried with her every night, stopped sleeping, and was a wreck by the end of the first week. So she changed tack. Still kind, still there. But a run in the mornings, and Friday evening with her own friends, and a quiet reminder to herself, roughly hourly, that this was her housemate’s pain to feel and hers to keep company with.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything leads, eventually, to resentment, exhaustion and not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can actually give, so your yes means something. And a kind no that comes with an offer of what you can do is still help.

    Kojo’s sister keeps asking to borrow money. Fifty here, eighty there. None of it comes back. He loves her and he’s running out, of money and of something else. On the phone, he says it. “I can’t lend money any more. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you want.” There’s a silence. “Fine,” she says, not fine. On Sunday she turns up with a carrier bag full of envelopes.

    Ways to say it “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    If you need words for a “not this time”, Say no kindly helps you make your own, with something to offer instead.

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot can burn out. Carers, parents, nurses, teachers, the friend everyone rings. The signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, or numb and cynical about the people you help, or dreading the next call. They mean you need help too.

    Gwen has looked after her husband since his stroke two years ago. Last month she snapped at him over the remote control, properly snapped, and felt terrible for the rest of the day. She mentioned it to her doctor almost in passing, on the way out of an appointment about her knee. The doctor sat her back down. “That sounds like carer strain.” There’s a carers’ group on Tuesday mornings now, and a few hours of respite a week, and she has, she says, about a third of herself back.

    Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love.

    Dig deeper: burnout and refilling

  7. In a crisis, stay with them and bring in help.

    If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s okay to ask them directly. Research, such as a review led by Dazzi and colleagues (2014), suggests asking about suicide doesn’t put the idea in someone’s head; it can bring relief. You don’t have to be their therapist. Your job is to listen, stay with them, and help them reach trained support.

    For example (a coworker): A colleague says, “Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me.” Kirsty says gently: “I’m really glad you told me. Are you thinking about ending your life?” She says she’s had thoughts. Kirsty stays with her and they call a crisis line together.

    Words to try “Are you thinking about suicide?” “I’m glad you told me. You don’t have to go through this alone. Let’s call someone together.”

    One small step. Save a crisis number in your phone now, so you have it if you ever need it. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines. If someone is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

    Dig deeper: helping in a crisis

  8. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something going wrong, each one can assume someone else will deal with it. Psychologists call this the bystander effect, and knowing about it helps you break it. Small, safe actions count. “Are you okay?” Fetching a member of staff.

    On a train out of Sheffield, a man was making a young woman uncomfortable. Leaning in. Asking where she was getting off. Yvonne didn’t confront him. She sat down in the seat next to the woman and said brightly, “Hiya! Is this the train to Leeds?” The woman grabbed the conversation with both hands. They talked about Leeds, which neither of them knew much about, until the next stop, when the man got up and moved down the carriage.

    It helps to decide in advance what your safe move would be, distracting or getting help or checking in afterwards. People with a plan act. People without one tend to freeze.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  9. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways, and people who are good at giving help are often hopeless at receiving it. Letting someone help is a gift to them as well, because most people like being useful to someone they care about. And relationships where only one person ever helps start to feel lopsided.

    After the baby came, Nkechi said “We’re fine, thanks” to every single offer, including several when she very much wasn’t. When she finally said yes to the woman next door, who had offered a lasagne four times, the neighbour was so pleased she nearly dropped it. They walk their buggies round the park together on Thursday mornings now. Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage.

    Words you could use “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

    Dig deeper: receiving help

Your part, and only your part. You can offer help and listen well, and be honest about your limits. You can’t make someone accept help or get better on your timetable. Their life and their choices are theirs. If someone is in danger, your part is to bring in help, not to carry it alone.

In everyday life

Helping yourself counts too, and you can ask yourself the same question, ideas or rest. Caring for a parent, or a child with extra needs, is long-haul help, so share the load early (see Caring for someone you love). The friend everyone rings needs someone to ring too. Covering for a struggling coworker is kind for a week, and after that it needs a proper conversation with the team. In an emergency, point to one person (“You in the blue coat, please call for help”), because a crowd tends to wait. If you have ADHD, helping can feel interesting and urgent, so a fast yes comes easily, and “I’d like to help. Let me check my week and come back to you tonight” buys you time. Common mix-ups

People think helping means solving the problem. Often it means listening, or being there, or helping someone find their own answer. People think good helpers never say no, when it’s knowing their limits that lets good helpers last. Some people worry that asking about suicide might give someone the idea. Research suggests it doesn’t, and asking can help someone open up. And “I’ll make it worse if I step in” stops a lot of people. There are small, safe ways to help, and doing nothing often feels worse to the person in trouble.

If you want to help someone else with this

The helpers around you need looking after too. Notice them, the carer, the friend everyone rings, the coworker who always covers, and ask how they are as if you mean it. Offer something specific. “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is far easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.” Give them permission to rest (“You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this”). And if a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love or suggest they talk to a doctor.

Ways to say it “You look after everyone. Who looks after you?” “I’d love to do something for you this week. What would help?”

When to get more help

  • If someone is in danger now, call your local emergency number.
  • If someone talks about suicide, stay with them and contact a crisis line together. In the US and Canada, call or text 988. In the UK and Ireland, call Samaritans free on 116 123. In Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14. In New Zealand, call or text 1737. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com lists free helplines.
  • If someone tells you they’re being hurt or controlled at home, believe them, don’t confront the other person yourself, and share Not safe at home?
  • If you’re burned out as a helper or carer, talk to your doctor, a carers’ organisation or a counsellor. Respite and support are not luxuries.
  • If helping someone is becoming unsafe for you, step back and bring in professionals. You can still care from a safer distance.
