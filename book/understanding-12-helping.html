Helping others well: support without fixing, boundaries, and not burning out

Paperback textRead the expanded version

How to help someone without taking over, by asking what they need, listening first, caring without drowning and keeping kind limits, and how to keep going without burning out.

In the paperbacks: Book 3, Understanding Each Other, Chapter 7.

At a glance

  • Ask what kind of help they want. “Ideas, or just someone to listen?” saves a lot of misfires.
  • Listening is help. Fixing a problem for someone can take away their power.
  • Your limits make your help last, and caring from a steady place helps more than drowning in their feelings.

Wednesday, 7:15pm

Your friend is telling you about her awful week. Before she’s finished her first sentence you have five good ideas. You give her all five, in order, with reasons.

“Yeah,” she says. “Maybe.”

You both hang up feeling slightly worse, and neither of you could say why.

Most of us want to help the people around us, and helping well is a skill, because good intentions don’t always land. This chapter looks at how support works, why fixing can backfire, and how to care for people in a way you can keep up. Two tools elsewhere grew out of it. One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II), because caregivers burn out when everything counts as “just helping” and nobody else owns a job. And How much are you carrying? (Part Two, Chapter III), which helps you notice you’re running on empty before you snap or stop.

A friend’s awful week, in quick strokes. The surface is a phone call. Underneath, two kinds of help on offer and only one wanted. Underneath that, how much each of you has left. The roots are ownership and structure, and limited capacity. The chapter slows down on both.

  1. Ask what kind of help they want.

    Help comes in kinds. Some people want ideas. Some want someone to listen. Some want a practical hand, a meal or a ride, and some just want company on the couch, and giving the wrong kind, however kindly, can leave someone feeling unheard.

    That phone call could have gone differently. “That sounds like a rotten week. Do you want ideas, or do you just want to have a rant?” It feels a bit formal the first few times you say it, like reading off a card, but people tend to love being asked.

    Dig deeper: kinds of support

  2. Listening is a kind of help.

    Being properly heard helps people calm down and think, and quite often they find their own answer. Good listening means paying attention, letting them finish, checking you understood, and showing that their feelings make sense even if you’d feel differently.

    Matching the help to the problem Cutrona and Russell proposed that support works best when it fits the problem: practical help for things that can be changed, comfort for things that can’t. Later studies back this in part.

    Cutrona and Russell (1990) · Moderate: widely cited; later studies support it in part

    Ravi came home furious about his boss. His partner had “Just quit” ready on her tongue and kept it there. “So he went off at you in front of everyone,” she said, “after you’d stayed late all week. No wonder you’re livid.” Ravi’s shoulders came down about two inches. Ten minutes later he was sketching out a plan on the back of a takeout menu. It was a better plan than “just quit,” which he’d have hated being told anyway.

    In one conversation today, listen for two whole minutes without giving advice, then sum up what you heard. Two minutes is longer than you think.

  3. Fixing it for them can take away their power.

    When we love someone we want the problem gone. But doing things for people that they could do themselves can leave them feeling less capable, so helping well often means helping someone do it themselves. It’s their life, and their choice.

    Rosalind’s son is twenty-four and struggling with a job application for the city, and she is itching to rewrite it. She can see three things wrong with the first paragraph alone. She sits on her hands. “Would you like me to read it and say what stands out?” He makes the changes himself, some of them, not the ones she’d have made. When he gets the interview he’s quietly, enormously proud, and so is she, and she doesn’t mention the paragraph.

    Dig deeper: support without taking over

  4. Care without drowning in their feelings.

    Feeling someone’s pain as if it were yours can wear you out fast. Researchers separate this “empathic distress” from compassion, which is warm concern from a steadier place, still caring deeply without sinking alongside the other person. A steady helper is more use than a second person drowning.

    When Abi’s roommate went through a breakup, Abi cried with her every night, stopped sleeping, and was a wreck by the end of the first week. So she changed tack. Still kind, still there. But a run in the mornings, and Friday evening with her own friends, and a quiet reminder to herself, roughly hourly, that this was her roommate’s pain to feel and hers to keep company with.

    Dig deeper: empathy and compassion

  5. Your limits make your help last.

    Saying yes to everything leads, eventually, to resentment, exhaustion and not being able to help at all. Limits tell people what you can actually give, so your yes means something. And a kind no that comes with an offer of what you can do is still help.

    Kojo’s sister keeps asking to borrow money, fifty here and eighty there, and none of it comes back. He loves her and he’s running out, of money and of something else. On the phone one evening, with the TV on mute, he says it. “I can’t lend money anymore. I can help you go through your bills on Sunday, if you want.” There’s a silence long enough for him to hear her kettle click off. “Fine,” she says, not fine. On Sunday she turns up with a grocery bag full of envelopes.

    Ways to say it “I can’t do that, but I can do this.” “I want to help, and I need to stop at nine tonight.”

    If you need words for a “not this time,” Say no kindly helps you make your own, with something to offer instead.

  6. Notice when you’re running on empty.

    People who help a lot can burn out: caregivers, parents, nurses, teachers, the friend everyone turns to. The signs include feeling exhausted most of the time, or numb and cynical about the people you help, or dreading the next call, and any of them means you need a hand too.

    Gwen has looked after her husband since his stroke two years ago. Last month she snapped at him over the remote control, really snapped, and felt terrible for the rest of the day. That night she told her sister, at the kitchen table, “I think I’m running on empty.” Her sister took Tuesday mornings from then on, and Gwen walked to the end of the road and back, alone, on purpose. She has, she says, about a third of herself back.

    Rate your energy from 1 to 10 each evening for a week. If it’s often 3 or lower, ask someone for one specific kind of help. See Caring for someone you love.

    Dig deeper: burnout and refilling

  7. Don’t wait for someone else to step in.

    When lots of people see something going wrong, each one can assume someone else will deal with it. Psychologists call this the bystander effect, and knowing about it helps you break it. Small, safe actions count. “Are you okay?” Getting a staff member.

    On a train out of Philadelphia, a man was making a young woman uncomfortable, leaning in and asking where she was getting off. Yvonne didn’t confront him. She sat down in the seat next to the woman and said brightly, “Hi! Is this the train to Chicago?” The woman grabbed the conversation with both hands. They talked about Chicago, which neither of them knew much about, until the next stop, when the man got up and moved down the car.

    It helps to decide in advance what your safe move would be, distracting or getting help or checking in afterward. A plan makes it easier to act, and people without one are more likely to freeze.

    Sometimes the person in trouble is someone you know well. If someone tells you they’re thinking of ending their life, or you’re worried they might be, it’s okay to ask them plainly.

    Dig deeper: helping strangers and community

  8. Let other people help you too.

    Helping goes both ways, and people who are good at giving help are often hopeless at receiving it. Letting someone help is a gift to them as well, because most people like being useful to someone they care about. And relationships where only one person ever helps start to feel lopsided.

    People say yes more than we think In a set of experiments and field studies, people asking for help underestimated, by as much as half, how many others would agree, mainly because they forgot how awkward it is to say no. Later work finds the effect varies with how you ask (in person or in writing).

    Flynn and Lake (2008) · Moderate: several experiments and field studies; the size of the effect varies with how you ask

    After the baby came, Nkechi said “We’re fine, thanks” to every single offer, including several when she very much wasn’t. When she finally said yes to the woman next door, who had offered a lasagna four times, the neighbor was so pleased she nearly dropped it. They walk their strollers around the park together on Thursday mornings now. Say yes to the next small offer of help you get, even if you could manage.

    Words you could use “Yes, thank you. That would really help.” “Could you do one thing for me this week?”

    Dig deeper: receiving help

Your part, and only your part. You can offer help and listen well, and be honest about your limits. You can’t make someone accept help or get better on your schedule. Their life and their choices are theirs.

Two ways to read this

Help, two ways

Some people want ideas the moment they share a problem. Others want to be heard and nothing more, and an idea offered too soon feels like being corrected. If you fix, the listener isn’t ungrateful. They wanted company first. If you need to be heard, the fixer isn’t dismissing you. Ideas are how they show they care. Asking “Ideas, or just listening?” saves both of you an evening.

Where the fields meet. Business explains the bystander moment exactly: work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, so one person has to own it. Holistic therapies add the other half, which is to start with the body, because a helper who never rests stops being able to help. Some of these practices are well studied and some are debated.

In everyday life

Helping yourself counts too, and you can ask yourself the same question, ideas or rest. Caring for a parent, or a child with extra needs, is long-haul help, so share the load early (see Caring for someone you love). The friend everyone leans on needs someone to lean on too. Covering for a struggling coworker is kind for a week, and after that it needs a real conversation with the team. In an emergency, point to one person (“You in the blue coat, please get help”), because a crowd tends to wait. If you have ADHD, helping can feel interesting and urgent, so a fast yes comes easily, and “I’d like to help. Let me check my week and come back to you tonight” buys you time. Caring a lot, or losing yourself?

People sometimes call a devoted helper “codependent.” Codependency is not a diagnosis, and researchers debate how to define it, so it’s worth being careful with it.

For something to be caring that has tipped into losing yourself, these are usually all present:

  • your mood rises and falls with the other person’s, most days,
  • you can’t say no to them, even when you want to,
  • you keep covering for choices that hurt them or you,
  • your own needs, friends and rest have quietly disappeared for months.

Not enough on its own:

  • caring a lot about someone,
  • helping a partner or parent through a hard year,
  • being the person who organizes the family,
  • a culture or family where people come first and look after each other.

If you recognize yourself in the first list, the aim is to take some of yourself back, not to care less. One protected evening a week is a start.

Common mix-ups

People think helping means solving the problem. Often it means listening, or being there, or helping someone find their own answer. People think good helpers never say no, when it’s knowing their limits that lets good helpers last. And “I’ll make it worse if I step in” stops a lot of people. There are small, safe ways to help, and doing nothing often feels worse to the person in trouble.

If you want to help someone else with this

The helpers around you need looking after too. Notice them, the caregiver, the friend everyone turns to, the coworker who always covers, and ask how they are as if you mean it. Offer something specific, because “Can I take the kids on Saturday morning?” is far easier to accept than “Let me know if you need anything.” Give them permission to rest (“You’re allowed a night off. I’ve got this”). And if a helper seems burned out, share Caring for someone you love. Sometimes it’s as simple as asking them, “You look after everyone. Who looks after you?”
