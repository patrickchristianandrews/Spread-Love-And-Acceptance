Why we get out of tune

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.” “I know, I’m just asking when.” Four sentences later, nobody is talking about the trash anymore, and neither of you can point to the moment it turned.

A picture, not a measurement. Nothing in this chapter records, analyzes or scores anyone’s real voice. It borrows an image from radio to explain why calm, kind people can sound harsh to each other when they’re under stress.

This chapter looks at everyday clashes in a different way: not as one person attacking the other, but as two signals that are slightly out of tune, like two radios on nearly the same station.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Drawn from psychology and neurobiology, with a picture borrowed from radio.)

  • In you: Know your own pace, your usual tone and what “soon” means to you. Notice when stress speeds you up or slows you down.
  • Between you and others: When a clash starts, treat it as a tuning problem, not a moral failing. Retune together, then translate what you meant into words the other person can hear.

The radio whistle

Tune two radios to stations that are close but not the same, and you don’t get silence. You get a whistle. The two signals overlap just enough to interfere, and they make a third, uglier sound that neither radio was sending. Engineers call this a heterodyne, or a “beat”: the extra tone that appears when two signals are almost, but not quite, in step.

This book borrows that picture for a familiar moment. Two people each say something reasonable. Each one’s tone is shaped by their own tiredness, hurry or stress. Together they get an argument that neither of them meant to start. The words weren’t the problem. The mismatch was.

The reasoning. If you think the other person attacked you, you defend. If you think two signals crossed, you check. The picture of a whistle gives you a third option between “you started it” and “I started it”: it started, between us.

Worked examples

  • Two mums at breakfast: “Have you seen my keys?” (rushed) meets “Where you left them, I expect” (tired). Neither line is cruel, but together they sound like a row. A laugh and “Sorry, that came out wrong” clears it.
  • A coworker pair: “Can you send that over?” in a team chat, with no greeting, lands as cold. The sender was typing on a phone in a lift. See At work.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “The words were fine, so it’s their problem.” The words may be fine. The clash lives in the mix, so both of you can help clear it.
  • Arguing about the exact words. “I said ‘can you’, not ‘you should’” rarely helps. Talk about how it came across instead.

If only one of you is on board: You can still notice the whistle and lower your own pace and volume. One radio moving closer to the other already cuts the static.

Words you could use: “That came out sharper than I meant.” “I think we just crossed wires.”

Research notes

The whistle is real physics: two close frequencies combine into a “beat” at the difference between them. Using it for people is this site’s idea, a picture, not a measurement. Psychologists Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec (1998) described the “illusion of transparency”: we tend to think our inner states and intentions are more obvious to others than they really are. So the sender often assumes the kindness was clear.

Why being nearly in step is the noisy part

The pitch of that radio whistle depends on the gap between the two stations. When the gap shrinks to nothing, the whistle disappears. When two stations are very far apart, the two signals don’t mix into anything you can hear.

People can work the same way. Strangers who barely interact rarely clash. People who share a home, a family or a job are close enough to overlap all day. That closeness is a good thing, and it also means small differences in pace or tone rub against each other again and again. A clash between two close people usually doesn’t mean they are badly matched. Often it means they are nearly matched, and just slightly off. Research also suggests we check our meaning less with people we know well, because we assume they already understand us.

The reasoning. This flips a painful thought. “We clash, so we must be wrong for each other” becomes “We clash because we are close, and we skip the checking.” The fix isn’t distance. It’s a little more checking.

Worked examples

  • Best friends since school: They finish each other’s sentences, so they never check. One says “Let’s do something for your birthday,” meaning a meal; the other expects a weekend away. See Friends.
  • A grandparent and a grown-up child: A father says, “I’ll have the kids Saturday, as usual.” His daughter had planned a family day and feels her plans were taken over. They are so used to each other that nobody asked. See For grown-up children.
  • A couple together for decades: With the children gone, they are home together more, and small differences in pace rub more often. See Empty nest and Retired together.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “If they really knew me, I wouldn’t have to explain.” Knowing someone well makes us explain less, which is exactly when misreadings creep in. Explaining is care, not a sign of distance.
  • “We used to be in sync, so something is broken.” Lives change: new jobs, new babies, new hours. Drift is normal. Retune to the new life.

If only one of you is on board: Check your own meaning out loud more often, especially with the people closest to you. It costs a sentence and saves an evening.

Words you could use: “I know we usually just get each other, but let me check: did you mean…?”

Research notes

Kenneth Savitsky, Boaz Keysar, Nicholas Epley and colleagues (2011) found what they called the “closeness-communication bias”: people overestimated how well they communicated with friends and spouses, in some tests not doing better than with strangers. That is the finding behind this section.

What sets the frequency

Three things tend to shift how someone comes across, often without them noticing:

  • Pace. How fast you talk, how quickly you reply, and how long a pause feels. Someone who is tense may talk faster, jump in sooner, and read an ordinary pause as the silent treatment.
  • Tone. A comment meant as a simple update (“the sink’s full again”) can be sent, or heard, as a complaint. It depends on the tone underneath it.
  • Urgency. One person’s “when you get a chance” can mean tonight. Another person’s can mean next week. Neither is wrong, but they aren’t the same.

Here is how the same sentence can be sent one way and heard another:

The reasoning. Pace, tone and urgency are the parts of a message that aren’t in the words, so they’re the parts most likely to differ. Naming them gives you three quick questions to check, instead of one big, vague “Why are you being like this?”

Worked examples

  • Interfaith or cross-cultural families: In Amal’s family, people talk over each other to show they care. In Ben’s family, interrupting is rude. At Sunday lunch, Ben hears a fight and Amal hears a lively chat. Neither family is wrong; their pace is set differently. See Two faiths, one family.
  • Gaming and screen time: “Coming!” from a teenager in the middle of an online match means “when the round ends,” which might be ten minutes. To the parent at the table, it meant now. Agreeing what “coming” means ends a nightly fight. See Gaming and time together.
  • Shift workers: A text sent at 3am by a night-shift nurse (“Can we sort the bins?”) arrives to a partner just waking up, and feels urgent. It wasn’t. A rule helps: “Non-urgent texts go in one morning message.” See Different hours.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “My tone was fine.” Maybe it was, to you. Ask how it landed rather than defending how it was sent.
  • “‘When you get a chance’ is obvious.” It isn’t. Add a time.
  • “Texting is safer.” Texts are calmer to send but easier to misread. For anything tender, add a feeling word, or call.

If only one of you is on board: Fix your own sending first. Add a time to your requests, say “this is just an update” before updates, and slow your own pace. One clearer signal already cuts the noise.

Words you could use: “Is this a now thing, a today thing, or a this-week thing?” “I’m not upset, I’m just in a hurry.”

Research notes

Justin Kruger, Nicholas Epley and colleagues (2005) found that people sending emails were too confident their tone (such as sarcasm or seriousness) would be read correctly. Anthropologist Edward T. Hall described “high-context” and “low-context” communication styles (1976): in some families and cultures, much of the meaning is in tone and setting; in others, it is in the exact words. This site’s idea: the three dials of pace, tone and urgency.

How stress changes your frequency

How your body feels changes how you sound and how you hear. The site describes three everyday states, on what it calls the Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check. It gives you a best guess, not a reading:

  • Calm and connected. Breathing is easy. You can hear something hard without getting defensive.
  • Revved up. You feel tense and fast. Your heart is quicker and thoughts move fast. Small things feel urgent or personal. Pace speeds up, and tone gets sharper.
  • Running on empty. You feel tired and flat, and words are harder to find. Replies get slower and shorter, which the other person may read as not caring.

These labels come from polyvagal theory, whose main claims are still debated by scientists. Here they are only everyday words, not a medical model. “Running on empty” just means ordinary, everyday tiredness. The point is simple: when you are revved up or running on empty, you are more likely to send and hear signals off-key. Chapter III goes deeper into how much stress you bring into a conversation from elsewhere.

The reasoning. The same words can be easy on a calm day and impossible on a tired one. If you check your state first, you can choose to wait, to warn (“I’m low, so I’ll be brief”), or to go ahead. If you skip it, your state chooses for you.

Worked examples

  • A partner with a long-term illness: On high-pain days, Jo’s replies get short. Her partner learned to read short replies as “pain,” not “cross,” and to ask, “Is this a low day?” See When one of you is ill.
  • A foster carer and a child: A child who has been through a lot may jump straight to “revved up” at a raised voice. The carer lowers their own voice and slows down first, before any talk about the broken plate. See Foster, adoptive and kinship families.
  • A parent and a child with ADHD: At 5pm, after a long school day, both are running on empty. They move homework talk to after a snack and ten minutes outside. See When a child has ADHD.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “You’re revved up” as a put-down. The ladder is for checking yourself. Don’t use it to label someone else.
  • “I’m always running on empty, so I can never talk.” If that’s true for weeks, it’s a sign to rest and get support, not to stop talking forever. Talk briefly, at your best time of day.
  • “Calm means I’m not allowed to be upset.” Calm and connected doesn’t mean happy. You can be calm and sad, or calm and firm.

If only one of you is on board: Check your own state and say it out loud. “I’m running on empty, so I’ll keep this short” changes how your words land, even if the other person never uses the ladder.

Words you could use: “I’m revved up right now. Give me ten minutes and I’ll be easier to talk to.”

A note on safety: If you are tense because you are afraid of how someone will react, that is not just stress. Your body may be warning you. Not safe at home? has free, private help.

Research notes

Sleep researchers in Matthew Walker’s lab (2007) found that after a night without sleep, the brain’s alarm center (the amygdala) reacted much more strongly to upsetting pictures. John Gottman’s research on couples described “flooding”: feeling so overwhelmed in a conflict that listening becomes very hard. As noted above, the three labels here are everyday words, not a medical model.

Nobody has to be wrong

None of this needs either person to be at fault. Two radios that both work perfectly will still whistle if they are slightly off. No amount of arguing about who’s “really” to blame fixes the interference. Retuning does.

Zhuangzi’s Empty Boat makes the same point. A man in a boat is angry when another boat bumps into his, until he sees that it is empty. With no one to blame, his anger goes. Much of the time, the bump was no one’s plan. That doesn’t mean a bump never matters. It means starting with curiosity instead of blame.

People are also wired differently. Some notice every shift in tone. Others take words very literally. Wired Differently explains how a mix-up between two differently wired people usually runs both ways.

The reasoning. Blame narrows attention to one person. “No fault” widens it to the setup: the time of day, the tiredness, the unclear words. Once the setup is in view, there is usually something easy to change.

Worked examples

  • A neurodivergent and a neurotypical partner: One partner says exactly what they mean. The other listens for hints. “It’s fine” means “it’s fine” to one, and “it’s not fine” to the other. Both are reading carefully, just in different ways. See Wired Differently.
  • Separated co-parents: A message about pickup times turns into a row about the past. They agree that pickup messages are facts only, and feelings go in a separate monthly call. See Co-parenting.
  • Someone coming out to family: After Leah comes out, her mother asks, “Are you sure?” Leah hears doubt; her mother meant fear for her. Naming that both were scared helps them start again. See Coming out as an adult.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • “No fault means nothing matters.” It means start with curiosity. If something hurtful keeps happening after it’s been named, that matters, and it is fair to say so.
  • “If I don’t blame them, I must blame me.” No. Neither. Look at the setup.

If only one of you is on board: You can drop blame on your side, even if the other person doesn’t. “I don’t think you meant that the way I heard it” often softens things quickly.

Words you could use: “I don’t think you meant that the way I heard it. Can you tell me what you meant?”

A note on safety: “Nobody has to be wrong” is for ordinary clashes. It never means excusing threats, put-downs or fear. If someone frightens you, it is not a tuning problem. Not safe at home?

Research notes

Psychologist Lee Ross named the “fundamental attribution error” (1977): we tend to blame someone’s character for what was really caused by their situation. Damian Milton (2012) described the “double empathy problem”: when autistic and non-autistic people misunderstand each other, the gap runs both ways. Zhuangzi’s Empty Boat is a story, not a study.

Retuning, step by step

Before you carry on with a conversation that has started to whistle:

  1. Name the mismatch out loud, without blame. “I think we’re on different wavelengths right now.” This turns “you against me” into “us against the static.”
  2. Check your own state on the Nervous System Ladder. It’s hard to retune while you are revved up or running on empty. If you are, the Calm-Down Kit (WP-11), a simple plan for heated moments, comes first. Either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.
  3. Slow the pace on purpose. Take shorter turns. Leave more pauses. Lower your voice. Keep going like this until the static clears.
  4. Only then, go back to the topic. The topic still matters. You are just coming back to it when you can both hear properly.

The reasoning. Each step does one job. Naming it stops the spiral. Checking your state tells you whether you can go on. Slowing down lowers the heat. Going back to the topic keeps retuning from turning into avoiding.

Worked examples

  • Housemates: A row about noise at midnight. One says, “We’re both tired. Can we talk about this at breakfast?” At breakfast, it takes five minutes. See Sharing a room.
  • Three partners: When a talk gets heated, the third partner gently says, “Static,” and all three take a pause. They agreed the word on a calm day, and anyone can use it. See More than two partners at home.
  • Long-distance partners: A video call drops mid-argument and each assumes the other hung up. Now they have a rule: if the line drops in a hard talk, text “line dropped, still here” and call back after ten minutes. See Long distance.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Pausing without a time to return. That feels like being shut out. Always name a time.
  • Coming back too soon. If you’re still revved up, take longer. A few minutes is often not enough.
  • Retuning only when you are losing. Use the pause early, when you notice the static, not as an escape hatch.

If only one of you is on board: You can still pause yourself: “I need a few minutes so I can listen properly. I’ll be back at eight.” Then come back at eight.

Words you could use: “I want to hear you properly, and I can’t right now. Can we pick this up at eight?”

A note on safety: Anyone can call a pause, and nobody should be blocked from leaving the room. If you are followed, blocked or punished for pausing, that is a safety issue, not a tuning one. Signs to look out for.

Research notes

John Gottman’s work on couples suggests taking a real break when flooded, often at least twenty minutes, and doing something soothing rather than rehearsing the argument. Matthew Lieberman and colleagues (2007) found that putting feelings into words (“affect labeling”) can lower the brain’s alarm response, which may be part of why naming the mismatch helps. This site’s idea: the four steps in this order.

A worked example: the trash, replayed

Here’s the Tuesday scene again, slowed down.

Alex is tired and has a late shift tonight. Sam is tired and worried because the trash goes out first thing tomorrow. Sam asks, “Are you taking out the trash?” The words are fine. But Sam’s urgency is set to “tonight,” and Alex’s is set to “before bed.” Sam is revved up, so the question comes out quickly. Alex hears the speed as nagging and says, “I said I’d do it.” Sam hears the snap and says, “I’m just asking when.” Now both are defending themselves, and the trash is forgotten.

Now the retuned version. After Alex’s snap, Sam pauses and says, “I think we’re out of tune. I’m a bit tense about tomorrow.” Alex takes a breath: “Me too, long day.” They slow down. Sam goes back in with one clear sentence: “The trash needs to be out by 7am tomorrow. I’ll feel calmer if I know it’s out tonight. Could you take it out before bed?” Alex says yes, and names a time. The whole thing takes under a minute.

Two more replays

Adult siblings planning a parent’s birthday: Priya messages her brother, “We need to decide about Mum’s party.” Arun, who lives far away and feels left out of decisions, hears “you’re late again.” He replies, “Do whatever you want.” Retuned: Priya calls instead. “I think the message came out wrong. I really want your ideas. Could we decide by Sunday?” Arun says he’d love to do the cake. See Family rifts.

Two dads and a toddler at bedtime: Chris (revved up, late for a call) says, “Can you just do bath tonight?” Malik (running on empty) hears “you never do bath.” Malik snaps. Retuned: Chris says, “Sorry, I’m rushing. I’ve got a call at seven. Could you do bath, and I’ll do the morning?” A fact, a reason, an offer. Malik says yes.

Going back in: say it so it lands

When you return to the topic, it helps to have one clear sentence ready. WP-09, Say it so it lands, sorts what you want to say into three parts:

  • The fact: one sentence, with no blame words. “The trash wasn’t out before pickup.”
  • The feeling: what’s underneath. “I felt a bit on my own with it.”
  • The ask: what you would like to happen next. “Could we pick a fixed night for it?”

If your sentence starts with “you always” or “you never,” pause and swap it for one fact and one ask. The Signal Translator can show you how a sentence might land before you say it.

The reasoning. After a clash, both people are still a bit raw. A clear sentence gives them something solid to hold: one fact to agree on, one feeling to understand, one ask to say yes or no to.

Worked examples

  • Business partners: “The invoice to Patel & Co went out three weeks late. I felt worried about cash flow. Could we set a reminder for the 1st of each month?”
  • A teen to a parent: “You read my messages on Tuesday. I felt really embarrassed. Could you ask me first next time?” See For teens.
  • A grown-up child to a parent: “You gave the baby sweets after I said no. I felt overruled. Could you check with me about food?” See For grown-up children.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Packing in three facts and two asks. One of each. Save the rest for another day.
  • A feeling that is really a blame (“I feel you don’t care”). Use a feeling word about you: worried, alone, rushed, hurt.
  • An ask that is really a rule (“You will…”). An ask can get a no. If it can’t, it isn’t an ask.

If only one of you is on board: A clear sentence works even if the other person doesn’t know the method. You don’t have to explain it. Just say it.

Words you could use: “Here’s what happened, how I felt, and what would help.”

Research notes

Sybil Carrère and John Gottman (1999) found that the first few minutes of a couple’s conflict conversation, gentle or harsh, predicted a lot about how things went for them later. The fact, feeling, ask pattern is close to well-known methods such as “I-statements” (from Thomas Gordon’s work in the 1970s) and Marshall Rosenberg’s Nonviolent Communication, which uses observation, feeling, need and request.

Staying in tune: small and often

Radios drift. So do people. Habit research suggests that small routines tied to a regular cue, like the same time each day, are easier to keep than big efforts made rarely. That hasn’t been proven for couples, but it is a sensible way to stay in tune: tiny corrections often, instead of one big correction after weeks of drift.

The quiet stretches drift most. When things have been fine for a long time, nobody listens for the whistle, and a small offset can grow for months without a sound. That is complacency: nothing breaks on the day the checking stops. A tiny, regular correction is the cure, and it works best when each of you can add a little context about your own signal (how the day went for you), so a flat reply is read as “tired,” not “cold.”

That’s the idea behind WP-13, the 90-second daily check-in. The idea comes from electronics, where a small circuit keeps one signal in step with another by making tiny, constant corrections. Once a day, each of you says how much you were carrying today, one thing you appreciated, and, if you like, one small thing from your day that didn’t feel great (about the day, not about each other) and one thing that would help tomorrow. Either of you can pass, any day. You just listen. Nothing gets solved there.

If you want to see whether retuning is becoming a habit, the full chore-split calculator (CALC-01) has a “retuning frequency”: the number of moments that got retuned, divided by the number of moments that landed badly. For example, if five things landed badly this week and you retuned three of them before answering, that’s 3 ÷ 5 = 0.60. It isn’t a grade. It only shows whether repair is happening more often over time. If nothing landed badly this week, leave it blank. With numbers this small, watch the trend over several weeks, not any one week.

The reasoning. Big talks are hard to schedule and easy to dread. A tiny daily one costs almost nothing, and it catches drift while it’s still small enough to fix with one sentence.

Worked examples

  • Parents on opposite shifts: They leave a 90-second voice note at handover: how full their tank is, and one thing the other should know. See Different hours.
  • Friends who live far apart: A Sunday “rose and thorn” message: one good thing, one hard thing from the week.
  • Someone returning home after time away: For the first two weeks back, a short evening check-in helps them learn the new rhythm of the house. See Coming home.

Common mix-ups, and how to fix them

  • Turning the check-in into a problem-solving session. Just listen. Save fixes for the weekly catch-up.
  • Dropping it when things are good. The good stretches are when drift starts. Keep it tiny so it survives.
  • Making it a duty. Either person can pass, any day, no questions asked.

If only one of you is on board: Do a check-in with yourself: how full is my tank, what did I appreciate, what would help tomorrow. You can also simply share your own line each day and let the other person join in if they like.

Words you could use: “Quick check-in? Mine: medium tank today, and I liked that you made coffee.”

Research notes

A 2010 study led by Phillippa Lally found that new daily habits took, on average, around two months to feel automatic, with a wide range from person to person, and that missing a single day didn’t undo progress. Gottman’s research on “bids” (small moments of reaching out) and turning toward them is linked to how couples stay close. See Turning toward.

Your part, and only your part

You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace, tone and urgency, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow.

  • Learn your early signs of drifting: a tight chest, a clipped reply, wanting to win.
  • Learn how you take words in best, and say it once: “I hear things better in writing.”
  • When you drift, step out, settle and come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission to retune yourself.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, both people push on. Each tries to be understood before trying to understand. Voices rise, the topic changes three times, and later neither can remember how it started. The next time the same topic comes up, both already feel tense.

When it goes right, one person notices the static early and names it kindly. Both slow down. The topic comes back a few minutes later, or at a time you agree on, with one clear sentence. Next time, the phrase “we’re out of tune” works a little faster, because you both know what it means.

Questions people often ask

Isn’t “we’re out of sync” just a way of avoiding the subject?

It can be, if it’s used to end every hard talk. That’s why the retuning steps always finish by going back to the topic, and why a pause always comes with a time to return.

What if only one of us wants to retune?

You can still retune yourself. Slow your own pace, lower your own voice, and say your own sentence clearly. One radio moving closer to the other already cuts the static.

What if we keep getting out of tune about the same thing?

Then it’s probably not just a tuning problem. Repeated clashes about one job often mean it has no clear owner: one person who does it, start to finish. Chapter IV and One owner per job (WP-03) help with that, and Chapter V shows how to spot the pattern.

Does this work over text?

Partly. Text strips out tone, so it is easy to drift. Keep texts for facts and times, add a feeling word if something is tender, and move to a call or a face-to-face talk if two messages in a row land badly.

What about with my teenager, or a child?

Children and teens drift faster when they’re tired, hungry or worried, and they rely on you to settle first. Lower your own voice, shorten your sentences, and come back to the topic after a snack or some sleep. For teens and When a child has ADHD have more.

What if we speak different first languages?

Then pace and tone differ even more, and so do words like “soon.” Check meanings often and kindly, and assume good intent first. Two faiths, one family has ideas for mixed families.

Limits and cautions

  • This is a picture to think with. It doesn’t measure anyone’s voice, and it can’t prove who said what.
  • Retuning helps with ordinary clashes. It isn’t meant to hide a real disagreement. Once you’re back in tune, the disagreement still deserves a proper talk.
  • Use it on yourself first. “You’re out of tune” said as an accusation is just another off-key signal. “I think we’re out of tune” works better.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor on your own.
