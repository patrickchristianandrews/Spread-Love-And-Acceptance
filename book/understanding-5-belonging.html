Attachment, trust and belonging

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why we need each other, how the bonds we had early on shape what we expect today, and how trust and belonging are built, lost and rebuilt, one small moment at a time.

In short:

  • Belonging is a basic need. Feeling lonely or left out is a signal, like hunger.
  • Early bonds set expectations that can change. What we learned about being cared for can soften over time.
  • Trust grows in small moments. Doing what you said, again and again, matters more than big gestures.
  • You can start small, with one friendly exchange or one kept promise.

Why the tools work. Two tools in other chapters rest on what this one explains.

  • One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) works because trust is built from small promises kept, and a named owner turns a vague hope that someone will do it into a promise others can rely on.
  • The monthly look-back (Part Two, Chapter V) looks at the month and leaves both people out of the dock, because feeling safe is what lets people say what went wrong without bracing for blame.

Sunday, 9:30pm

Kai sent a long message to a friend at lunchtime, and it has been read without a reply. By evening Kai has gone from thinking she is busy to thinking she is annoyed to wondering whether anybody really likes him. The friend, meanwhile, is at her nephew’s birthday party with her phone in her coat pocket.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out, it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That hurt works like hunger, telling us to reach for connection.

    For example: Rhea moves to a new city for work. She is busy and doing well, yet by the third month she feels flat and teary on Sundays, when the office is shut and nobody in the city knows her well enough to call.

    Thoughts you could try: “Feeling lonely says I need connection, like anyone does.”

    Try this: This week, reach out to one person, even if it is only a short “thinking of you” message.

    Dig deeper: why we need each other

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children, we learn from the people who cared for us whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder or to stop asking. These lessons are a starting point that later relationships can change. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    For example: A toddler at the park runs off to explore, runs back to check that his grandmother is still on the bench, and runs off again, braver each time because he knows where she is.

    Words you could use: (to a child) “I’m right here. Go and look, and come back whenever you want.”

    Try this: Think of one person who was a safe base for you, at any age, and what they did that helped.

    Dig deeper: what attachment is

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close, and need more space. Many of us feel mostly secure, and most of us mix all three.

    These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they work as sliding scales.

    For example: When things feel tense, Ronan wants to talk it through straight away while his wife Clara needs to go quiet first. He reads her quiet as a sign she is leaving, and she reads his questions as pressure. Once they see the pattern, they agree that Clara will say “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and that Ronan will wait.

    Words you could use: “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet. Let’s talk at eight.”

    Try this: Notice whether you reach out more or pull back when you feel unsure of someone, and leave judgement out of it.

    Dig deeper: anxious, avoidant and secure

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust rarely comes from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again, such as doing what you said, turning towards someone when they reach out, and being the same person on a hard day as on a good one.

    For example: A new team member says she will send the figures by Thursday, and she does. The next week she warns early that something will be late. By the third month her manager trusts her completely, and he would struggle to name a single moment when it happened.

    Words you could use: “I said I’d do it by Friday. I’m running late, so here’s where it’s at.”

    Try this: Keep one small promise today, and notice how it feels to be someone who does what they said.

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle our nervous system. In one study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. It works both ways, so we can be that safe presence for others too, often without saying much.

    For example: After a panic attack, Kieran’s friend doesn’t try to fix anything. He sits next to him on the step outside the flats and says, “Take your time. I’m here,” and they stay there until Kieran’s breathing slows.

    Words you could use: “You don’t have to talk. I’ll just sit with you.” “Would company help right now, or quiet?”

    Try this: Notice who helps you feel calmer, and tell them simply that you always feel better after seeing them.

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Groups like teams, families, clubs and communities can offer both. Being left out of a group hurts even when the group doesn’t matter much to us, and real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    For example: At a new job, Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny and hides the fact that she doesn’t drink, and she goes home exhausted every Friday. At her book club she says what she really thinks, and people ask her to say more.

    Words you could use: (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it okay if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    Try this: Think of one place where you can be fully yourself, and how you might spend a little more time there.

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It returns slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    For example: Two adult sisters fall out after one shares a private story at a family dinner. The apology is a start, and what slowly rebuilds trust is the months afterwards, when the story never comes up again and the sister checks before sharing anything.

    Words you could use: “I know sorry isn’t enough. I’ll show you, and you can take whatever time you need.”

    Try this: If you’ve broken someone’s trust, name one specific thing you’ll do differently, and do it.

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can feel out of reach, and belonging can start much smaller, with a chat with a neighbour, a regular class or an online group about something you love. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    For example: After his divorce, Martin starts saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall, and a few weeks later they talk football every morning. It is a small friendship, and it makes Mondays easier.

    Words you could use: “I’m new around here. Is there anything you’d recommend?” “I loved what you said in the group. Would you like to get a coffee sometime?”

    Try this: Have one small, friendly exchange with someone you don’t know well this week.

Monday, 8:10am

Kai’s phone buzzes with an apology. Her phone was in her coat all day at her nephew’s party, she loved the message, and is he free for coffee on Thursday? Kai thinks about how far last night’s story ran, and decides that next time a reply is slow he will send a light “No rush, just thinking of you” and leave it there.

If you’re a teenager

Ezra is sixteen when he sees a photo of his three closest friends at the cinema and finds out there is a group chat he isn’t in. It hurts like a punch, because being left out really does register a bit like pain. He drafts something sharp, deletes it and messages one friend instead, saying he’s a bit gutted not to be asked and asking whether he did something. It turns out they thought he would be at football. One of the friendships really is drifting, though, and that is allowed to hurt too.

Words you could use: “Bit gutted not to be asked. Did something happen?” “I’d like to come next time, even if I can’t always make it.” “I miss how we used to be. Are we okay?”

In everyday life

When one partner texts a lot while apart and the other forgets, agreeing on one goodnight message can keep both feeling connected. A child who acts up at pick-up may simply be letting go of a long day with the safest person they know. A friend who always cancels may be overwhelmed, and “Is it easier if we do something short?” keeps the door open. At work, a team where mistakes can be admitted without blame builds trust faster than one where everyone hides them, and in a shared house, one simple weekly meal can turn people who share a kitchen into people who look out for each other.

On your own: belonging with yourself

Belonging also includes how you treat yourself. If you are kind to yourself on a bad day, you carry a small safe base wherever you go, and people who learn to comfort themselves often find it easier to let others in, because they are less afraid of being let down. You are allowed to need people, and a lonely day is hard without being a forecast. On my own has more for people living alone.

Common mix-ups

  • “Needing people means I’m needy.” Everyone needs connection, including the people who hide it well.
  • “My attachment style is fixed for life.” Patterns can change, especially through steady, kind relationships and reflection.
  • “Jealousy and checking up show how much they love me.” Reaching for reassurance is human, and so is asking for something out of care for the relationship. Controlling who someone sees, or checking their phone without agreement, is not love and is not an attachment style. See signs to look out for.
  • “If I forgive, I have to trust again straight away.” Forgiving and trusting are different. Trust can return slowly, or not at all, and that’s your call.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Be a steady presence. Reliability matters more than big gestures. Turn up when you said you would.
  • Take loneliness seriously. If someone says they feel alone, listen before you remind them how many friends they have.
  • Invite, and keep inviting. People who feel left out may say no a few times, and a warm invitation with no pressure still matters.
  • Make room in groups by noticing who is on the edge and asking their view.
  • Leave labels out. Telling someone they are “so avoidant” or “so clingy” rarely helps, while asking what would help often does.

Words you could use: “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” “We’re having a few people over on Friday. No pressure, but I’d love you to come.”

When to get more help

  • If loneliness has lasted a long time, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of not wanting to be here, please talk to a doctor or a mental health service. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number or see Upset right now.
  • If fear of being left, or of getting close, keeps hurting your relationships, a counsellor or therapist can help you understand where it comes from and how to change it.
  • If someone uses your need for them against you, by cutting you off from friends, checking where you are, or threatening to leave to get their way, that is not about attachment. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
  • If trust was broken in a way that frightened you, you don’t owe anyone a second chance. Your safety comes first.
