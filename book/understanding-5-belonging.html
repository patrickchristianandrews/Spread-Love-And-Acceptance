Attachment, trust and belonging

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why we need each other, how the bonds we had early on shape what we expect today, and how trust and belonging are built and lost and built again, one small moment at a time.

In short

  • Belonging is a basic need. Feeling lonely or left out is a signal, like hunger.
  • Early bonds set expectations that can change. What we learned about being cared for can soften over time.
  • Trust grows in small moments. Doing what you said, again and again, matters more than big gestures.
  • You can start small, with one friendly exchange or one kept promise.

Sunday, 9:30pm

Kai sent his friend a long message at lunchtime. Four paragraphs about the new job, and a question at the end. It says Read 12:47.

Nothing.

By teatime he’s gone from “she’s busy” to “she’s annoyed”. By half nine he’s wondering whether anybody really likes him at all. The friend, meanwhile, is at her nephew’s sixth birthday at a soft-play centre in Stockport, and her phone is in her coat pocket under nineteen other coats.

Two tools elsewhere rest on this chapter. One owner per job (Part Two, Chapter II) works because trust is built from small promises kept, and a named owner turns “someone will probably do it” into a promise people can rely on. The monthly look-back (Part Two, Chapter V) looks at the month and keeps both people out of the dock, because feeling safe is what lets anyone say what went wrong.

  1. Belonging is a basic need.

    People need to feel connected, the way we need food and sleep. When we feel left out it can hurt in a way that feels almost physical (Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues, 2003). That hurt works like hunger. It’s telling you to reach for someone.

    Rhea moved to Manchester for work. She’s busy, doing well, likes the job. And by the third month she’s flat and teary every Sunday afternoon, when the office is shut and nobody in the whole city knows her well enough to ring. She found this embarrassing for a while. Then she joined a running club, which she hated, and a pub quiz team, which she loved.

    Things to tell yourself “Feeling lonely says I need connection, like anyone does.”

    Dig deeper: why we need each other

  2. Early bonds teach us what to expect.

    As children we learn, from the people who looked after us, whether others will come when we need them. If help usually came, we tend to expect it. If it came sometimes, or rarely, we may learn to cling harder, or stop asking. These lessons are a starting point that later relationships can change. This is the idea of attachment, first described by John Bowlby (1969) and studied by Mary Ainsworth and colleagues (1978).

    Watch a toddler at the park. He runs off towards the ducks. Runs back to check his nan is still on the bench. Off again, a bit further. Back. Further still, right to the edge of the pond, where he stands and shouts at a goose. He gets braver every time, because he knows exactly where she is.

    You could say (to a child) “I’m right here. Go and look, and come back whenever you want.”

    Dig deeper: what attachment is

  3. Patterns, not boxes.

    As adults, some of us worry a lot about being left, and reach out more when we feel unsure. Some of us pull back when things get close and need more room. Many of us feel mostly secure. Most of us mix all three, depending on the day and the person.

    These are patterns that can soften and change, especially in steady, kind relationships. Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) first described them in adults, and later work, such as R. Chris Fraley and colleagues (2015), suggests they work as sliding scales.

    When things get tense, Ronan wants to talk it through, now, immediately. His wife Clara needs to go quiet first, usually in the garden, usually with the secateurs. He reads her quiet as her leaving. She reads his questions through the back door as pressure. It took them nine years to see it. Now she says “I need an hour, and I’m coming back,” and he waits, which for about the first month he found almost unbearable. He has taken up bread.

    Words you could use “I’m not going anywhere. I just need some quiet. Let’s talk at eight.”

    Dig deeper: anxious, avoidant and secure

  4. Trust is built in small moments.

    Trust rarely comes from big gestures. It grows from small, ordinary things done again and again, like doing what you said, and being the same person on a hard day as on a good one. Honestly, it’s a bit boring. That’s why it works.

    The new starter said she’d send the figures by Thursday, and she did. The following week she warned on Monday that something would be late on Friday. By the third month her manager trusted her completely, and if you’d asked him when that happened he couldn’t have told you. It didn’t happen at any one moment. It happened in about forty small ones.

    Dig deeper: how trust grows

  5. Feeling safe with someone calms the body.

    Being with someone we trust can settle the nervous system. In one study, holding a trusted partner’s hand calmed the brain’s response to threat (James Coan and colleagues, 2006). A calm voice, a hand to hold, or just someone nearby can make a hard moment easier to bear. It works both ways, so we can be that for other people, often without saying much.

    After his panic attack, Kieran’s friend didn’t try to fix anything. He sat down next to him on the cold step outside the flats. “Take your time. I’m here.” Then nothing else, for about twenty minutes, while a man walked past twice with a very small dog. Eventually Kieran’s breathing slowed. “Cheers,” he said. “Any time,” said his friend, and meant it.

    Notice who helps you feel calmer, and tell them. “I always feel better after seeing you” is a lovely thing to hear, and almost nobody says it.

    Dig deeper: a safe haven

  6. Fitting in isn’t the same as belonging.

    Fitting in means changing yourself to be accepted. Belonging means being accepted as you are. Teams, families, clubs and communities can offer either. Being left out of a group hurts even when the group doesn’t matter much to us, and real belonging grows where people feel safe to be themselves and to speak up.

    At her new job Ola laughs at jokes she doesn’t find funny. She orders a lime and soda “for the drive”, every Friday, so nobody asks why she doesn’t drink. She goes home shattered. At her book club, in the back room of the library on alternate Tuesdays, she says exactly what she thought of the book, which last month was “I hated every single character”, and someone said, “Oh, thank God,” and they talked till the caretaker turned the lights off.

    You could say (in a group) “I see it a bit differently. Is it okay if I say why?” “Has everyone had a chance to say something?”

    Dig deeper: groups, work and community

  7. When trust breaks, rebuilding takes time.

    When someone lets us down badly, trust doesn’t come back with one apology. It comes back slowly, as words are matched by actions over time. The person who broke trust can own what happened and be patient. The person who was hurt can decide, at their own pace, whether and how much to trust again.

    At Sunday dinner, for a laugh, Jen told the whole table a story her sister had told her in confidence. Nobody else thought it was a big deal. Her sister left before pudding. The apology, two days later, was a start. What actually rebuilt things was the months after, when the story never came up again, and Jen started checking before she told anyone anything. “Can I mention the thing about work?” “No.” “Okay.”

    Dig deeper: rebuilding trust

  8. You can start building connection from wherever you are.

    If you feel alone right now, big friendships can seem a long way off. Belonging can start far smaller than that. A chat with a neighbour. A regular class. An online group about something you love. Even small, light contact with people we barely know can lift our mood (Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, 2014).

    After his divorce, Martin started saying more than “thanks” to the man at the coffee stall outside the station. Weather, at first. A few weeks later they were talking football every morning, mostly about Stoke’s defence, which they agree about entirely. It’s a small friendship. It makes Mondays bearable. Small rituals like that are worth keeping going on purpose, and Keep your little rituals alive helps you notice the ones you already have.

    Ways to say it “I’m new around here. Is there anything you’d recommend?” “I loved what you said in the group. Would you like to get a coffee sometime?”

Monday, 8:10am

SO sorry, phone was in my coat all day, soft play hell. LOVED your message. Coffee Thursday?

Kai reads it twice. He thinks about how far last night’s story got, all on its own, with no help from anybody. Next time a reply is slow, he decides, he’ll send something light, no rush, just thinking of you, and leave it. He will probably still check his phone.

If you’re a teenager

Ezra is sixteen when he sees a photo of his three closest friends at the cinema and finds out there’s a group chat he isn’t in. It hurts like being punched, because being left out really does register a bit like pain. He drafts something sharp. Deletes it. Messages one friend instead, saying he’s a bit gutted not to be asked and was it something he did. It turns out they thought he’d be at football. One of the friendships really is drifting, though, and that’s allowed to hurt too.

Words to try “Bit gutted not to be asked. Did something happen?” “I’d like to come next time, even if I can’t always make it.” “I miss how we used to be. Are we okay?”

Dig deeper: more worked examples

In everyday life

When one partner texts a lot while apart and the other forgets, agreeing on one goodnight message can keep both feeling connected. A child who acts up at pick-up may simply be letting go of a long day with the safest person they know. A friend who keeps cancelling may be overwhelmed, and “Is it easier if we do something short?” keeps the door open. At work, a team where mistakes can be owned without blame builds trust faster than one where everyone hides them. And in a shared house, one simple weekly meal can turn people who share a kitchen into people who look out for each other. Stay close has small ideas if you’re stuck for one.

On your own: belonging with yourself

Belonging includes how you treat yourself. If you’re kind to yourself on a bad day, you carry a small safe base wherever you go, and people who can comfort themselves often find it easier to let others in, because they’re less afraid of being let down. You’re allowed to need people. A lonely day is hard. It isn’t a forecast. On my own has more for people living alone.

Common mix-ups

“Needing people means I’m needy” is the one people say most quietly. Everyone needs connection, including the people who hide it well. Attachment style isn’t fixed for life, either. Patterns can change, especially through steady, kind relationships and a bit of reflection. And forgiving isn’t the same as trusting again straight away. Trust can return slowly, or not at all, and that’s your call.

  • “Jealousy and checking up show how much they love me.” Reaching for reassurance is human, and so is asking for something out of care for the relationship. Controlling who someone sees, or checking their phone without agreement, is not love and is not an attachment style. See signs to look out for.

If you want to help someone else with this

Be steady. Turn up when you said you would, which matters more than any big gesture. If someone says they feel alone, listen before you remind them how many friends they have. Invite, and keep inviting. People who feel left out may say no a few times, and a warm invitation with no pressure still counts every time. In groups, notice who’s on the edge and ask what they think. And leave the labels out. Telling someone they’re “so avoidant” or “so clingy” has never helped anyone, as far as I can tell. “What would help?” often does.

You could say “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere.” “We’re having a few people over on Friday. No pressure, but I’d love you to come.”

When to get more help

  • If loneliness has lasted a long time, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of not wanting to be here, please talk to a doctor or a mental health service. If you are in danger right now, call your local emergency number or see Upset right now.
  • If fear of being left, or of getting close, keeps hurting your relationships, a counsellor or therapist can help you understand where it comes from and how to change it.
  • If someone uses your need for them against you, by cutting you off from friends, checking where you are, or threatening to leave to get their way, that is not about attachment. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
  • If trust was broken in a way that frightened you, you don’t owe anyone a second chance. Your safety comes first.
