The Epistemic Verdict Engine & P(Solvency)

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about. Nobody is lying when they say it feels even. From inside each person’s own week, it does.

Before anything else: this chapter describes a rough self-reflection tool, not a scientific instrument. “P(Solvency)” is not a proven statistical model, and it never judges either person or the worth of the relationship. It scores one narrow thing: whether the way you currently share the household work looks like it can last, based on what you wrote down. Think of a small shop’s cash-flow snapshot. It tells you about this month’s trading, not about whether the owners are good people.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. (Borrowed from ledger accounting, systems thinking and nervous-system science.)

  • In you: See your own share honestly, from what you wrote down rather than from memory. See the way you share work as a setup you can redesign, not a flaw in you.
  • Between you and others: Put both people’s numbers on one page, and read the result as a note about the setup, never a verdict on a person. Clear owners replace blame.

What the names mean

The formal names sound grander than the tool is, so here they are in plain words:

  • Epistemic means “to do with what we know.” The Epistemic Verdict Engine is simply a check that only uses what you have both written down, not what either of you remembers or feels in the moment.
  • Solvency is an accounting word. A business is solvent when it can keep paying its way. Here, a solvent setup is one where the work gets done without one person quietly running out of energy.
  • P(Solvency) looks like a probability, but it isn’t one. The “P” is only a name carried over from the original manuscript. The number is a simple weighted sum, not a prediction.

What the check is for

Sooner or later, everyone who shares a home asks some version of “Is this actually working?” Usually the answer comes from whatever happened most recently: last night’s dishes, this morning’s tone. That answers the question at the wrong scale.

The Verdict Engine asks a narrower, calmer question: based on what’s actually written down this month, does the way we share the work look like it can last, or is it quietly running at a loss? “Written down” means three things: the hours logged in the Lemonade Stand or WP-01, the jobs with named owners in WP-03, and each person’s own stress score from WP-02.

The three inputs

P(Solvency) combines three readings, each one covered elsewhere in the book:

Input Where it comes from What it shows
Workload balance The Lemonade Stand, or Who did what (WP-01) How evenly the logged hours are shared
Ownership clarity One owner per job, the RACI Treaty (WP-03) How many regular jobs have a clear, agreed owner
Autonomic saturation The Battery & Stress Meter (WP-02), explained in Chapter III How much stress each person is already carrying from other things

Each input is a number from 0 to 1, worked out from the worksheets, not from how anyone feels in the moment.

Workload balance

Take the week’s logged hours and work out each person’s share as a percentage. Then:

workload_balance = 1 − (difference between the two percentages ÷ 100)

A perfectly even week (50% and 50%) scores 1.00. A week where one person did everything (100% and 0%) scores 0.00. A 70/30 week scores 1 − 40 ÷ 100 = 0.60.

The score is the same whichever person is doing more. The arithmetic doesn’t care who is carrying the load, only whether it is shared. Rough hours are fine. Counting to the minute would only look more precise than it is.

Ownership clarity

In WP-03, every regular job gets two names: the Responsible person, who does it, and the Accountable person, who notices if it didn’t happen and gently follows up. They can be the same person. (RACI is a planning term from project work. This book only uses its first two letters.)

ownership_clarity = jobs with both names filled in ÷ all jobs on the list

So if you list 10 jobs and 8 have both names, the score is 0.80.

One warning: don’t improve this score by shortening the list. Deleting the jobs nobody owns raises the number and changes nothing at home. If a job is real and unowned, it belongs on the list as an unowned row. That’s exactly the row the score is there to show.

Autonomic saturation

Each of you fills in your own WP-02, about yourself only. You score five things from 0 to 4: sleep, workload elsewhere, any unresolved conflict, how your body feels, and time pressure today. Add them up and divide by 20. Then take the average of your two scores.

This is the only input that counts against the result. The formula uses 1 − saturation, so more stress means a lower score. That’s on purpose: a way of sharing work that only holds up when both people are well rested isn’t one that can last.

Never estimate the other person’s score for them. If one column is missing, the full calculator won’t work it out, and that’s the right answer.

The formula, in plain language

Rather than a mystery score, P(Solvency) is a simple weighted sum:

solvency_read = (workload_balance_score * 0.4)
              + (ownership_clarity_score * 0.35)
              + (1 - average_autonomic_saturation) * 0.25

The weights add up to 1, so the result is always between 0 and 1. Read it as a snapshot, not a sentence. The Solvency Read calculator (CALC-01) does the arithmetic for you:

  • 0.7 or above: The current setup is carrying its own weight. Keep the same rhythm of check-ins.
  • 0.4 up to 0.7: Something in the three inputs is drifting, often ownership first. Look at the WP-03 job list before anything else.
  • Below 0.4: The work, as it is currently shared, doesn’t look like it can last. That’s a statement about the setup, not about either person’s love, effort or worth. Setups get renegotiated all the time.

Why these weights?

The weights are a judgment, not a result from research. They say that uneven hours matter a little more than unclear ownership, and that both matter more than everyday stress. Unclear ownership comes a close second because it’s what turns a fixable imbalance into a repeating one: work with no owner falls to whoever notices first, every time. If you change the weights in the full calculator, write the change down, because your old results won’t compare with your new ones.

A worked example

Sam and Alex each log one ordinary week.

  • Balance: Sam logged 14 hours and Alex logged 6. That’s 70% and 30%, so balance = 1 − 40 ÷ 100 = 0.60.
  • Ownership: They list 10 regular jobs. 8 have both names. Ownership = 8 ÷ 10 = 0.80.
  • Stress: Sam’s WP-02 adds up to 11, so 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55. Alex’s adds up to 5, so 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25. The average is 0.40.
(0.60 × 0.4) + (0.80 × 0.35) + ((1 − 0.40) × 0.25)
=    0.24    +     0.28      +        0.15
=    0.67

0.67 is in the middle band: something is drifting. Now watch what small changes do:

  • If Alex takes on 3 more hours a week, the split becomes 11 and 9 hours (55% and 45%). Balance rises to 0.90, and the result becomes 0.36 + 0.28 + 0.15 = 0.79.
  • If they also give owners to the 2 unowned jobs, ownership rises to 1.00, and the result becomes 0.36 + 0.35 + 0.15 = 0.86.

For contrast, a household where the split is 90/10 (balance 0.20), only 4 of 10 jobs are owned (0.40), and average stress is 0.70 scores 0.08 + 0.14 + 0.075 = about 0.30. That’s the “kind rethink” band. It doesn’t mean anyone failed. It means the setup is asking too much of someone, and it’s time to redesign it together.

Reading your result: start with the shortfall

Each part can earn up to its weight: 0.40 for balance, 0.35 for ownership, and 0.25 for stress. The gap between what a part earned and what it could have earned is the shortfall. The shortfall, not the total, is the part you can act on.

In Sam and Alex’s first week:

Balance0.400.240.16

The biggest shortfall is balance, so that’s the best place to start. Often the two are linked: giving a job a clear owner is how some of the hours move. That’s why the calculator’s general advice for the middle band is to look at WP-03 first.

The wider view: the apex score

The full CALC-01 also shows a second number, the apex score. It adds a fourth input, retuning frequency: of the moments this week that landed badly, how many did you retune before answering (see Chapter I)? Its weights are 0.35 for balance, 0.30 for ownership, 0.20 for stress and 0.15 for retuning.

The two numbers answer different questions. P(Solvency) asks, “Can this workload last as it’s set up?” The apex score asks, “Is the whole system working, including how we repair after friction?” They can disagree, and that difference is useful. A home can share the work well and still be struggling to repair after clashes.

One reading is a snapshot. A month of readings is a trend.

A single reading tells you about one week. The trend over several weeks tells you whether anything is really changing. REPORT-01 helps you keep the numbers week by week. The monthly look-back in Chapter V feeds back into this chapter: a month with fewer jobs slipping through the gaps should show up as better ownership the next month.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, one person guesses both sets of numbers, gets a low score, and says, “See? The number proves it.” The other person argues with the number instead of talking about the week. Now the tool has become a weapon.

When it goes right, each person brings their own log and their own battery score. They work out the number together, look at the biggest shortfall, and choose one small change. A month later, they check again and see whether it moved.

Questions people often ask

Our score is 0.69. Should we worry?

No. The bands are rough guides, not cliffs. 0.69 and 0.71 describe almost the same week. Watch the trend and the shortfalls, not the exact border.

What if our hours are even but it still feels unfair?

That’s important information. Hours don’t capture everything, such as who does the planning or who covers the gaps. Chapter IV is about the different kinds of fair.

Does our score mean we’re a good or bad match?

No. It says nothing about compatibility. It only describes how the work is shared right now.

Can I ask someone to walk me through it?

Yes. Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can explain any part of the calculation.

What this number is not

It is not a relationship-compatibility score. It is not a fairness verdict about who’s the better person. It never uses anyone’s tone of voice, private thoughts or way of talking as an input. It only uses the three worksheets above, which each person fills in for themselves and both then read together.

If the number is ever used to end a conversation (“well, the number says…”) instead of starting one, it’s being misused. A low score doesn’t assign blame, and a high score doesn’t silence anyone. The number’s only job is to give two people a shared, low-drama place to start closing the books together, which Chapter V describes at the monthly scale.

Aristotle’s Three Kinds of Friendship and Buber’s I and Thou explain why the number must never stand in for the person.
