How feelings work: signals, the body, and naming what you feel

Paperback textRead the expanded version

What feelings are for, where they start, and why putting a name to one can make it easier to carry, whether you’re on your own, at home, with friends or at work.

In the paperbacks: Book 1, Know Yourself Kindly, Chapter 4.

At a glance

  • A feeling is a signal that something matters to you. It doesn’t tell you what to do about it.
  • Feelings start in the body. A tight chest or a hot face often arrives before the word for it.
  • Naming helps. “I feel let down” is easier to hold than a big, nameless rush.
  • You can let a feeling be there and still choose what you do next.

Thursday, 4:50pm

A message from your manager. Have you got a minute tomorrow? No period, no smiley, nothing else at all.

By the time you get in you’ve snapped at the cat, decided you’re not hungry, and gone back over every mistake of the past month, plus one from March that nobody else remembers. Nobody has said anything bad. You check the message again anyway, in case it’s grown a period.

Feelings sit underneath nearly everything else in this book, so they get looked at first. Two tools elsewhere lean on this chapter. My weather today and the daily check-in (Part One, Now; Part Two, Chapter I) ask for one word about how you are, because naming a feeling turns its volume down a little and a daily word catches feelings while they’re still small. And Say it so it lands (Part Two, Chapter IV) asks you to describe a feeling, because “I felt let down” is a good deal easier to hear than “you never care.”

Here is the knot in the stomach in quick strokes. On top, a short message from a manager. Under it, the body’s alarm, which has already decided something is wrong. Under that, the meaning your mind gives the alarm. The roots are state and setting, and signal and noise. Then, slowly, each layer gets its own page or two.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear says watch out. Anger says this seems unfair, or this is in my way. Sadness says something that mattered has gone. A signal is worth listening to. It can also be badly wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm goes off for burned toast with exactly the same urgency it would give a house fire.

    Sunny days and big questions In a classic study, people phoned on sunny days rated their whole lives as more satisfying, and the bad-weather dip vanished once the interviewer mentioned the weather. Much bigger studies later found the weather effect itself is tiny. The useful part survives: spotting where a feeling comes from stops it coloring everything else.

    Schwarz and Clore (1983) · Moderate: a classic study of feelings as information; the weather effect proved very small

    Hayley’s best friend has a new best friend, or that’s how it feels, and Hayley is jealous and a bit ashamed of being jealous at thirty-four. The jealousy proves nothing about her, and nothing about whether the friend is drifting off either; it just shows how much the friendship matters, which is worth knowing, and possibly worth saying. “I’m being silly,” she texts eventually, “but I miss you.” The friend calls within the minute.

    Things to tell yourself “This feeling is telling me something matters. What is it?” “Is this the fire, or the toast?”

  2. Feelings start in the body.

    Often the body goes first. Racing heart, hot face, a weight on the chest, a knot in the stomach. Then the brain tries to work out what the signal means. It guesses, and it isn’t always a good guesser. Which is why being hungry, or cold, or in pain, can feel so much like being upset.

    Reece is fifteen and has come home in a fury about a towel. Specifically, about his mom asking where his gym towel was. There is a good deal of door involved. After a grilled cheese sandwich and twenty minutes on the couch with the dog’s head on his knee, most of it has drained away, and he admits, through a mouthful, that he didn’t have any lunch. “I wasn’t hungry,” he says. Then, “I was a bit hungry.”

    A few times today, stop and ask what your body feels like right now, and whether any of it is simply hunger, or cold, or a sore back.

    Dig deeper: the body and the brain’s best guess

  3. Naming a feeling helps you hold it.

    Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down. In brain-scan research by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), simply putting a name to a feeling (“I feel scared”) turned down the brain’s alarm response. A name gives a big, cloudy rush some edges, so you can see it and talk about it and decide what to do.

    It works even when it doesn’t feel like it A review by Torre and Lieberman found that naming a feeling can turn down its intensity even though, in the moment, it doesn’t feel as if you’re doing anything at all. So carry on with “something like sad” even when it seems pointless.

    Torre and Lieberman (2018) · Moderate: consistent lab evidence; how it works is still being worked out

    Two roommates spent a whole evening passing each other in the hall without a word. Nobody had done anything, as far as either of them knew. At about ten, one of them stopped on the stairs. “I think I’m embarrassed about the noise complaint,” she said, “and I’ve been taking it out on you by being weird.” The other one, who had spent two hours going back over everything she’d said that week, laughed so hard she had to sit down. Then she made some tea.

    Once a day, name one feeling, out loud or on paper. A guess like “something like sad” counts.

  4. More words, better tools.

    “Bad” is a blunt instrument. Disappointed is different from left out, and both are different from overwhelmed or ashamed. Each one needs something different. A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need a big vocabulary. A number does the job. So does a color.

    Stephen told his partner he felt bad about his mom and they went around in circles for half an hour. Bad how? Bad about what? Then, out of nowhere, staring at the fridge: “I feel guilty I haven’t been up since Easter. And I’m scared she’s getting worse.” Within ten minutes they were looking at trains to Pittsburgh, and the cheapest one turned out to leave at 6:12 in the morning, which led to a whole different conversation about who was going to drive them to the station.

    Words you could use “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a gray day.”

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the one you felt first. Anger can sit on top of hurt or fear, because anger is a stronger place to stand. And we have feelings about our feelings (ashamed of being sad, mad about being scared) and that second layer can sting more than the first.

    The visit has been canceled again. Brenda’s daughter calls to say so, with a lot of reasons, all of them good ones, and Brenda says, “Fine. Well. Another time,” in a voice like a closing door. Under that is hurt, and under the hurt, if she let herself look, there is a quieter worry that she isn’t needed much anymore by anyone, which is the one she has spent the last two years carefully not looking at. She doesn’t look. She puts the phone down and sits next to it for a while with her coat still on.

    Next time you’re angry, you might ask whether there’s hurt or worry underneath. You don’t have to tell anyone what you find.

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise and peak and settle, as long as you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are something else. A mood is more like weather, a background feeling that hangs about for hours or days and colors everything without announcing itself.

    Not every wave is the same length Of 27 emotions in one study, sadness lasted longest, and shame, fear and disgust were among the shortest. The long ones tended to follow events that mattered, and to be chewed over.

    Verduyn and Lavrijsen (2015) · Moderate: a single self-report study, in line with other research on how long feelings last

    By eleven o’clock on a Saturday morning Rachel had decided her whole life was going nowhere. In fairness it had been a morning. The water heater, a letter from the city, and a dream about her ex in which he was, for some reason, a dentist. By seven that evening, after a walk along the canal and an hour with her sister, the same life looked fairly ordinary and mostly all right, with an actual dentist on Tuesday and a library book she wanted to finish. Nothing in her life changed between eleven and seven.

    So if you can, leave big decisions until the wave has gone past. Write it down. Look at it tomorrow.

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We catch each other’s feelings from a tight voice, a sigh, a door shut slightly too hard, usually without noticing we’ve caught anything. It’s part of how people bond. It also means stress can go around a house or a team like a cold in February.

    Bryony came into the Monday meeting late and rattled, and within ten minutes six people who had been perfectly fine at nine o’clock felt behind on everything. The following Monday she stood outside the door for two slow breaths before going in. The meeting finished on time. In that office this was regarded as a minor miracle and nobody could work out why.

    You can care about someone’s feeling without catching the whole of it. Good moods are catching too, and Stay close has small ideas for passing one on. Notice who you tend to catch things from, and give yourself a minute before you next talk to them.

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Lots of people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private. “Don’t make a fuss.” “Big boys don’t cry.” “We don’t talk about that.” It’s especially common among men and older people, and in some cultures keeping your feelings to yourself is a sign of respect and strength.

    It isn’t a flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every day by doing things, fixing the faucet or driving someone to the ER at two in the morning and waiting in the parking lot till it’s over. They turn up. Caring by doing is real caring. Words can be added slowly, a few at a time, if you want them.

    Len was a builder for forty years and his wife died in the spring. He has never in his life said how he feels about anything, apart from the Steelers. On the sideline at his grandson’s game, during a long dull bit of the first half, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this. But I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder and leaves it there. Neither of them says anything else for the rest of the game. They watch the second half, which is also dull, and it’s one of the better afternoons either of them has had all year.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    If one of those fits, you could say it once this week, to one person, or just out loud in the car. A text counts.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually arrives with an urge attached, to shout or leave or hide or send the text you’ll regret by dinnertime. You don’t choose the feeling, but you get rather more say over the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to bring it back bigger, and acting on every urge hurts people, so the middle way is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    A crash from the yard. Through the kitchen window, Imran’s son is standing very still beside the greenhouse with a soccer ball, and there is a lot less greenhouse than there was. Imran feels the heat go up his neck. He opens the back door, says, “I’m too angry to talk about this. Ten minutes,” shuts it again, and goes to find the dustpan. By the time he comes out his son has picked up most of the big bits, very carefully, in gardening gloves two sizes too large.

The knot’s back. This time you give it a name, which is scared, and then you do the thing you’d been avoiding and just ask. Your manager looks honestly baffled. “Oh God, no. It’s the summer schedule.” You go back to your desk and eat the breakfast you skipped, a banana that’s been in your bag since Wednesday.

Two ways to read this

A feeling, sent and received

Some people feel first and find the words later, sometimes much later. Asked “What’s wrong?” they honestly may not know yet. Others name a feeling almost as it arrives and expect everyone else to as well. If you name things fast, the quiet person may not be hiding anything. Their words are still on the way. If you need time, the quick namer may not be pushing. To them, saying it is how a feeling gets smaller.

Where the fields meet. Music has known for a long time that tone and tempo carry more than the words, and a feeling is often heard in the tune before the lyrics. Psychology adds that mood filters what we hear, so the same sentence lands differently on a bad day.

In everyday life

If you live on your own, nobody is going to notice how you are, so a one-word check-in with yourself each day matters more (see On my own). With a partner, “What are you feeling?” can sound like an exam. “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is much easier to answer. Children learn feeling words from the grown-ups near them, and hearing you say “I’m frustrated, so I’m going to take a breath” teaches more than any lesson. At work, “I’m a bit low today and I’m not upset with anyone” can stop a whole team guessing. And if someone close to you shows care by doing, like clearing the drive on your bad day, you can thank them for it in words. They may not know what to do with it the first time.

Common mix-ups

Some feelings hurt and some actions do harm, but a feeling on its own isn’t bad. It’s information. Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid, either, so treat a feeling as something to check before you obey it. People also expect to know right away what they feel, and plenty can’t, some of them most days, which is why body words and numbers count as answers here. The most stubborn mix-up is the idea that a face tells you what someone feels. Faces are a poor guide. People show the same feeling in very different ways, and asking is kinder than guessing and a lot more accurate.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can land like a label. “You seem upset, am I reading that right?” leaves them room to put you straight. Then help the feeling make sense (“Of course you’re upset, that was a lot”), which does more than advice and doesn’t mean you agree with everything they did. Ask whether they want ideas or just company. A lot of people only want the second and are relieved to be asked. Take other ways of saying it, a number or a text or “I’m at a seven.” And settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go in, or they’ll end up looking after you.

You could say “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”
