How feelings work: signals, the body, and naming what you feel

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What feelings are for, where they start, and why putting a name to one can make it easier to carry, whether you’re on your own, at home, with friends or at work.

In short

  • A feeling is a signal that something matters to you. It doesn’t tell you what to do about it.
  • Feelings start in the body. A tight chest or a hot face often arrives before the word for it.
  • Naming helps. “I feel let down” is easier to hold than a big, nameless rush.
  • You can let a feeling be there and still choose what you do next.

Thursday, 4:50pm

A message from your manager: “Have you got a minute tomorrow?” No full stop, no smiley, nothing else. Your stomach drops. By the time you get home you’ve snapped at the cat, skipped dinner and replayed every mistake of the last month, including one from March that nobody else remembers, although nobody has said anything bad at all.

Feelings sit underneath almost everything else in this book, from how we talk to how we handle stress. This chapter looks at how they work first, and then at what to do with them. Two tools elsewhere lean on it. My weather today and the daily check-in (Part One, Now; Part Two, Chapter I) ask for one word about how you are, because naming a feeling turns its volume down a little and a daily word catches feelings while they’re small. And Say it so it lands (Part Two, Chapter IV) asks you to describe a feeling, because “I felt let down” is a great deal easier to hear than “you never care.”

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear warns that something might be dangerous. Anger says something seems unfair, or in your way. Sadness says you’ve lost something that mattered. A signal is worth listening to, but it can be wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm goes off for burnt toast with exactly the same urgency it would use for a fire.

    Say you feel jealous when a friend makes a new close friend. The jealousy proves nothing about you, and nothing about whether your friend is drifting off. What it shows is how much the friendship matters to you, which is worth knowing, and possibly worth telling her.

    Things to tell yourself “This feeling is telling me something matters. What is it?” “Is this the fire, or the toast?”

    Dig deeper: what feelings are for

  2. Feelings start in the body.

    Often your body reacts before your mind has a word for what’s going on: a racing heart, a hot face, a heavy chest, a knot in the stomach. Then the brain tries to make sense of the signal, and it guesses. That’s why being hungry or tired or in pain can feel so like being upset.

    A teenager comes home furious with his mum over very little, something about a towel. After a cheese sandwich and twenty minutes on the sofa with the dog, most of the fury has gone, and he admits, with some reluctance, that he skipped lunch. A few times today, stop and ask what your body feels like right now. Is there hunger in it, or cold, or a sore back?

    Dig deeper: the body and the brain’s best guess

  3. Naming a feeling helps you hold it.

    Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down. In brain-scan research by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), simply putting a name to a feeling (“I feel scared”) turned down the brain’s alarm response. A name gives a big, cloudy rush some edges, so you can see it and talk about it and decide what to do.

    Two housemates are tense all evening, passing each other in the hall without a word. Eventually one says, “I think I’m embarrassed about the noise complaint, and I’ve been acting cross.” The other, who had spent two hours wondering what she’d done wrong, puts the kettle on.

    You could say “I think what I’m feeling is…” “I’m not sure what this is yet. Maybe worried?”

    Once a day, name one feeling, out loud or on paper. A guess like “something like sad” still counts.

    Dig deeper: why naming works

  4. More words, better tools.

    “Bad” is a very blunt word. Disappointed is different from left out, and both are different from overwhelmed or ashamed; each points somewhere different and needs something different. A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need fancy words. Numbers, colours and body words all work.

    A dad tells his partner he feels bad about his mum, and they go round in circles for half an hour. Then he says, “Actually, I feel guilty that I haven’t been up to see her since Easter, and scared she’s getting worse,” and within ten minutes they’re looking at train times to Carlisle.

    Words you could use “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a grey day.”

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the one you felt first. Anger is very often a cover for hurt or fear, because anger feels stronger and safer to stand in. We also have feelings about our feelings, like being ashamed of being sad, and that second layer can sting more than the first.

    A grandmother is curt with her daughter on the phone after the half-term visit is cancelled for the second time. Under the curtness is hurt. Under the hurt, if she let herself look, is a quieter fear that she isn’t needed much any more. She doesn’t say any of that. She says “Fine, well, another time,” and then sits by the phone for a while. Next time you feel angry, ask yourself whether there’s hurt or worry underneath. You don’t have to tell anyone what you find.

    Dig deeper: first feelings and feelings about feelings

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise and peak and settle if you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are another matter. A mood is more like weather, a background feeling that lasts hours or days and colours everything, often without you noticing it’s there.

    A woman who lives alone has a bad morning (the boiler, a bill, a dream about her ex) and by eleven has decided her whole life is going nowhere. By evening, after a walk along the canal and a long call with her sister, the same life looks ordinary and fairly okay, with a dentist’s appointment on Tuesday and a library book she wants to finish. Nothing in her life changed between eleven and seven. That’s a good reason to leave big decisions until the wave has passed: write the decision down and look at it again tomorrow.

    Things to tell yourself “This is a wave. It will peak and pass.” “Is this a feeling about now, or a mood colouring everything?”

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We pick up each other’s feelings from a tense voice, a sigh or a door shut a bit too hard, usually without noticing. That’s part of how people bond. It also means stress can move round a home or a team like a cold in February.

    A team leader comes into the Monday meeting rushed and anxious, and within ten minutes the whole team feels behind on everything. The next week she stops outside the door for two slow breaths before she goes in, and the meeting finishes on time, which in that office counts as a minor miracle. You can care about someone’s feeling without catching all of it. Notice who you tend to catch feelings from, and before your next talk with them, give yourself one minute first.

    Things to tell yourself “Is this feeling mine, or did I pick it up?” “I can stay beside their storm without standing in the middle of it.”

    Dig deeper: how feelings spread

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Lots of people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private. “Don’t make a fuss.” “Big boys don’t cry.” “We don’t talk about that.” It’s especially common among men and older people, and in some cultures keeping your feelings to yourself is a sign of respect and strength.

    It isn’t a flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every single day by doing things: fixing the tap, driving someone to the hospital at two in the morning, simply turning up. Caring by doing is real caring. Words can be added slowly, a few at a time, if you want them.

    A retired builder whose wife has died has never talked about his feelings in his life. On the touchline at his grandson’s Saturday match, during a dull bit of the first half, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder, and they watch the second half together without saying much else.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    If one of those fits, you could say it once this week, to one person or just out loud in the car. A text counts, and so does writing it down.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually comes with an urge attached, to shout or run or hide or check or fix or text. You don’t choose the feeling. You have rather more say over the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to bring it back stronger, and acting on every urge can hurt people, so the workable middle is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    A father hears glass go and looks out to see his son standing very still in the garden next to a football. He feels a hot rush of anger, says, “I’m too cross to talk right now. Let’s sort this out in ten minutes,” and goes to fetch the dustpan. If there’s one urge you often act on too fast, like the sharp text, make yourself a rule to wait ten minutes before you send it.

    Words to try “I’m feeling a lot right now. I’m going to take a minute before I answer.” “I want to reply when I’m calmer.”

The knot is there again, and this time you name it. You’re scared you’re in trouble. So you go and ask your manager straight away whether today’s chat is anything to worry about, and she looks genuinely surprised and says it’s about the summer rota. You go back to your desk and eat the breakfast you skipped, a slightly squashed banana.

In everyday life

If you live on your own, there’s nobody to notice how you are, so a one-word check-in with yourself each day matters more (see On my own). With a partner, “What are you feeling?” can sound like a test you might fail, while “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is much easier to answer. Children learn feeling words from the adults around them, and hearing you say “I’m frustrated, so I’m taking a breath” teaches them more than any lesson would. At work, a short “I’m a bit flat today, and I’m not upset with anyone” can stop a whole team guessing. And if someone close to you shows care by doing, clearing the drive or cooking on a bad day, you can thank them for it in words. They may not know what to do with it the first time.

Common mix-ups

Some feelings are painful and some actions are harmful, but a feeling in itself isn’t bad; it’s information. Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid either. A feeling is a signal to check, not a fact to obey. Lots of people expect to be able to name what they feel straight away, and plenty can’t, some of them most days, which is why body words, numbers and colours all count. The last one is the most stubborn. We think a face tells us what someone feels, but faces are a poor guide. People show the same feeling in very different ways, and asking is both kinder and more accurate than guessing.

If you want to help someone else with this

Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can land like a label, while “You seem upset. Am I reading that right?” leaves room for them to correct you. Then help the feeling make sense (“Of course you’re upset, that was a lot”), which helps more than advice and doesn’t mean you agree with everything they did. Offer a choice between ideas and just listening; a lot of people only want the second, and are relieved to be asked. Accept other ways of saying it, like a number, a text, a drawing or “I’m at a seven”. And settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go to them, so they don’t end up looking after your feelings as well as their own.

You could say “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”

When to get more help

Everyone has hard days and big feelings. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or another trained professional if:

  • a low, numb, anxious or angry feeling stays most days for two weeks or more;
  • feelings are stopping you from sleeping, eating, working or seeing people;
  • you are using alcohol, drugs or other things to stop feeling;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that’s happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. You deserve help today.

If you feel afraid of someone you live with or are close to, that fear is an important signal. It is not something to “manage” on your own. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
