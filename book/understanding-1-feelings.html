How feelings work: signals, the body, and naming what you feel

Simple versionRead the full chapter

What feelings are for, where they start, and why putting a name to one can make it easier to carry, whether you are on your own, at home, with friends or at work.

In short

  • A feeling is a signal that something matters to you. It doesn’t tell you what to do about it.
  • Feelings start in the body. A tight chest or a hot face often comes before the word for it.
  • Naming helps. “I feel let down” is easier to hold than a big, nameless rush.
  • You can let a feeling be there and still choose what you do next.

Why the tools work. Two tools in other chapters rest on what this one explains.

  • My weather today and the daily check-in (Part One, Now; Part Two, Chapter I) ask for one word about how you are. Naming a feeling turns its volume down a little, and a daily word catches feelings while they are still small.
  • Say it so it lands (Part Two, Chapter IV) asks you to describe a feeling, because “I felt let down” is easier to hear than “you never care.”

Thursday, 4:50pm

A message from your manager asks whether you have a minute tomorrow, and your stomach drops. By the time you get home you have snapped at the cat, skipped dinner and replayed every mistake of the last month, although nobody has said anything bad at all.

Feelings sit underneath almost everything else, from how we talk and how we see each other to how we handle stress. This chapter looks first at how feelings work and then at what to do with them.

  1. A feeling is a signal, not a verdict.

    Feelings are fast messages about what matters to you. Fear warns that something might be dangerous, anger that something seems unfair or in your way, and sadness that you have lost something that mattered. A signal is worth listening to, though it can be wrong about the details, the way a smoke alarm can go off for burnt toast.

    For example, you feel jealous when a friend makes a new close friend. The jealousy proves nothing about you, and nothing about whether your friend is leaving. What it does show is how much the friendship matters to you.

    Things to tell yourself “This feeling is telling me something matters. What is it?” “Is this the fire, or the toast?”

    Try this. Next time a strong feeling arrives, finish this sentence: “This feeling is trying to protect…”

    Dig deeper: what feelings are for

  2. Feelings start in the body.

    Often your body reacts before your mind has a word for it, with a racing heart, a hot face, a heavy chest or a knot in the stomach, and your brain then tries to make sense of the signal. That is why being hungry, tired or in pain can feel so like being upset, because the mind is guessing at what a strong signal means.

    A teenager comes home furious with his mum over very little. After a cheese sandwich and twenty minutes on the sofa, most of the fury has gone, and he admits he skipped lunch.

    Things to tell yourself “Where is this in my body?” “Am I hungry, tired, cold or sore right now?”

    This week. Three times today, pause and ask yourself what your body feels like right now, and leave it at noticing.

    Dig deeper: the body and the brain’s best guess

  3. Naming a feeling helps you hold it.

    Putting a feeling into words can turn its volume down a little. In brain-scan research by Lieberman and colleagues (2007), simply putting a name to a feeling (“I feel scared”) turned down the brain’s alarm response. A name gives a big, cloudy rush some edges, so that you can see it, talk about it and decide what to do.

    For example, two housemates are tense all evening. Eventually one says, “I think I’m embarrassed about the noise complaint, and I’ve been acting cross,” and the other, who had been wondering what she had done wrong, puts the kettle on.

    You could say “I think what I’m feeling is…” “I’m not sure what this is yet. Maybe worried?”

    One small step. Once a day, name one feeling out loud or on paper. A guess like “something like sad” still counts.

  4. More words, better tools.

    “Bad” is a very blunt word. “Disappointed,” “left out,” “overwhelmed” and “ashamed” each point to something different, and each needs something different. A review by Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight (2015) found that people who can tell their feelings apart more finely tend to cope with them in healthier ways. You don’t need fancy words, and numbers, colours or body words work too.

    A dad tells his partner that he feels bad about his mum, and they talk in circles for half an hour. Then he says, “Actually, I feel guilty that I haven’t visited, and scared she’s getting worse,” and they start looking at train times.

    Words you could use “On a scale of one to ten, I’m about a six.” “It’s less angry and more… let down.” “It feels heavy, like a grey day.”

    Something to try. When you notice “bad,” try for one more exact word. When you notice “fine,” try for one more honest one.

    Dig deeper: finer words, finer tools

  5. Feelings come in layers.

    The feeling you show first isn’t always the first one you felt. Anger is often a cover for hurt or fear, because anger feels stronger and safer. We also have feelings about our feelings, such as being ashamed of being sad or scared of being angry, and that second layer can hurt more than the first.

    For example, a grandmother is short with her daughter on the phone after the grandchildren’s visit is cancelled. Under the shortness is hurt, and under the hurt is a quieter fear that she is no longer needed.

    Thoughts you could try “What might be under this one?” “It’s okay to feel this. I don’t also have to feel bad for feeling it.”

    Try this. Next time you feel angry, ask yourself gently whether there is hurt or worry underneath. You don’t have to say it to anyone.

    Dig deeper: first feelings and feelings about feelings

  6. Feelings rise and pass like waves.

    Most feelings, even big ones, rise, peak and settle if you don’t keep feeding them. Moods are different. A mood is more like weather, a background feeling that lasts hours or days and colours everything, often without you noticing. A grey mood can make a small thing feel huge, and a good mood can make the same thing feel like nothing.

    A woman living alone has a bad morning and decides her whole life is going nowhere. By evening, after a walk and a long call with her sister, the same life looks ordinary and fairly okay, with a dentist’s appointment on Tuesday and a book she wants to finish.

    Things to tell yourself “This is a wave. It will peak and pass.” “Is this a feeling about now, or a mood colouring everything?”

    This week. Leave big decisions until the wave has passed. Write the decision down and look at it again tomorrow.

    Dig deeper: waves and weather

  7. Feelings spread between people.

    We pick up each other’s feelings from a tense voice, a sigh or a slammed door, often without noticing. That is part of how people bond, and it also means stress can move round a home or a team like a cold. You can care about someone’s feeling without catching all of it.

    For example, a team leader comes into a meeting rushed and anxious, and within ten minutes the whole team feels behind. The next week she stops at the door for two slow breaths before she goes in, and the meeting runs to time.

    Things to tell yourself “Is this feeling mine, or did I pick it up?” “I can stay beside their storm without standing in the middle of it.”

    One small step. Notice who you tend to “catch” feelings from. Before your next talk with them, settle yourself for one minute first.

  8. If you weren’t brought up to talk about feelings.

    Many people grew up in homes, generations or cultures where feelings were kept private, with sayings like “Don’t make a fuss”, “Big boys don’t cry” and “We don’t talk about that.” This is common for men and for older people, and in some cultures it is a sign of respect and strength.

    Keeping feelings private is no flaw, and it doesn’t mean you don’t feel. Many people who rarely put a feeling into words show it every day by doing, whether that is fixing the tap, driving someone to hospital or simply turning up. Caring by doing is real caring, and words can be added slowly, a few at a time.

    A retired builder whose wife has died has never talked about his feelings. On the touchline at his grandson’s football match, he says to his son, “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her. Every morning.” His son puts a hand on his shoulder, and they watch the second half together.

    Ways to say it “I wasn’t brought up to say this, but I miss her.” “I’m not good with words for this. It’s been a hard week.” “I don’t know what to call it. I just feel heavy.” “I’m not one for talking, but I wanted you to know I’m proud of you.”

    Something to try. Pick one sentence above and say it once this week, to one person or just out loud on your own. A text counts, and so does writing it down.

    Dig deeper: when feelings weren’t talked about

  9. Feeling it isn’t the same as doing it.

    A feeling usually comes with an urge to shout, run, hide, check, fix or text. You don’t choose the feeling, but you have more choice over what you do with the urge. Pushing a feeling down tends to make it come back stronger, and acting on every urge can hurt people, so the middle path is to let the feeling be there and choose the action.

    For example, a father feels a hot rush of anger when his son breaks a window with a football. He says, “I’m too cross to talk right now. Let’s sort this out in ten minutes,” and goes to fetch the dustpan.

    Words to try “I’m feeling a lot right now. I’m going to take a minute before I answer.” “I want to reply when I’m calmer.”

    Try this. Pick one urge you often act on too fast, like sending a sharp text, and make a rule to wait ten minutes first.

Friday, 9:05am

You notice the knot in your stomach again, and this time you name it. You are scared you are in trouble. So you ask your manager straight away whether today’s chat is something you should worry about, and she looks surprised and says it is about the summer rota. You go back to your desk and eat the breakfast you skipped.

In everyday life

If you live on your own, nobody else is there to notice how you are, so a daily one-word check-in with yourself matters more (see On my own). With a partner, “What are you feeling?” can sound like a test, while “Do you want to tell me, or shall I just sit with you?” is easier to answer. Children learn feeling words from the adults around them, and saying “I’m frustrated, so I’m taking a breath” teaches more than any lesson. At work, a short “I’m a bit flat today, and I’m not upset with anyone” can stop a whole team guessing. If someone close to you shows care by doing, by clearing the drive or cooking on a bad day, you can thank them for it in words.

Common mix-ups

  • “Some feelings are bad.” Some feelings are painful and some actions are harmful, but the feeling itself is information.
  • “If I feel it, it must be true.” Feeling stupid doesn’t make you stupid. A feeling is a signal to check.
  • “I should be able to name it straight away.” Many people find feelings hard to name, and some find it very hard. Body words, numbers and colours all count.
  • “Their face tells me what they feel.” Faces are a poor guide, because people show the same feeling in different ways, and asking is kinder and more accurate than guessing.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Ask before you name it for them. “You’re angry” can feel like a label, while “You seem upset. Am I reading that right?” leaves room for them to correct you.
  • Make the feeling make sense. “Of course you’re upset, that was a lot” helps more than advice, and you can make sense of a feeling without agreeing with every action.
  • Offer choices. “Do you want ideas, or just someone to listen?” Many people only want the second.
  • Accept other ways of saying it. A number, a text, a drawing or “I’m at a seven” is a real answer.
  • Settle yourself first. If their sadness makes you anxious, calm down before you go to them, so they don’t have to look after your feelings too.

You could say “That sounds really hard.” “Do you want to talk, or would company help more?” “You don’t have to find the right word. A number is fine.”

When to get more help

Everyone has hard days and big feelings. It may be time to talk to a doctor, a counsellor or another trained professional if:

  • a low, numb, anxious or angry feeling stays most days for two weeks or more;
  • feelings are stopping you from sleeping, eating, working or seeing people;
  • you are using alcohol, drugs or other things to stop feeling;
  • you feel you can’t cope, or have thoughts of harming yourself. If that’s happening now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line straight away. You deserve help today.

If you feel afraid of someone you live with or are close to, that fear is an important signal. It is not something to “manage” on your own. Not safe at home? lists free, private help.
