Know yourself: now, your wiring, your weather and your words

Simple versionRead the full version

Why the same words can land differently on different days and in different people, and how to understand today’s you well enough to say what helps.

Sunday, 7:50pm

“You okay? You’ve been quiet all day.” Your friend has paused the film to ask. You say you’re fine. The honest answer is that you can’t tell whether you’re fine, tired or quietly bothered by something someone said on Thursday, and you couldn’t explain it to yourself, let alone to her, so you press play.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part: your temperament, your pace, your senses, how you take in words. You work with it, by building a setup that suits you. Today’s conditions are the passing part, things like sleep and hunger and the time of day, and you work around them by eating first or sleeping on it. A lot of frustration comes from mixing the two up. (Then covers the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    A dad who works nights finds the school-run chatter unbearable at ten past eight in the morning, and for a few months he quietly wonders whether he’s cut out for parenting at all. Then he swaps with his partner and does the half-three pick-up instead, and the same chatter about who sat next to whom at lunch is funny again (see Different hours). The children hadn’t changed. The hour had. Write down one thing that always bothers you, which is probably wiring, and one that only bothers you when you’re tired.

    You could say “It’s the hour, honestly. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

  2. Know how you like to be spoken to.

    Pace, tone, how much you need spelled out, what a silence means to you: they all change how words land. How you connect helps you find words for how you take things in and give them out, and it gives you a four-letter Wave Code, which is meant to be light-hearted, so please don’t put it on your CV. The “How to talk to me” card puts how you take in words on one page you can hand over before a hard talk.

    Agnes, who is seventy-two, loves a phone call. Her grandson Jago loves a text. For about a year each of them privately decided the other had gone a bit distant, until a cousin pointed out the obvious over a Christmas trifle, and now there’s a Sunday call at six with texts in between. If you want somewhere to start, finish the sentence “I take things in best when…” and keep it for your card.

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Every conversation runs through your own state before it reaches anyone else. Part Two, Chapter III looks closely at the stress tank in a shared home, so all this chapter adds is the self side: a one-minute reading on your own before you decide what a hard moment means. My weather today is the quick check, and How much are you carrying? is a fuller one on a short worksheet.

    A long-distance couple notice that every difficult call falls at the end of one partner’s late shift, around midnight her time, so they move serious talks to Saturday mornings with coffee (see Long distance).

    Words you could use “I’m running on empty tonight. Can we pick this up tomorrow morning?”

    One state, many words. The chapters and tools use a few everyday pictures for one thing, how much room you have today.

    • The three states (Chapter 2 calls them the ladder) are calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty.
    • My weather today uses sky words for the same three. Clear is calm and connected, gusty is revved up, and fogged in is running on empty. Its pressure is how much you were already carrying.
    • The stress tank is the score from How much are you carrying?, from 0 to 1, where higher means fuller.
    • The battery is the other side of the tank, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery.

    “Gusty”, “revved up”, “a fairly full tank” and “a low battery” can all describe the same Tuesday afternoon. I’d pick one set and stick with it.

  4. Ask where this feeling is coming from.

    Name the feeling first, even roughly (“tight, annoyed, a bit scared”). Then ask whether it’s the size of the moment or feels older, which layer it belongs to, and what it needs right now. Food, space, a heads-up, some reassurance, a proper conversation later: the answer is often more ordinary than you’d expect. It takes a minute and works best once the first wave has passed. A colleague leans over the partition at 4.45 on a Friday and says “Quick question?”, your heart starts going, and if you stop to look you might find it’s jumpy, with a former boss somewhere in it and a long week on top, and that what it needs is five minutes before you answer.

    When feelings are hard to name. For some people, “What are you feeling?” is a hard question most days and nearly impossible on bad ones. They notice a racing heart or a heavy stomach and can’t tell whether it’s worry or hunger, or something else entirely. Psychologists call this alexithymia, which means “no words for feelings”. It’s a trait that runs from a little to a lot, it’s common, especially among autistic people, and it isn’t an illness. The feelings are there and the words come slowly. Start with the body (“tight chest, skipped lunch”). Choose between two words rather than finding one from scratch (“more tired or more annoyed?”). Use the sky words if feeling words won’t come, and give it time. “I’ll tell you tomorrow” is a real answer.

  5. Plan what settles you on a calm day.

    The middle of being wound up is a terrible time to work out what helps you calm down. The Calm-Down Kit is a plan you make while you’re calm: what tends to set you off, two calming steps, and one sentence for stepping away that says when you’ll be back. It’s for you. It is never a list of instructions for the other person.

    Fourteen-year-old Elsie makes one for the rows she has with her mum about her phone. The next time a row heats up, she says, “I need ten minutes in my room, then I’ll come back,” goes upstairs, lies on her bed with her headphones in, and twelve minutes later comes back down to the kitchen, where her mum has put the kettle on. On a calm day, write your one stepping-away sentence, with the coming-back time in it.

  6. Your needs and limits are information, not demands.

    What helps you, what matters to you and what you’ve got left are facts about you, and said kindly they let other people plan around you. “A heads-up helps me.” “I can take this one, but not that one.” “I’m low today. Can we talk tomorrow?” You don’t have to justify a need. A reason is a kindness you can offer when you want to.

    Quinn lives with his two partners in a terraced house with one bathroom and a lot of shoes in the hall. He needs an hour alone after work, and once he has said so, calmly, it stops being a mystery and becomes part of the plan, like bin day. Two workpapers from other chapters can help with the words: Who did what, from the Preface, has ready-made kind ways to say no, and Say it so it lands, from Part Two, Chapter IV, turns a raw reaction into a plain request. Try writing one need you’ve never said out loud, and say it to the mirror as a fact, without the apology on the front.

    Dig deeper: saying your needs kindly

  7. Being wired differently is not being wrong.

    Some people take in words, noise, change and feelings differently from the way most social customs were built. That’s wiring, which you can understand, plan around and get support for if you choose to. A woman with ADHD keeps forgetting plans with a friend and has been calling herself hopeless for years. Now she tells her friends the one thing that helps, a text the day before, and she turns up (see Neurodivergent relationships).

    A mix-up runs both ways. When an autistic and a non-autistic person misread each other, the autistic person usually gets the blame. The double empathy problem, named by the autistic researcher Damian Milton, is the idea that the gap runs both ways: each person reads the other through their own way of communicating, and each finds the other hard to read. In one study, a story passed along a chain of autistic people kept as much detail as one passed along non-autistic people, while mixed chains lost detail faster. I find that study quietly cheering. Wired Differently shows how two differently wired people can each misread the other, and “Where did we lose each other?” lets both of you adjust a little.

    Dig deeper: wiring is not a flaw

  8. Explain yourself without over-sharing.

    Most moments need one of three short sentences. “This is how I’m wired. It won’t change, but here’s what helps.” “This is an old alarm. I’m working on it, and here’s what helps in the moment.” “Today’s a low-battery day.” You never owe anyone your history. Share the part that helps them help you. A man with a long-term illness tells a new friend from the climbing wall, “Some days I have less energy, and if I cancel, it isn’t about you,” and leaves his medical notes out of it (see When one of you is ill).

    Dig deeper: scripts, and how much to share

  9. Do your part, and only your part.

    Today you control your words, your effort, your rest, your limits and what you ask for. You don’t control anyone else’s mood or reply or pace, and trying to manage them will drain you faster than almost anything. It also helps to match energy with energy, meeting warmth with warmth, and giving a little less where you keep giving far more than comes back. A protected battery is what keeps you kind.

    A grown-up daughter has spent years trying to stop her father worrying about her. She can’t. What she can do is send “Home safe x” after long drives on the M6 and then let his worry be his (see For grown-up children). Write one thing you keep trying to control that isn’t yours, and next to it one thing that is. (If you are afraid of someone, this isn’t about managing them. Not safe at home? has people to talk to.)

Friday, 6:40pm

“Surprise! I’ve booked us dinner with my cousins tonight. Half seven at the Turkish place.” They meant it as a gift. You felt your chest tighten and heard yourself say “Great” in a voice so flat it could have been ironed. Later, at the sink, you wonder whether that was just you, something from years ago or the end of a long week. Probably some of each, and you still haven’t said anything.

You, in all your relationships

Your wiring and your weather come with you everywhere, starting with how you treat yourself on a hard day, when noticing that you’re low ought to come before deciding that you’re failing. Partners can give each other a heads-up or a quiet hour, and every partner has wiring of their own. With friends and family the question is often how you like to keep in touch and how much you can give this month. At work it may be an agenda the day before, or a minute before you answer. Housemates mostly negotiate noise, and who gets the bathroom first. If you live on your own, you get to build a week that suits how you’re built (see On my own).

Common mix-ups

A few come up again and again. Knowing yourself is mostly about what helps. A label can come later, or never, and the three layers and a Wave Code are everyday words with no diagnosis in them. “That’s just how I am” may well be true, especially about wiring, but it works far better as the start of a conversation (“This is how I’m wired, and here’s what helps”) than as the end of one. Telling people how you’re wired doesn’t mean they now have to manage you. It helps their words land, and you still do your part while they still have needs of their own.

And “I’m fine” sometimes means fine. It can also mean you’ve been tired for so long you stopped noticing. A quick reading once a week shows you the baseline you’ve got used to, which is sometimes a surprise.

A note on care: This part is general education. It isn’t a diagnosis, a test or treatment, and it can’t tell you whether you’re autistic, have ADHD or anything else, or what anyone else has. If you’d like to understand yourself more deeply, a qualified professional can help, if and when you want that. Nothing here is required, and nothing you try needs to be shared. If talking about any of this with someone ever feels unsafe, or you feel you can’t say no, stop and talk with a trained counselor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counseling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

To put the chapter to use, pick one reaction you had this week and write a line for each layer: the wiring part, the older part, today’s part. Take a one-minute reading of your weather. Then write one sentence about what helps you, in your own words, and keep it to yourself for a few days before you try it out on one person on a calm day. Know your own wiring, the “How to talk to me” card and How much are you carrying? are free on the site.
