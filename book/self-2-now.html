Know yourself: now, your wiring, your weather and your words

Paperback textRead the expanded version

Why the same words can land differently on different days and in different people, and how to understand today’s you well enough to say what helps.

Sunday, 7:50pm

Your friend pauses the movie. “You okay? You’ve been quiet all day.”

“Fine.”

Which isn’t a lie, exactly. You genuinely don’t know if you’re fine, or tired, or still chewing on something somebody said on Thursday. You couldn’t explain it to yourself, never mind to her. You reach for the remote and she lets you.

Quickly first. A flat “Fine.” on a Sunday evening is the surface. Under it sits the wiring, how much noise and change you can take in before it costs you. Under that sits the weather, how much sleep and food and week you’ve had. The roots are state and setting, and limited capacity. Now slowly, one layer at a time.

  1. Today is made of two layers you can work with.

    Your wiring is the lasting part, your temperament and pace and senses and the way words go in, and you work with that, by building a life that suits it. Today’s conditions are the passing part (sleep, hunger, the hour) and you work around those, usually by eating something or sleeping on it. Most of the frustration here comes from mixing the two up. (Then covers the third layer, the patterns life taught you.)

    Dean works nights at a distribution center. For months the school drop-off chatter at 8:10 was unbearable to him, all of it, the who-sat-next-to-who and the who-said-what in PE, and he started quietly wondering whether he was any good at being a dad. Then his partner swapped with him, so he does the 3:30 pickup now, after he’s slept, and the same chatter is funny again (see Different hours). His daughter told her teacher that her dad has “got nicer,” which he found out about at the parent-teacher conference.

    Write down one thing that always bothers you, which is probably wiring, and then one that only bothers you when you’re tired.

  2. Know how you like to be spoken to.

    Pace, tone, how much needs spelling out and what a silence means all change how words land. How you connect gives you words for it, and a four-letter Wave Code that’s meant to be light-hearted, so please don’t put it on your resume. The “How to talk to me” card fits the same thing onto one page you can hand over before a hard talk.

    Agnes is seventy-two and loves a phone call. Her grandson Liam loves a text and regards the phone ringing as a kind of emergency. For about a year each of them privately decided the other had gone a bit cool. It took a cousin, over the pie at Christmas, to say, “Grandma, he’s not ignoring you, he just doesn’t answer the phone. To anyone. Ever.” There’s a Sunday call at six now, with texts in between, and Liam mostly picks up.

    If you want somewhere to start, finish this. “I take things in best when…”

  3. Check your weather before you speak.

    Everything you hear goes through your own state on the way in. Part Two, Chapter III looks closely at the stress tank in a shared home. This chapter only adds the self side, a one-minute reading on your own before you decide what a hard moment means, and for that My weather today is the quick check and How much are you carrying? the fuller one, on a short worksheet.

    A couple living five hours apart worked out, eventually, that every bad call they’d had came at the end of her late shift, around midnight her time. Every single one. They moved anything serious to Saturday mornings, with coffee, and found they mostly agreed with each other (see Long distance).

    Words you could use “I’m running on empty tonight. Can we pick this up tomorrow morning?”

    One state, many words. The chapters and tools use a few everyday pictures for one thing, how much room you have today.

    • The three states (Chapter 2 calls them the ladder) are calm and connected, revved up, and running on empty.
    • My weather today uses sky words for the same three. Clear is calm and connected, gusty is revved up, and fogged in is running on empty. Its pressure is how much you were already carrying.
    • The stress tank is the score from How much are you carrying?, from 0 to 1, where higher means fuller.
    • The battery is the other side of the tank, the energy you have left, so a full tank means a low battery.

    “Gusty,” “revved up,” “a fairly full tank” and “a low battery” can all describe the same Tuesday afternoon. I’d pick one set and stick with it.

  4. Ask where this feeling is coming from.

    It’s 4:45 on a Friday and a colleague leans over the partition. “Quick question?”

    Your heart goes. Why? If you stop and look, there’s a fair bit in there. A jumpiness that belongs to a manager you had six years ago, who never once had a quick question. A long week. And nothing to eat since a cookie at eleven. What it needs is five minutes and a sandwich, and then you can answer him like a normal person.

    That’s the whole check, really: name the feeling, roughly, then ask whether it fits the size of the moment or feels older, and what it needs. The answer is usually more ordinary than you’d think, and it works best once the first wave has gone past.

    When feelings are hard to name. For some people “What are you feeling?” is a hard question most days and nearly impossible on bad ones. They notice a racing heart or a heavy stomach and can’t tell if it’s worry or hunger or something else. Psychologists call this alexithymia, which means “no words for feelings.” It’s a trait that runs from a little to a lot. It’s common, especially among autistic people, and it isn’t an illness. The feelings are there. The words come slowly. Start with the body (“tight chest, skipped lunch”). Pick between two words instead of hunting for one (“more tired or more annoyed?”). Use the sky words if feeling words won’t come. “I’ll tell you tomorrow” is a real answer.

  5. Plan what settles you on a calm day.

    Halfway through being wound up is a terrible time to work out what calms you down, so the Calm-Down Kit is a plan you make beforehand, with what tends to set you off, two things that help, and one sentence for stepping away that says when you’ll be back. It’s for you. It is never a list of instructions for anyone else.

    Elsie is fourteen and she and her mom have the same fight about her phone roughly twice a week. She made a kit, grudgingly, mostly to get her mom off her back. The next time it kicked off she said, “I need ten minutes, I’ll come back,” and went upstairs and lay on her bed with her headphones in, furious. Twelve minutes later she came down to find her mom had made some tea. Neither of them mentioned the phone, which wasn’t exactly a resolution, and was a great deal better than the usual.

    You could say “I need ten minutes. I’ll be back at a quarter after.”

  6. Your needs and limits are information, not demands.

    What helps you and what you’ve got left are facts about you. Said kindly, they let other people plan around you. “A heads-up helps me.” “I can do this one. Not that one.” “I’m low today, can it wait till tomorrow?” You don’t have to justify a need. A reason is a kindness you can add if you want to.

    For years I was told I was too sensitive and too particular about plans, and what was actually true is that I need less coming at me at once, and I need to know what’s coming. Heard as complaints, needs like that sound like things to fix in you. Understood as needs, they’re simply things to arrange.

    Needs as plain facts Marshall Rosenberg’s Nonviolent Communication teaches people to say what they saw, what they feel, what they need and what they’d like. It’s very widely used and has had few rigorous studies. The piece borrowed here is small: a need, said as a fact about you.

    Rosenberg (1999) · Early: a widely used practice with few rigorous studies

    Quinn lives with his two partners in a row house with one bathroom and an alarming number of shoes in the hall. He needs an hour on his own after work, and before he said so, that hour looked to everyone else like sulking. Once he’d said it, plainly, it turned into part of the week, like trash day, and one of the others now makes dinner on Thursdays.

    Two workpapers from other chapters help with the words. Who did what, from the Preface, has ready-made kind ways to say no. Say it so it lands, from Part Two, Chapter IV, turns a raw reaction into a plain request. Or try this. Write one need you’ve never said out loud, and say it to the bathroom mirror without the apology on the front.

  7. Being wired differently is not being wrong.

    Some people take in words, noise, change and feelings differently from the way most social customs were built, and that’s wiring, which you can understand and plan around if you want. Shona has ADHD and spent years calling herself hopeless because she kept missing plans with friends. Now she just tells people the one thing that works, a text the day before, and she turns up, usually on time and always with an apology ready in case (see Neurodivergent relationships).

    “Putting on my best normal” That’s the title of a study in which 92 autistic adults described camouflaging their differences, mostly out of a wish to fit in and connect, and often paying for it afterward in exhaustion. If a perfectly nice day leaves you flattened, it may be worth asking how much of it you spent performing.

    Hull and colleagues (2017) · Moderate: an interview study that shaped later research

    A mix-up runs both ways. When an autistic and a non-autistic person misread each other, the autistic person usually gets the blame. The double empathy problem, named by the autistic researcher Damian Milton, is the idea that the gap runs both ways. Each person reads the other through their own way of communicating, and each finds the other hard to read. In one study, a story passed along a chain of autistic people kept as much detail as one passed along non-autistic people, while mixed chains lost detail faster. I find that study quietly cheering. Wired Differently shows how two differently wired people can each misread the other, and “Where did we lose each other?” lets both of you adjust a little.

  8. Explain yourself without over-sharing.

    Most moments need one short sentence, and there are really only three. “This is how I’m wired, it won’t change, but this helps.” “This is an old alarm going off, I’m working on it.” “Low-battery day, sorry.” You never owe anyone your history, only the bit that helps them help you.

    Rob has a long-term illness and a new friend from the climbing wall who keeps suggesting things. “Some days I’ve got less in the tank,” he told him, on the bench, unlacing his shoes. “If I cancel, it isn’t you.” The friend said “Fair enough” and asked if he wanted a ride on Thursday, and that was the end of it, with no medical notes required (see When one of you is sick or disabled).

  9. Do your part, and only your part.

    Today you control your own words, your effort, your rest, your limits and what you ask for. You don’t control anyone else’s mood or pace, and trying to will flatten you quicker than almost anything. It helps, too, to match energy with energy. Meet warmth with warmth, and give a little less where you keep giving far more than comes back.

    Lorna is thirty-eight and her dad still worries about her driving. She has tried reassurance, statistics and once, unwisely, sarcasm, and none of it worked. What she does now is text “Home safe x” after any long run up I-95, and then she lets his worry be his (see For grown-up children). He still worries, and she has stopped trying to talk him out of it.

Friday, 6:40pm

“Surprise! I’ve booked us dinner with my cousins tonight. Seven thirty, the Turkish place.”

They meant it as a present. Your chest went tight and you heard yourself say “Great” in a voice with all the life ironed out of it. Later, at the sink, you try to work out which bit that was, the plan changing or something older or just the end of the week, and decide it was probably some of each. You still haven’t said anything, and the taxi’s booked for ten after.

Two ways to read this

Wiring and weather

If your wiring takes in noise, light and change at full volume, a busy room can use up the day’s energy by lunchtime, and needing a quiet hour afterward is simply the bill. If your wiring filters more of it out, the same room may feel lively and fine, and the quiet hour can look like sulking. Neither is the right way to be built. Each person mostly needs the other to believe their account of what it costs.

Where the fields meet. Neurobiology’s version is that your state sets the volume: a body on high alert hears every comment louder. Aromatherapy, read modestly, makes the same point about the setting, that softer light or a quieter room can help some people slow down. The claims about particular oils are debated, and nothing here leans on them.

You, in all your relationships

Your wiring and your weather come with you everywhere, starting with how you treat yourself on a bad day. Noticing that you’re low ought to come before deciding that you’re failing. Partners can give each other a heads-up or a quiet hour, and every partner has wiring of their own. With friends and family, the question is usually how you like to keep in touch and how much you can give this month. At work it might be an agenda the day before, or a minute before you answer. Roommates mostly negotiate noise, and the bathroom. If you live on your own, you get to build a week that fits how you’re built (see On my own).

Common mix-ups

Knowing yourself is mostly about what helps. A label can come later, or never, and the three layers and a Wave Code are everyday words with no diagnosis in them. “That’s just how I am” may well be true, especially about wiring, but it works much better as the start of a conversation than the end of one. Telling people how you’re wired doesn’t make them your managers either, though it does help your words land. You still do your part, and they still have needs of their own.

And “I’m fine” does sometimes mean fine. Sometimes it means you’ve been tired for so long you’ve stopped noticing. A quick reading once a week shows you the baseline you’ve got used to, which can be a bit of a shock.

A note on care: This part is general education. It isn’t a diagnosis, a test or treatment, and it can’t tell you whether you’re autistic, have ADHD or anything else, or what anyone else has. Nothing here is required, and nothing you try needs to be shared.

To put this to use, take one reaction you had this week and give it a line for each layer (the wiring bit, the older bit, today’s bit). Then take a one-minute reading of your weather. Then write one sentence about what helps you, and keep it to yourself for a few days before trying it on someone. Know your own wiring, the “How to talk to me” card and How much are you carrying? are free on the site.
