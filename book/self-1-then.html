Why you expect what you expect, and how the rules you picked up growing up still make small decisions for you today.

In the paperbacks: Book 1, Know Yourself Kindly, Chapter 1.

Sunday, 10:15am

Last night’s pan is soaking on the stove, there’s a can of tomatoes open by the kettle, and one of the three mugs has grown something. Your partner is asleep. You haven’t had a single thought yet, not one, and already there’s a voice going of course it’s you, it’s always you.

Nobody said that, and nobody else is even awake yet.

Across town Ray is drying a plate that nobody will ever see. His wife died two years ago, and he still can’t sit down with the paper until the last fork is back in the drawer. His mother couldn’t either.

A strong reaction can be read in quick strokes before it’s read slowly. There’s the moment itself, somebody late or a door shut too hard. Under it, today: sleep, hunger, the week. Under that, the rulebook you grew up with, and under that the root, which is mostly ownership and structure: who made the rules, and who gets to change them now. This chapter slows right down on the rulebook.

  1. You’re not one thing.

    Dot is seventy-one and her son is forty minutes late for Sunday lunch. When he finally comes in, cold air and apologies and a bunch of gas-station carnations, she says, “Well, the beef’s ruined,” in a voice she will regret by dessert.

    Three things went into that sentence. She has liked things on time since she was a girl; that’s wiring, and it isn’t going anywhere. Her father treated lateness as a kind of insult, and she picked that up without ever deciding to. And she was awake at four listening to the wind rattle the side gate. A strong reaction is usually all three at once, and it helps to know which one is shouting loudest. This chapter is about the middle one, what life taught you, and Now deals with the other two.

    Dot doesn’t say any of this. She says, “Sit down, then,” and gives him the end bit, which he likes, and the rest of lunch is all right.

    Dig deeper: the three layers

  2. Your lens came from somewhere.

    You learned what to expect from people long before you had words for it, at home mostly, but also at school and from whatever was on the TV at dinnertime. By the time you’re grown up the lens doesn’t feel like a lens anymore, it feels like simply seeing what’s there.

    Birthdays give it away. Imogen grew up with a party every year, a sheet cake, and cousins driving down from Baltimore with a balloon tied to the side mirror. Stuart got a card propped against the cornflakes and a slightly better dinner. So when Stuart books a quiet table for two on her thirtieth, he thinks he has nailed it. She spends the appetizers trying not to look at the door.

    It took them two more birthdays to say it out loud. “In my family this was a big deal,” she said in the end. “What was it like in yours?” He thought about it. “Mom made a pie.” The free tool Old rules, new choices walks through the idea slowly, and you can read one section and stop.

    The rules from home aren’t good or bad. They’re just yours, and worth a proper look. How you like to talk is a good first one to look at.

  3. Each stage of growing up asks its own question.

    A small child is working out whether the world is safe. A bit older and the questions change to whether you’re any good at things, where you fit, and whether you can be close to someone without disappearing into them. Later still, grown-ups start to wonder whether any of it has meant anything. What sticks is the answer you landed on at each stop, because you can go on using it for decades without noticing you’re using it.

    Eight questions in a life The idea that each stage of life asks its own question goes back to Erik Erikson, who described eight of them, from trust against mistrust in a baby to integrity against despair in later life. Think of it as a way of looking back, not a schedule anyone has measured.

    Erikson (1950) · Theory: very influential; a framework for reflection, not a measured sequence

    Jago moved schools four times between eleven and sixteen. He’s forty-four now, a site manager, and on the first morning of every new contract he still stands by the site trailer feeling like the new kid with the wrong shoes. He only figured out why last year. I suspect most of us have one of those questions still running somewhere.

    Dig deeper: the stages

  4. When something happened matters.

    An experience goes deeper when it lands on the question you were busy with at the time. Moving at four is mostly about a new bedroom. Moving at fourteen, in the thick of working out where you belong, can leave you feeling on the outside for years.

    Two sisters were six and fifteen when their parents split up. The younger one remembers glow-in-the-dark stars on her new ceiling and a different bus to school, and remembers it fondly, which drives her sister up the wall. The older one remembers every other Friday, standing in the hall with a duffel bag, being torn in two. They are both right, which is why that year still comes up at Christmas (see Family rifts). Deeper doesn’t mean permanent. People go on learning all their lives.

    Dig deeper: why timing matters

  5. Some old answers were learned without all the facts.

    Plenty of adults carry a sentence about themselves that they picked up before they were old enough to check it. I’m only all right when I’m useful. My needs are a nuisance. If I get it wrong, I’m bad. A child can’t always see what is going on for the grown-ups, so they go by how the room felt, and the conclusion they reach is often about themselves.

    Sheila has fostered for eleven years and she’s very good at it. She also can’t ask for a weekend off, the planned weekend when someone else has the children, not even in the week she put the cereal in the fridge and the milk in the cupboard. “They’ve got enough on,” she told her sister, who had called specifically to offer it.

    Looking again as an adult doesn’t mean deciding it was all fine back then. It means you’re allowed the full picture now. Try writing down one old belief about yourself, then underneath it what you’d say to a friend who believed the same thing about themselves.

    Dig deeper: old answers and fairer readings

  6. An old rule can read other people for you.

    If silence at home meant someone was angry, then a partner’s quiet evening can feel like weather coming in. And a rule nobody has ever said out loud can’t be agreed to, so the other person can only break it. If you loved me you’d just know. Whoever notices, does it. Each is somebody’s family rule, enforced on someone from a different family.

    Gemma’s roommate goes out at 7:10 without saying goodbye. Gemma, who grew up in a house where the door going without a word meant a fight was coming, spends the whole morning at work going over what she might have done. The roommate was on the 7:14 to a shift she’d nearly slept through.

    If you catch yourself reading a silence, you can just ask. “You went off quick this morning. Everything all right?” Usually it’s a train.

    When an old rule jumps in before you’ve had a chance to think, you don’t have to argue with it. Write it down, and look at it again on a calm day.

  7. Write down the rulebook from home.

    If you only do one exercise in this book, I’d make it this one. Finish these six lines the way the home you grew up in would have finished them.

    “In my family, love looked like…” “When someone was upset, we…” “The jobs were split like this…” “Resting was okay when…” “Asking for help meant…” “The unforgivable thing was…”

    The last one is usually the interesting one. The rulebook page has room to write them, and nothing is saved anywhere except on your own device.

    Rules handed down Family therapists in the Bowen tradition look at a family as one emotional unit, with roles passed from one generation to the next, which is more or less what your rulebook lines catch. It’s influential in practice and lightly tested in research, so I’d hold it loosely.

    Kerr and Bowen (1988) · Debated: influential in family therapy, limited testing

    Amira and Daniel did it one December, after Daniel’s mom had asked about Christmas again and Amira had gone very quiet. Somewhere on each of their pages, in different words, was the same rule. Christmas belongs to my family. It explained four years of tug of war in one go, and they laughed, a bit, before the actual negotiating started (see Two faiths, one family). One line today is plenty. If you show it to someone, you might say, “This is a rule I grew up with. I’m not saying it’s right. I just want you to know it’s there.”

    Dig deeper: the six rulebook lines

  8. You get to choose which rules to keep.

    Go back over your lines and ask of each one whether you still want it. Some you’ll keep. Some you’ll look at and think, well, that one can go.

    When a reaction feels too big for the moment, wait till you’ve settled and give it a minute’s questioning. Where did I learn this? How old does it feel? Is it true here, with this person, today?

    Bev runs a team at a credit union and realized, eventually, that she hid problems from her manager until they were too big to hide, because at home a mistake meant a mood that lasted days. She wrote a new rule on a yellow sticky note and stuck it to the bottom of her monitor. Mistakes are information. We fix them together. Three years on it has gone curly, and she won’t take it down.

    Pick one of your rules and write the version you’d rather live by. “I used to think ___. Now I’m choosing ___.” Later, if you like, swap rulebooks with someone you trust and pick some shared rules on purpose.

    Dig deeper: the lens check, and choosing together

  9. What you carry forward is yours.

    You didn’t choose the home you grew up in, or what anyone else did, or what you concluded at seven about how things work. What’s in your hands now is smaller than that and a good deal sturdier. You decide which rules you keep. You decide how you talk to yourself about them, and where your energy goes, and that includes people. Give your care where it’s met with care. Where it’s all one way, let it shrink back to something that feels fair, so you have some left.

    Marcia has organized every meet-up with an old school friend for nine years, and the friend always comes, always seems pleased, and has never once in all that time suggested a date. Last spring Marcia stopped chasing. She sent one message (“I’d love to see you, let me know when works”) and then nothing, which was harder than it sounds. The friend didn’t reply for five weeks, and in those five weeks Marcia went to a quiz, twice, with people who did reply (see Always the one reaching out?).

    Is there somewhere you give far more than comes back? You could give a little less there this month and see what happens.

    Dig deeper: your part, then and now

Tuesday, 11:30

Two people get the same text from the same friend. Can we talk tonight? Fern is pleased, because she likes a plan. Callum feels his stomach go, because in his house “we need to talk” never once meant anything good, and he spends his lunch hour sitting in the car rehearsing answers to accusations nobody has made.

The friend wanted to know if they were still up for splitting a cabin in Maine in August. Callum said yes too quickly and then had to call back about the dates.

Two ways to read this

A rule from home

Some brains take a rule at its word. “We don’t make a fuss” gets learned literally, and kept, long after anyone meant it. Other brains pick the rule up from the air of a room and couldn’t tell you the words at all. If yours is the first kind, the other person’s rule may sound vague. Ask for the plain version. If it’s the second, theirs may sound oddly rigid. It may simply be the only version they were ever given.

Where the fields meet. Politics would call a family a small government: the rules were made before you could vote on them. Philosophy adds a sorting habit, the fact from the guess, and a rule learned at six is often a guess that hardened into a fact.

You, in all your relationships

Your old rules come with you everywhere, starting with the voice you use on yourself after a mistake. At home they decide who notices, who plans and what a quiet evening means. Two roommates can share a fridge for a year before finding out they’ve been living by different rulebooks about mess. In families, the old roles switch back on before you’ve got your coat off (the helper, the peacemaker, the one who makes it funny). At work, someone praised mainly for being useful says yes to everything and then quietly seethes, and seeing the rule is often enough to buy them a pause before the next yes. After a breakup old rules can get louder, which makes it a good time to look at them kindly (see On my own). How you connect is a two-minute quiz on the talking rules you brought with you. Help by relationship lists pages for many more situations.

When a reaction is older than today

Some of what you learned at home, at school or in your first relationships still sets off reactions now, and some of it came from hard or frightening times. You might notice a reaction that feels older than the moment, arrives in your body before you can think, or follows the same kind of trigger each time. That isn’t a flaw, and having lived through something hard is never your fault. The aim is to take back control, so the old event stops running today. Notice it (“this feels older than today”), name it, steady your body, and then choose what to do next. How hurt heals goes further into how old hurts shape new reactions.

Common mix-ups

The worry people bring to this chapter most often is that looking back means blaming their family. It doesn’t have to. Many of the adults who raised us had lenses of their own, and if that doesn’t fit your story, you don’t have to make it fit. Then there’s the fear that anything which started in childhood is fixed for good. Many old patterns soften once you can see them and feel safe enough to try something else, and some leave a lasting sensitivity that you learn to carry and explain. Not every big reaction is an old wound, either. Quite often it’s just a full tank (Chapter III helps you tell leftover stress from this-moment stress). And some people decide there’s no point looking at their own rules if the other person won’t look at theirs. I don’t agree with that one at all. Knowing your own rules changes how you react whether or not anyone else reads a word of this.

A note on care: This part looks back at growing up. It is general education, not a diagnosis or therapy, and it can’t tell you what anyone has. Not every home felt calm or kind, so take only what is useful. If looking back feels heavy, you can stop and come back another day. Nothing here is required, and nothing you write needs to be shared.

If you want to go on, notice one “always” or “never” that went through your head this week. Whose voice was it in? How old did you feel? Where your lens came from, Old rules, new choices and Know your own wiring go further.
