The heterodyne squeal

Tune two radios to frequencies that are close but not identical, and you don't get silence — you get a squeal. The two signals overlap just enough to interfere, producing a third, uglier sound that neither radio was actually broadcasting. Engineers call this a heterodyne: the audible beat frequency created by two signals that are almost, but not quite, in sync.

This book borrows that image for a familiar moment: two people who are each saying something reasonable, in a tone shaped by their own fatigue, urgency, or nervous-system state, and getting a fight out of it that neither of them intended to start. The words weren't the problem. The mismatch in frequency was.

What sets the frequency

Three things tend to shift someone's "transmission frequency" without their noticing:

  • Pace. Someone who's been mobilized (see Chapter II's ladder — sympathetic activation) talks faster, interrupts sooner, and reads neutral pauses as stonewalling.
  • Register. A comment meant as a status update ("the sink's full again") can be transmitted — or received — as an accusation, depending on the tone underneath it.
  • Urgency. One person's "when you get a chance" and another person's "when you get a chance" can mean completely different windows of time.

None of this requires either person to be wrong. Two correctly-functioning radios on slightly different frequencies will squeal every time, and no amount of arguing about who's "really" at fault fixes the interference. What fixes it is retuning.

Retuning, practically

Before continuing a conversation that's started to squeal:

  1. Name the mismatch out loud, without blame: "I think we're on different frequencies right now."
  2. Check your own state against the ladder in Chapter II. You can't accurately retune while mobilized or shut down.
  3. Slow the pace deliberately — shorter turns, more pauses, lower volume — until the interference clears.
  4. Only then return to the actual content of the conversation.

The Radio Frequency Paradigm isn't a diagnosis of who's transmitting badly. It's a shared vocabulary for the moment when both of you are just out of sync, so neither of you has to carry the whole blame for a fight that was mostly a timing problem.
