Why we get out of tune

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar IV, Tune how you send and receive, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.” “I know, I’m just asking when.” Four sentences later, nobody is talking about the bins any more, and neither of you can point to the moment it turned.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. It’s like two radios slightly out of tune: together they make a crackle that neither one was making alone.

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on very different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they are close but not quite matched. That’s why people who know each other well can still clash.

    Dig deeper: why close can be noisy

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    How fast you’re talking. The tone, when a simple update is heard as a complaint. And how urgent it feels: “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and next week to the other.

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you’re wound up, you talk faster and hear a pause as a snub. When you’re worn out, everything sounds far away. The Nervous System Ladder helps you spot which one you’re in, in about thirty seconds.

    Dig deeper: how stress shifts your tune

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work fine will still crackle if they’re slightly off. Arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning will.

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say it out loud: “I think we’re out of sync.” Check how wound up you feel. Gently slow down, with shorter turns and more pauses. Only then go back to the actual topic.

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    When you return to the topic, say the fact, how you feel, and what you’d like. “The bins go out Wednesday morning. I get anxious when it’s late. Could you do it tonight?”

    Dig deeper: going back in

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A 90-second chat each day, like the one in WP-13, makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big argument.

    Dig deeper: staying in tune

Saturday, 10am

“We should sort out the spare room at some point.” One of you meant this month. The other heard today and has already cancelled their plans in their head. By lunchtime you’re both cross about a room nobody has touched.

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: Know your own pace, your usual tone and what “soon” means to you. Notice when stress speeds you up or slows you down.
  • Between you and others: When a clash starts, treat it as a tuning problem, not a moral failing. Retune together, then translate what you meant into words the other person can hear.

In real life

  • Partners: “Did you pay the gas bill?” is meant as a reminder. After a long day, it lands as “you forgot again.”
  • Family: A parent asks, “Are you coming on Sunday?” They mean it as a simple question. Their grown-up child hears guilt.
  • Roommates: A message says, “Can we talk about the kitchen?” One roommate meant a quick chat. The other spends all afternoon worrying.
  • Coworkers: “Quick question” arrives at 5:55pm. The sender meant tomorrow is fine. The reader thinks it’s urgent and stays late.

Common mix-ups

  • “Retuning means giving in.” It doesn’t. You still come back to the topic. You just come back when you can hear each other.
  • “If we’re out of sync, one of us must be wrong.” Two fair points in two different tones can still clash.
  • “Slowing down means I don’t care.” Slowing down is how you show you want to get it right.
  • “We should push through while we’re on the subject.” Pushing on through static usually makes more static.

Try this: Next time a conversation starts to go sideways, pause and say so kindly, instead of pushing through.

Before then, agree a shared phrase, like “I think we’re out of tune.” That way it feels like a signal, not a criticism.

For one week, each of you notes one moment you felt out of step. At the end, compare: was it mostly pace, tone or urgency?

When someone says “when you get a chance,” kindly ask, “By when would be good?”
