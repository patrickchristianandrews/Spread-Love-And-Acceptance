Part Two: Between us · Chapter I

Why we get out of tune: communication at home

Paperback textRead the expanded version

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

In the paperbacks: Book 3, Understanding Each Other, Chapter 1.

At a glance

  • A clash is often about how things were said. Pace, tone and urgency can be out of step even when both of you mean well.
  • Nobody has to be wrong. Say that you are out of tune, pause with a time to come back, and slow down.
  • Go back in with one clear sentence that says what happened, how you feel and what you would like.
  • A 90-second daily check-in keeps small drifts small.

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.”

“I know. I’m just asking when.”

“Why does it matter when?”

“It doesn’t, I just…”

Four sentences later nobody is talking about the trash. Somebody has brought up last Christmas. Neither of you could point to the moment it turned.

The radio picture in this chapter is my own. When two people who care about each other keep missing, what’s usually lacking is timing and a shared way of putting things, and this chapter is an attempt at the shared way.

Quickly: the trash is the surface. Underneath, two different paces, one fast and one slow. Underneath that, two bodies in two different states at the end of a long day. The roots are signal and noise, and state and setting. Then slowly, one knob at a time.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something perfectly reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. Think of two radios slightly out of tune with each other. Together they make a crackle neither of them was making on its own.

    “Is anyone using the oven tonight?” Lisa asked. Simple question. She had a lasagna in mind. Her roommate went pink and said, “Why, did I leave a mess?” and there was a pause in which the baking pan from Sunday, still soaking, became the only thing in the room. “I meant the lasagna,” Lisa said eventually. “I know,” said the roommate, who hadn’t known, and went and washed the tray.

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on completely different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they’re close but not quite matched, which is why people who know each other well still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we’re close to, because we assume they already understand.

    Two sisters who are on the phone every single day argue about vacation plans far more than either of them ever does with the cousins they see once a year, at baptisms and funerals. The cousins get things spelled out. The sisters get shorthand, half a sentence, “you know what I mean.” Quite often they don’t.

    With someone close to you, check one meaning today. “Just so I’ve got this right, you mean…?” It feels a bit formal, which is rather the point.

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    Pace is one, and tone is another, as when a plain update lands as a complaint. The third is urgency, since “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and some time before Easter to another. Texts lose most of their tone, so urgency is especially easy to misread there.

    We think our tone travels Across five experiments, people writing emails overestimated how well readers would pick up their tone, sarcasm or seriousness, largely because they couldn’t hear their own message without their own voice in it.

    Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005) · Strong: several experiments, widely cited

    Between meetings, Vikram texted his business partner two words. Need to talk. She read it as a crisis and lay awake until two in the morning running through every disaster available, including the bank, the landlord and a client she’d always slightly distrusted. At nine he called. It was about the printer. She has since asked him, politely and also not entirely politely, to add a time and the word “printer.”

    Ways to say it “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you’re tense you may talk faster and hear a pause as a snub. When you’re tired and low, your replies get slower and shorter. My weather today, a one-minute check, gives you a best guess at which state you’re in. People’s ordinary rhythms differ too, before stress even arrives, and Rhythms side by side lets you compare whose engine runs fast in the morning and whose at night.

    Barry comes in off a night shift at the depot and his son asks him about Saturday. He gets “Fine” and “Yep” and “Later.” His son decides, on the spot, that his dad doesn’t care about Saturday or, by extension, him. Barry is simply running on empty after ten hours of forklifts and a forty-minute drive home with the window down to stay awake. He’d have told you so if he’d had any words left. He went to bed.

    Dig deeper: how stress shifts your tune

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work perfectly will still crackle if they’re slightly off, and arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning will, which just means getting back in step.

    On the doorstep, coat still on, Sylvia tells her grown-up son he looks tired. She means it as care. He hears “you’re not coping,” which is what he has been privately worrying about all week, and says “Thanks, Mom” in a voice that could strip paint. Both of them are right about what they meant and what they heard. That’s exactly why arguing about it goes nowhere. They manage to get through the visit, just, and both feel bad about it on the way home.

    You could say “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say out loud that you think you’re out of sync, and check how tense you are. Either of you can call a pause, and whoever pauses names a time to come back. Slow down, take shorter turns, and only then go back to the actual topic.

    Two students sharing a room in the dorms agreed a pause word one calm Sunday. They picked “static,” mainly because it made them laugh. Three weeks later, at one in the morning, in the middle of a fight about someone’s music, one of them said it, through gritted teeth. They stopped. Both lay there fuming in the dark. They came back to it at nine the next evening, as agreed, and worked it out in ten minutes with headphones, a second set of headphones and a slightly sheepish package of cookies.

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    The first three minutes In 124 newlywed couples, how the first three minutes of a disagreement went predicted which couples had divorced six years later. It’s one lab’s sample and not a crystal ball, but it’s a good argument for opening gently.

    Carrère and Gottman (1999) · Moderate: one research group’s sample; striking, not widely replicated as a predictor

    When you go back to the topic, say what happened, how you feel and what you’d like, in that order and in one go. “The trash goes out early on Wednesday. I get anxious when they’re late. Could you put them out tonight?” Or, from one co-parent to the other, on a Sunday evening, “The swimsuit wasn’t in the bag and I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?” Write one about something small before you need it, so the shape is in your hand when you do.

    When you go back in, one clear sentence beats a speech. Write it down first. Speeches have a way of growing.

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in, makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big fight. Habit research suggests small routines at a regular time are easier to keep than big ones done rarely. The quiet, easy stretches are the ones that drift most, so keep it going even when nothing seems wrong (see Complacency).

    Orla is in Chicago and Marc is in Toronto. Every morning each of them sends one 90-second voice note, usually something like “Trash went out, my boss is still weird, I miss you.” On the weeks they forget, they notice it by Thursday, usually as an odd phone call about nothing in particular that leaves them both slightly put out.

    Ways to say it “Today I was carrying quite a lot. One thing I liked today was…”

    Dig deeper: staying in tune

Your part, and only your part. You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back. Saturday, 10am

“The spare room needs cleaning out at some point.”

She meant this month. He heard today, has already canceled soccer in his head and is quietly furious about it. By lunchtime you’re both upset about a room nobody has touched. When you finally stop and ask each other what “at some point” meant, it turns out you still don’t agree.

Faith, culture and family voices

For many couples, faith is where they turn first, and a shared faith can be a real help for many couples in staying in tune. Praying together, or a quiet moment after worship, can serve as a daily check-in. Some couples each pray on their own before they come back to a hard talk, with the pause still ending at a named time. Someone in your community you both trust can be a good first person to talk to.

Emeka and Ngozi, married twenty-six years, do their monthly look-back on a Sunday after church, over lunch, with the radio off. When a talk gets heated, either of them can say, “Let’s pray on it and come back at eight,” and at eight they do. Emeka says this works about nine times in ten. Ngozi says eight.

Culture shapes how you talk, too. In many families, respect for elders decides who can speak plainly to whom, and one habit helps a great deal. Each of you speaks to your own parents and relatives, once you’ve agreed the message together.

Words to try “Can we pray about this and talk again tonight?” “Mom, we love you, and we’ve thought about it carefully. This year we’ll visit in August instead of at Easter.”

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace and your own sense of urgency, so you notice when you’re the one running fast or slow. Learn your early signs of drifting. A clipped reply is a common one. So is a sudden urge to win. Learn how you take words in best and say it, once (“I hear things better in writing”). When you drift, step out, settle, come back. You don’t need anyone’s permission for that. Hamid lives alone and has noticed that his texts come out curt when he’s tired, so he now waits until morning to answer anything that matters (more in On my own).

One example, followed through

Cerys, Marisol and Dougie share a house and a car. Cerys and Marisol talk fast, while Dougie speaks slowly and thinks before he answers. At house meetings he kept getting talked over, and for a while the other two took his silence as agreement. It came out in March, when he said, quite mildly, that he had never actually agreed to the new car schedule. Marisol said “Since when?” and Dougie said “Since always,” and there was a long, uncomfortable pause. They tried a simple change, a round where each person speaks once before anyone answers. It felt stilted, and Cerys kept wanting to jump in and sat on her hands, literally. By the third meeting Dougie was saying more, and the schedule he suggested is the one they use. Some evenings it still turns into two fast talkers and one quiet one, until somebody says “round” and they reset. Help by relationship has pages for many other kinds of shared life.

Two ways to read this

Out of tune, both ways

Some people think by talking and need the answer right away. Others think first and talk second, and a quick reply asked for too soon comes out wrong. If you talk to think, the slower person’s pause isn’t agreement, and it isn’t a protest. It’s thinking. If you think first, the fast talker isn’t trying to win. Out loud is where their thinking happens.

Where the fields meet. Music has a name for a good conversation: call and response, one phrase answering another at a tempo both can keep. Neurobiology explains why the tempo slips, since your state sets the volume, and a stressed body changes station without asking you.

Common mix-ups

Retuning isn’t giving in, because you still come back to the topic, at a time when you can hear each other. Being out of sync doesn’t mean one of you is wrong, either. Two fair points in two different tones will still clash. And a pause never means the topic is dropped, since a pause comes with a time to come back, or it isn’t a pause.

To try it, agree a shared phrase while things are calm (“I think we’re out of tune” does fine) so that it works as a signal when you need it. For a week, each of you note one moment you felt out of step, and on the weekend compare whether it was mostly pace, tone or urgency. Rewind the talk, Say it so it lands and the 90-second daily check-in are free on the site.

A pause is for both of you. If one person is never allowed to pause, or a “pause” is used to punish with days of silence, that isn’t retuning.
