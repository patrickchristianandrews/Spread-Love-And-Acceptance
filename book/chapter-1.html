Why we get out of tune: communication at home

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why two kind, reasonable people can end up in a tangle neither of them started, and how to get back in step.

In short

  • A clash is often about how things were said. Pace, tone and urgency can be out of step even when both of you mean well.
  • Nobody has to be wrong. Say that you are out of tune, pause with a time to come back, and slow down.
  • Go back in with one clear sentence that says what happened, how you feel and what you would like.
  • A 90-second daily check-in keeps small drifts small.

Tuesday, 6:40pm

“I said I’d do it.”

“I know, I’m just asking when.”

Four sentences later nobody is talking about the bins any more. Somebody has mentioned last Christmas. Neither of you could point to the moment it turned.

The radio picture in this chapter is my own. The About page on the website describes how, for years, my closest relationships “filled up with static”, with the same misunderstanding arriving again and again, and how what was missing was never care, on either side. It was timing, and a shared way of putting things. This chapter is the shared way.

  1. The tangle often isn’t about the words.

    Two people can each say something perfectly reasonable and still clash, because the tone underneath doesn’t match. Think of two radios slightly out of tune with each other. Together they make a crackle that neither of them was making alone.

    One housemate asks, “Is anyone using the oven tonight?” The other says, “Why, did I leave a mess?” A plain question about a lasagne has been heard as a complaint about a baking tray. After your next small clash, ask yourself whether it was the words or the way they came out. It’s usually the way.

    You could say “I think the words were fine. Something in how we said them got crossed.”

    Dig deeper: the radio static

  2. Nearly in step is the noisy part.

    Radios on very different stations don’t whistle at each other. The whistle comes when they are close but not quite matched, which is why people who know each other well can still clash. Research suggests we check our meaning less with people we’re close to, because we assume they already understand us.

    Two sisters who talk on the phone every day argue about holiday plans far more often than either of them does with the cousins they see once a year at a christening or a funeral. The cousins get spelled out; the sisters get shorthand. With someone close to you, check one meaning today: “Just so I’ve got it right, you mean…?”

    Words you could use “I think I assumed you knew what I meant. Let me say it more clearly.”

  3. Three things knock you out of tune.

    How fast you’re talking is one. Tone is another, as when a simple update is heard as a complaint. The third is how urgent something feels, since “when you get a chance” can mean tonight to one person and some time next week to another. Texts lose most of their tone, which makes urgency especially easy to misread.

    One of two business partners texts “Need to talk” between meetings. The other reads it as a crisis and lies awake until two in the morning running through every possible disaster, including the bank. It turns out to be about the printer. Add a time to your next request, so “at some point” becomes “by Friday”, and an update can say it’s an update.

    Ways to say it “By when would be good?” “Is this an update, or a worry?”

    Dig deeper: pace, tone and urgency

  4. Stress changes your station.

    When you are tense, you may talk faster and be more likely to hear a pause as a snub. When you are tired and flat, your replies get slower and shorter. The Nervous System Ladder, a thirty-second self-check, gives you a best guess at which state you are in.

    A dad gets home from a night shift at the depot and answers his teenage son in single words. “Fine.” “Yep.” “Later.” His son decides he doesn’t care. He’s simply running on empty, and he’d tell you so if he had the words left.

    Words to try “I’m running low right now, and it isn’t about you. Can we talk after I’ve slept?”

    Dig deeper: how stress shifts your tune

  5. Nobody has to be wrong.

    Two radios that both work perfectly will still crackle if they are slightly off, and arguing about whose fault the static is won’t clear it. Retuning will, which just means getting back in step.

    A grandmother tells her grown-up son, at the door, that he looks tired. She means it as care. He hears “you’re not coping”. Both of them are right about what they meant and what they heard, which is exactly why arguing about it goes nowhere. In your next clash, try “we” instead of “you”: “We got crossed there.”

    You could say “I don’t think either of us meant it that way. Can we try that again?”

    Dig deeper: taking the blame out

  6. Get back in tune before you carry on.

    Say out loud that you think you’re out of sync, and check how tense you feel. Either of you can call a pause, and whoever pauses names a time to come back. Slow down, take shorter turns, and only then go back to the actual topic.

    Two roommates in halls agree on a pause word on a calm Sunday, and they pick “static” because it makes them laugh. Three weeks later, in the middle of a row about someone’s music at one in the morning, one of them says it. They stop. They come back at nine the next evening, as agreed, and sort it out in ten minutes. Agree a pause word with someone today, while you’re both calm.

    Words you could use “I need ten minutes. I’ll come back at half past.”

    Dig deeper: the four steps to retune

  7. Go back in with a clear sentence.

    When you return to the topic, say what happened, how you feel and what you’d like, in that order and in one go. “The bins go out early on Wednesday morning. I get anxious when they’re late. Could you put them out tonight?” Or, from one co-parent to another: “The swim kit wasn’t in the bag and I felt stressed at drop-off. Could you pack it on Sunday nights?” It’s worth writing one of these about something small before you need it, so the shape is in your hand when you do.

  8. Small daily check-ins keep you in tune.

    A short chat each day, like the 90-second daily check-in, makes tiny corrections before small things drift into a big argument. Habit research suggests small routines at a regular time are easier to keep than big ones done rarely. The quiet, easy stretches are the ones that drift most, so keep it going even when nothing seems wrong (see Complacency).

    A long-distance couple, one in Leeds and one in Toronto, send each other one 90-second voice note every morning. On the weeks they forget, they notice the difference by Thursday, usually as a slightly odd phone call about nothing much.

    Ways to say it “Today I was carrying quite a lot. One thing I liked today was…”

    Pick a time and a cue (straight after dinner works for a lot of people) and try it for three days.

    Dig deeper: staying in tune

Your part, and only your part. You control what you send, whether you check how it landed, and whether you step out to retune. You don’t control how it’s heard or how fast the other person settles. Matching energy here means matching care and effort, never volume: if someone gets louder, you don’t have to follow. Stay at your level, and give the conversation the energy it gives back. Saturday, 10am

One of you says the spare room needs clearing out at some point. She meant this month, and he heard today and has already cancelled his plans in his head. By lunchtime you are both upset about a room nobody has touched, and when you stop to ask what “at some point” meant, you find you are still not sure you agree.

Faith, culture and family voices

For many couples, faith is where they turn first, and a shared faith can be one of the strongest tools for staying in tune. Praying together, or a quiet moment after worship, can serve as a daily check-in. Some couples each pray on their own before they come back to a hard talk, with the pause still ending at a named time. A pastor, imam, rabbi, priest or trusted elder can be a good first person to talk to, and many can suggest a trained counsellor who shares your faith.

Emeka and Ngozi, married for twenty-six years, do their monthly look-back on a Sunday after church, over lunch. When a talk gets heated, either of them can say, “Let’s pray on it and come back at eight,” and they do.

Culture shapes how you talk too. In many families, respect for elders decides who can speak plainly to whom, and one habit helps a great deal, which is that each of you speaks to your own parents and relatives once you have agreed the message together.

Words to try “Can we pray about this and talk again tonight?” “Mum, we love you, and we’ve thought about it carefully. This year we’ll visit in August instead of at Easter.”

On your own: your side of the tuning

Getting out of tune takes two, but retuning can start with one. You can learn your own pace and your own sense of urgency, so you notice when you are the one running fast or slow. Learn your early signs of drifting. A clipped reply is a common one, and so is a sudden need to win. Learn how you take words in best and say it once, as in “I hear things better in writing.” When you drift, step out, settle and come back, which you can do without anyone’s permission. Hamid lives alone and has noticed that his texts to friends come out short when he is tired, so now he waits until morning to answer anything that matters (more in On my own).

One example, followed through

Cerys, Marisol and Dougie share a house and a car, and two of them talk fast while the third, Dougie, talks slowly. At house meetings Dougie kept getting talked over, and for a while the other two read his silence as agreement. It came out in March, when he said he had never actually agreed to the new car rota. They tried a simple change, a round in which each person speaks once before anyone answers. It felt stilted at first, and Cerys kept wanting to jump in. By the third meeting Dougie was saying more, and the rota he suggested is the one they now use. They still have the occasional evening when the meeting turns into two fast talkers and one quiet one, and someone usually says “round” to reset it. Help by relationship has pages for many other kinds of shared life.

Common mix-ups

Retuning isn’t giving in. You still come back to the topic, at a time when you can hear each other. Being out of sync doesn’t mean one of you is wrong, either; two fair points in two different tones will still clash. And a pause never means the topic is dropped. A pause always comes with a time to come back, or it isn’t a pause.

To try it, agree on a shared phrase while things are calm, such as “I think we’re out of tune”, so that it works as a signal when you need it. For one week, each of you can note one moment you felt out of step, and at the end you can compare whether it was mostly pace, tone or urgency. Rewind the talk, Say it so it lands and the 90-second daily check-in are free on the site.

A limit to keep in mind: These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking things through ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counsellor on your own. When someone is afraid of their partner, counselling together isn’t advised. Not safe at home? lists free, private hotlines.

A pause is for both of you. If one person is never allowed to pause, or a “pause” is used to punish with days of silence, that isn’t retuning. Signs to look out for.
