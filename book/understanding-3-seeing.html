How we see each other: perception, bias, and the stories we tell

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why two people can watch the same moment and come away with two different stories, and how to see others, and yourself, a little more fairly.

In short

  • Seeing is a best guess. Your brain fills in a lot of what you think you see and hear.
  • We fill gaps with stories, and the story often feels like a fact.
  • We judge ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions, and that gap causes a lot of hurt.
  • Asking is kinder and more accurate than trying to read someone’s mind.

Wednesday, 12:15pm

You wave at a colleague across the canteen, tray in one hand, soup slopping. She looks straight through you and walks off.

All afternoon. All afternoon you go back through Monday’s meeting, and that thing you said about the budget, and the email you didn’t reply to. At four o’clock she messages. Sorry I didn’t stop earlier, I’d just had a call about my dad. She never saw you.

How feelings work and Stress and the nervous system look inward. This chapter looks outward, at how we see other people, and why misunderstandings are so common even between kind and clever people. Before I built any of this I spent my career as a government auditor, walking into situations where nobody could tell me straight what had happened. Almost everyone, it turned out, was doing their best with the piece of the picture they could actually see. That’s more or less this chapter.

Some of the tools elsewhere depend on it. Who did what (the Preface to Part Two) exists because everyone sees their own effort more clearly than other people’s, so two honest guesses of “I do about 60%” can add up to well over 100%, and one shared page settles it. Rewind the talk (Part Two, Chapter III) slows down the habit of judging ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions. And the Chat analyzer follows this chapter’s last idea, that asking beats guessing.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think, and your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well, and fast. It also means what you see is partly what you expected, so two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

    Under-12s, a freezing Sunday morning, a pitch that is mostly mud. Archie’s mum sees him giving up. She says so, quietly, into her scarf. His dad, beside her with the flask, sees him being careful with the ankle he went over on last week. Same boy. Same touchline. Same tea. They argue about it all the way home and Archie, in the back, says nothing, because he was actually thinking about his tea.

  2. You see what you’re looking for.

    Attention is like a torch in a dark room. You see what it lights up and can miss what it doesn’t, even when it’s right in front of you. If you’re looking for proof that someone doesn’t care, you will notice every missed call and none of the little kindnesses.

    In her first week Rosie decided her new manager didn’t like her. For a month she clocked every short email he sent. There were a lot. He sends short emails to everyone, including, as it turned out, his wife. What Rosie somehow didn’t clock was that he’d given her the best project on the team. A colleague pointed it out in the pub. Rosie said, “Oh.” Then, “Oh no.”

    If there’s someone you’re struggling with, try writing down one kind thing they do each day for a week. It’s an odd exercise. It tends to move the torch.

    Dig deeper: the torch of attention

  3. We fill the gaps with stories.

    When we don’t know why someone did something, the mind makes up a reason, fast. A late reply becomes anger. A closed door becomes rejection. And the story arrives feeling like a fact. Memory works the same way, because every time we remember something we rebuild it a little, so two people can honestly remember the same evening differently.

    Two days without a reply. By Sunday evening you have decided she’s fed up with you, and you’ve drafted, then deleted, a message saying you’re sorry for whatever it was. On Monday morning she sends seventeen photographs from a campsite in Snowdonia with no signal, most of them of the same sheep.

    You could say “The story I’m telling myself is that you’re upset with me. Is that right?”

    Dig deeper: stories and memory

  4. We judge ourselves by our reasons, and others by their actions.

    When you’re late, you know why. The traffic, a phone call, a child who lost a shoe. When someone else is late you only see the lateness, and it’s very easy to decide they’re careless. We all tend to blame other people’s character for things that are really about their situation.

    A driver cuts in front of you on the ring road and you call him an idiot, not quite under your breath. The next week your mum has a fall and you’re racing to the hospital, and you cut someone up yourself, badly, at the roundabout. You know exactly why you did it. You’d quite like them to know too. They don’t, and in their car, right now, you are an idiot.

    Thoughts you could try “What might be going on for them that I can’t see?” “If I did this, what would my reason be?”

    Dig deeper: actions and reasons

  5. Quick shortcuts can lead us astray.

    The brain uses shortcuts to save time, and plenty are useful. Some lead to unfair judgements. A first impression that colours everything after it. One bad moment that outweighs ten good ones. A quick guess about someone from their age or accent or looks or group. Everyone has these. The trouble starts when nobody notices them.

    On day one, the new tenant left a box in the hall. Just one, for an afternoon. Tash decided there and then that he was messy, and every bit of mess after that was proof. Her own mug collection on the landing, eleven mugs at the last count, went entirely unseen, by her. He saw it. He didn’t say anything for months, which, when it finally came out, was its own small row.

    Dig deeper: shortcuts and bias

  6. Everyone feels like the one who sees clearly.

    Most of us feel we see things as they really are, and anyone who disagrees must be missing something. The other person feels exactly the same about you. Each of you can see the other’s lens much more easily than your own.

    Two brothers in their fifties argued, at their mum’s eightieth, about how they’d been brought up. “I’m just being realistic,” said one. “So am I,” said the other. They were both right. Each was being entirely realistic about the childhood he had lived through, which turned out, when they finally compared notes in the car park, to have been quite a different childhood, in the same house, four years apart.

    Words you could use “I think we’re both seeing this from where we stand. Can you tell me how it looks from yours?”

    Dig deeper: why we each feel like the realist

  7. “Us” and “them” changes what we see.

    People split into groups quickly, even over tiny things. We tend to see our own group as varied and full of individuals and other groups as all the same, and we give our own side the benefit of the doubt. Spending real time with people from the other group, as equals, is one of the best ways to soften this.

    At one depot the night shift calls the day shift lazy and the day shift calls the night shift sloppy, and has done since before anyone can remember. Then there was a joint training day. Bad buffet. A fire drill in the rain. By the afternoon people were using each other’s names. It didn’t fix everything. It did stop the notes on the kettle.

    Have one real conversation this month with someone from a group you tend to see as “other”, and ask about their day before you ask about their views.

    Dig deeper: us and them

  8. Ask, don’t guess.

    Imagining yourself in someone’s shoes is a kind start, though research suggests it doesn’t make us much more accurate about what they think, while asking does. A plain, curious question like “What was that like for you?” beats the cleverest guess.

    Every Sunday Moira’s grandson sat at her kitchen table glued to his phone, and every Sunday she decided a bit more firmly that he hated coming. One week she just asked. “Do you not like coming here, love?” He looked up, horrified. He’d been messaging a friend who was having a terrible time. He liked her house because it was calm, he said. And the crumble. He wouldn’t have mentioned the crumble if she hadn’t asked. She makes it every week now. He still looks at his phone.

    Ways to say it “Can I check what you meant?” “What was going on for you?” “I might have this wrong. How did it look from your side?”

Thursday, 12:20pm

She’s in the canteen again. This time you go over and ask how her dad is. She tells you about the hospital, and the waiting, and the car park charges, which seem to be the thing she’s angriest about, and you end up sharing a table until the end of the break. Your soup goes cold. You don’t mind.

In everyday life

We tell stories about ourselves too, and “I always mess things up” is usually built from a few bright memories that leave a lot out. After years together, partners stop checking and start assuming. And “I know what you meant” can do a lot of quiet damage in a long relationship. Children get labelled early, the difficult one, the easy one, and the label becomes a lens that only sees what fits. With ADHD, attention gets pulled by whatever is new or urgent and can lock on hard, so someone may genuinely not have seen or heard. Get their attention first and put requests in writing. Common mix-ups

Nobody sees things exactly as they are. Everyone sees through a lens, and knowing that is where fairness starts. Memory isn’t a recording, either. It gets rebuilt each time, so two honest people can remember the same evening differently and both be telling the truth. Giving someone the benefit of the doubt doesn’t mean ignoring what happened. It means checking the story before you decide, and if something hurtful keeps happening, that matters. And a face or a tone is a clue to what someone meant. Faces and tones vary a lot between people and between cultures, so a face is a weaker clue than it feels.

If you want to help someone else with this

Start with how they feel. Make sense of the feeling first, and only then wonder aloud whether there might be another way to see it. Offer information instead of verdicts. “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.” Check your own guesses out loud (“I assumed he was annoyed, I’ll ask”), which shows how it’s done. Be careful with labels for children and colleagues, because they stick and then shape what everyone sees. And believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone that what happened to them didn’t happen.

Words to try “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”

When to get more help

Seeing other people’s side is a strength. It should never mean doubting yourself when you know something is wrong. If someone often tells you that what you saw, heard or remember didn’t happen, that you’re “too sensitive” or “imagining it,” and you find yourself unsure of your own mind, take that seriously. It can be part of controlling behaviour. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? can help, privately.

It may also help to talk to a counsellor or other trained professional if you notice you often feel others are against you, find it hard to trust anyone, or keep getting stuck in the same painful stories about yourself. Those patterns are common and understandable, and they often change with support.

This chapter is general education, not diagnosis or therapy.
