How we see each other: perception, bias, and the stories we tell

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why two people can watch the same moment and come away with two different stories, and how to see others, and yourself, a little more fairly.

At a glance

  • Seeing is a best guess. Your brain fills in a lot of what you think you see and hear.
  • We fill gaps with stories, and the story often feels like a fact.
  • We judge ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions, and that gap causes a lot of hurt.
  • Asking is kinder and more accurate than trying to read someone’s mind.

Wednesday, 12:15pm

You wave at a colleague across the canteen, tray in one hand, soup slopping. She looks straight through you and walks out.

All afternoon. All afternoon you go back through Monday’s meeting, that thing you said about the budget, the email you didn’t reply to. At four o’clock she messages. Sorry I didn’t stop earlier, I’d just had a call about my dad. She hadn’t seen you at all.

How feelings work and Stress and the nervous system look inward. This chapter looks outward, at how we see other people, and why misunderstandings are so common even between kind and clever people. Before I built any of this I spent my career as a government auditor, walking into situations where nobody could tell me straight what had happened. Almost everyone, it turned out, was doing their best with the piece of the picture they could actually see. This chapter is more or less about that.

Some of the tools elsewhere depend on it. Who did what (the Preface to Part Two) exists because everyone sees their own effort more clearly than other people’s, so two honest guesses of “I do about 60%” can add up to well over 100%, and one shared page settles it. Rewind the talk (Part Two, Chapter III) slows down the habit of judging ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions. And the Chat analyzer follows this chapter’s last idea, that asking beats guessing.

A wave that wasn’t returned, quickly. The surface is a colleague who walked past. Underneath, a story your mind wrote in a second. Underneath that, a brain saving effort with a shortcut. The roots are signal and noise, and limited capacity. Then slowly, shortcut by shortcut.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think, and your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well, and fast. It also means what you see is partly what you expected, so two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

    They saw a game Students from two rival universities, Princeton and Dartmouth, watched the same rough American football game and saw it very differently, right down to how many fouls each side committed. Same match, two games.

    Hastorf and Cantril (1954) · Moderate: a classic single case study, widely cited

    Under-12 soccer, a freezing Sunday morning, a field that is mostly mud. Archie’s mom sees him giving up, and says so, quietly, into her scarf. His dad, beside her with the flask, sees him being careful with the ankle he went over on last week. They argue about it all the way home and Archie, in the back, says nothing, because he was actually thinking about his tea.

  2. You see what you’re looking for.

    Attention is like a flashlight in a dark room. You see what it lights up and can miss what it doesn’t, even when it’s right in front of you. If you’re looking for proof that someone doesn’t care, you will notice every missed call and none of the little kindnesses.

    In her first week Rosie decided her new manager didn’t like her. For a month she clocked every short email he sent, and there were a lot. He sends short emails to everyone, including, as it turned out, his wife. What Rosie somehow didn’t clock was that he’d given her the best project on the team. A colleague pointed it out at the bar after somebody’s going-away party, and Rosie said, “Oh.” Then, “Oh no.”

    If there’s someone you’re struggling with, try writing down one kind thing they do each day for a week. It’s an odd exercise, and it tends to move the flashlight.

  3. We fill the gaps with stories.

    When we don’t know why someone did something, the mind makes up a reason, fast. A late reply becomes anger, and a closed door becomes rejection. And the story arrives feeling like a fact. Memory works the same way, because every time we remember something we rebuild it a little, so two people can honestly remember the same evening differently.

    Two days without a reply. By Sunday evening you have decided she’s fed up with you, and you’ve drafted, then deleted, a message saying you’re sorry for whatever it was. On Monday morning she sends seventeen photographs from a campsite in Snowdonia with no signal, most of them of the same sheep. One message, “The story I’m telling myself is that you’re upset with me. Is that right?” would have saved two days of it.

  4. We judge ourselves by our reasons, and others by their actions.

    When you’re late, you know why, because it was the traffic or a phone call or a child who lost a shoe. When someone else is late you only see the lateness, and it’s very easy to decide they’re careless. We all tend to blame other people’s character for things that are really about their situation.

    The fundamental attribution error Lee Ross gave this habit its name: when we explain what someone else did, we tend to underrate the situation and overrate their character. It’s well replicated, though weaker in some cultures than in others.

    Ross (1977) · Strong: foundational and widely replicated

    A driver cuts in front of you on the beltway and you call him an idiot, not quite under your breath. The next week your mom has a fall and you’re racing to the hospital, and you cut someone up yourself, badly, at the intersection. You know exactly why you did it. You’d quite like them to know too. They don’t, and in their car, right now, you are an idiot.

    Thoughts you could try “What might be going on for them that I can’t see?” “If I did this, what would my reason be?”

  5. Quick shortcuts can lead us astray.

    The brain uses shortcuts to save time, and plenty are useful. Some lead to unfair judgments, like a first impression that colors everything after it, or one bad moment that outweighs ten good ones, or a quick guess about someone from their age or accent or looks or group. Everyone has these, and the trouble only starts when nobody notices them.

    Bad is stronger than good That’s the real title of a wide review, which found that bad events, feedback and behavior usually hit harder and last longer than equally good ones. There are exceptions, but it explains how one box in the hall can outweigh months of tidiness.

    Baumeister and colleagues (2001) · Strong: a widely cited review; the pattern is accepted, with known exceptions

    On day one, the new tenant left a box in the hall, just the one, for an afternoon. Tash decided there and then that he was messy, and every bit of mess after that was proof. Her own mug collection on the landing, eleven mugs at the last count, went entirely unseen, by her, though he saw it. He didn’t say anything for months, which, when it finally came out, was its own small row.

  6. Everyone feels like the one who sees clearly.

    Most of us feel we see things as they really are, and anyone who disagrees must be missing something. The other person feels exactly the same about you. Each of you can see the other’s lens much more easily than your own.

    Two brothers in their fifties argued, at their mom’s eightieth, about how they’d been brought up. “I’m just being realistic,” said one. “So am I,” said the other. They were both right. Each was being entirely realistic about the childhood he had lived through, which turned out, when they finally compared notes in the parking lot, to have been quite a different childhood, in the same house, four years apart.

    Words you could use “I think we’re both seeing this from where we stand. Can you tell me how it looks from yours?”

    Dig deeper: why we each feel like the realist

  7. “Us” and “them” changes what we see.

    People split into groups quickly, even over tiny things. We tend to see our own group as varied and full of individuals and other groups as all the same, and we give our own side the benefit of the doubt. Spending real time with people from the other group, as equals, is one of the best ways to soften this.

    At one depot the night shift calls the day shift lazy and the day shift calls the night shift sloppy, and has done since before anyone can remember. Then there was a joint training day, with a bad buffet and a fire drill in the rain. By the afternoon people were using each other’s names. It didn’t fix everything, though the notes on the kettle stopped.

    Have one real conversation this month with someone from a group you tend to see as “other,” and ask about their day before you ask about their views.

    Dig deeper: us and them

  8. Ask, don’t guess.

    Imagining yourself in someone’s shoes is a kind start, though research suggests it doesn’t make us much more accurate about what they think, while asking does. A plain, curious question like “What was that like for you?” beats the cleverest guess.

    Every Sunday Moira’s grandson sat at her kitchen table glued to his phone, and every Sunday she decided a bit more firmly that he hated coming. One week she just asked. “Don’t you like coming here, sweetie?” He looked up, horrified, with his thumb still on the screen. He’d been messaging a friend who was having a terrible time. He liked her house because it was calm, he said, and for the crumble. He wouldn’t have mentioned the crumble if she hadn’t asked. She makes two now, and sends one home with him in a tin she never gets back.

She’s in the canteen again. This time you go over and ask how her dad is. She tells you about the hospital and the waiting and the parking lot charges, which seem to be the thing she’s angriest about. You end up sharing a table until the end of the break. Your soup goes cold. You don’t mind.

Two ways to read this

Blunt feedback, two ways

Some people hear softening as part of the message and take a bare comment as a sign that something’s wrong between you. Others hear the softening as noise and want the point first, because a clear point is quicker to fix. If you like softening, “The second half doesn’t work” may be the most helpful thing that person can say, with no judgment of you in it. If you like it plain, adding one warm line for the other person costs very little and lets the point land.

Where the fields meet. Philosophy’s habit of separating the fact from the guess is most of this chapter. Economics explains why the guess comes so fast: attention is a budget, and the brain saves it with shortcuts that are usually right and sometimes badly wrong.

In everyday life

We tell stories about ourselves too, and “I always mess things up” is usually built from a few bright memories that leave a lot out. After years together, partners stop checking and start assuming. And “I know what you meant” can do a lot of quiet damage in a long relationship. Children get labeled early, the difficult one, the easy one, and the label becomes a lens that only sees what fits. With ADHD, attention gets pulled by whatever is new or urgent and can lock on hard, so someone may genuinely not have seen or heard. Get their attention first and put requests in writing. Two memories of one evening, or gaslighting?

Two people often remember the same evening differently. Memory isn’t a recording. Each person noticed and kept different parts, and stress, tiredness and strong feelings blur it further, so most “That’s not what happened!” arguments are two honest memories. Gaslighting describes something much rarer, and the word does real harm when it’s used for an ordinary disagreement.

For something to be gaslighting, these are usually all present:

  • the same person denies, again and again, things the other person saw, heard or knows happened,
  • they know it happened, so the denial is deliberate,
  • the aim is to make the other person doubt their own memory or judgment in general,
  • it usually gains them something, such as winning, avoiding blame or getting their way.

Not enough on its own:

  • two people remembering it differently,
  • someone misremembering, especially when tired, stressed or upset,
  • disagreeing about what something meant,
  • one heated “That never happened!” in the middle of a fight,
  • “I don’t think I meant it like that.”

Ask whether it happens in both directions, and whether the conversation settles when you look at a message or a calendar. Then talk about what each of you remembers rather than the word itself. “I remember you saying Saturday. You remember Sunday. Can we check the message and plan from there?” keeps both memories in the room.

Common mix-ups

Nobody sees things exactly as they are. Everyone sees through a lens, and knowing that is where fairness starts. Memory isn’t a recording, either, since it gets rebuilt each time, so two honest people can remember the same evening differently and both be telling the truth. Giving someone the benefit of the doubt doesn’t mean ignoring what happened. It means checking the story before you decide, and if something hurtful keeps happening, that matters. And a face or a tone is a clue to what someone meant. Faces and tones vary a lot between people and between cultures, so a face is a weaker clue than it feels.

If you want to help someone else with this

Start with how they feel, and make sense of the feeling first, and only then wonder aloud whether there might be another way to see it. Offer information instead of verdicts. “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.” Check your own guesses out loud (“I assumed he was annoyed, I’ll ask”), which shows how it’s done. Be careful with labels for children and colleagues, because they stick and then shape what everyone sees. And believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone that what happened to them didn’t happen.

Words to try “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”
