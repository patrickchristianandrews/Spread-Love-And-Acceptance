How we see each other: perception, bias, and the stories we tell

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why two people can watch the same moment and come away with two different stories, and how to see others, and yourself, a little more fairly.

In short

  • Seeing is a best guess. Your brain fills in a lot of what you think you see and hear.
  • We fill gaps with stories, and the story often feels like a fact.
  • We judge ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions, and that gap causes a lot of hurt.
  • Asking is kinder and more accurate than trying to read someone’s mind.

Why the tools work. Three tools in other chapters rest on what this one explains.

  • Who did what (the Preface to Part Two) works because everyone sees their own effort more clearly than other people’s, so two honest guesses of “I do about 60%” can add up to more than 100%. Writing it down replaces two guesses with one shared page.
  • Rewind the talk (Part Two, Chapter III) slows down the habit of judging ourselves by our reasons and other people by their actions.
  • The Chat analyzer follows this chapter’s last idea, that asking works better than guessing.

Wednesday, 12:15pm

You wave at a coworker across the canteen, and she looks straight through you and walks off. All afternoon you wonder what you did, until at four o’clock she messages to say sorry for not stopping, because she had just had a call about her dad. She never saw you wave.

How feelings work and Stress and the nervous system look inward. This chapter looks outward, at how we see other people, why misunderstandings are so common even between kind and clever people, and what helps.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think, and your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well and fast, but it means that what you see is partly what you expected to see, so two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

    For example, two parents watch their son in a football match. One sees him giving up, and the other sees him being careful with the ankle he twisted last week.

    Things to tell yourself “This is what I see, and there may be more to it.” “What else could this be?”

    Try this. Next time you’re sure what someone meant by a look, think of two other things it could have meant.

    Dig deeper: perception as a best guess

  2. You see what you’re looking for.

    Attention is like a torch in a dark room. You see what it lights up and can miss what it doesn’t, even when it is right in front of you. If you are looking for proof that someone doesn’t care, you will notice every missed call and none of the little kindnesses.

    A woman decides that her new manager doesn’t like her, and for a month she notices every short email he sends. She misses the fact that he gave her the best project on the team.

    Things to tell yourself “What might I be missing?” “If I were looking for the opposite, what would I see?”

    This week. For one week, write down one kind thing that a person you are struggling with does each day.

    Dig deeper: the torch of attention

  3. We fill the gaps with stories.

    When we don’t know why someone did something, the mind makes up a reason fast. A late reply becomes anger and a closed door becomes rejection, and the story arrives feeling like a fact. Memory works this way too, because each time we remember something we rebuild it a little, so two people can honestly remember the same evening differently.

    For example, a friend doesn’t reply for two days and you decide she is fed up with you. She was camping in Snowdonia with no signal.

    You could say “The story I’m telling myself is that you’re upset with me. Is that right?”

    One small step. When something someone did hurts, write down the story you are telling yourself, and then one other possible story.

    Dig deeper: stories and memory

  4. We judge ourselves by our reasons, and others by their actions.

    When you are late, you know why, whether it was the traffic, a phone call or a child who lost a shoe. When someone else is late, you only see the lateness, and it is easy to decide they are careless. We all tend to blame other people’s character for things that are really about their situation.

    A driver cuts in front of you and you call him an idiot under your breath. The next week, rushing to the hospital, you cut someone up yourself, and you know exactly why.

    Thoughts you could try “What might be going on for them that I can’t see?” “If I did this, what would my reason be?”

    Something to try. When someone annoys you today, think of one situation that would explain it without making them a bad person.

    Dig deeper: actions and reasons

  5. Quick shortcuts can lead us astray.

    The brain uses shortcuts to save time, and many are useful. Some lead to unfair judgements, such as a first impression that colours everything after it, a bad moment that outweighs ten good ones, or a quick guess about someone from their age, accent, looks or group. Everyone has these shortcuts, and the trouble starts when nobody notices them.

    For example, a housemate decides on the first day that the new tenant is messy because of one box left in the hall. Every bit of mess after that counts as proof, while her own goes unseen.

    Things to tell yourself “Am I looking for proof of what I already think?” “Would I judge this the same if someone I like did it?”

    Try this. Pick one person you’ve made your mind up about. Look for one thing that doesn’t fit your view of them.

    Dig deeper: shortcuts and bias

  6. Everyone feels like the one who sees clearly.

    Most of us feel that we see things as they really are and that anyone who disagrees must be missing something, biased or not thinking straight. The other person feels exactly the same about you, and each of you can see the other’s lens more easily than your own.

    Two adult siblings argue about how their parents raised them, and each insists they are just being realistic, which is true of what each of them lived through.

    Words you could use “I think we’re both seeing this from where we stand. Can you tell me how it looks from yours?”

    This week. In your next disagreement, explain the other person’s view back to them until they agree that you have got it, and then give yours.

    Dig deeper: why we each feel like the realist

  7. “Us” and “them” changes what we see.

    People split into groups quickly, even over tiny things. We tend to see our own group as varied and full of individuals and other groups as all the same, and we give our own side the benefit of the doubt. Spending real time with people from the other group, as equals, is one of the best ways to soften this.

    For example, at one depot the night shift calls the day shift lazy and the day shift calls the night shift sloppy. After a joint training day, people start using each other’s names.

    Things to tell yourself “Would I say this about one of ‘us’?” “Who in that group do I actually know?”

    One small step. Have one real conversation this month with someone from a group you tend to see as “other”, and ask about their day before you ask about their views.

    Dig deeper: us and them

  8. Ask, don’t guess.

    Imagining yourself in someone’s shoes is a kind start, though research suggests it doesn’t make us much more accurate about what they think, while asking does. A simple, curious question like “What was that like for you?” works better than the cleverest guess.

    A grandmother thinks her teenage grandson hates visiting because he is always on his phone. When she asks, she finds he is messaging a friend who is having a hard time, and that he likes being at her house because it is calm.

    Ways to say it “Can I check what you meant?” “What was going on for you?” “I might have this wrong. How did it look from your side?”

    Something to try. Once today, swap a guess for a question.

    Dig deeper: seeing more fairly

Thursday, 12:20pm

You see your coworker in the canteen again, and this time you go over and ask how her dad is. She tells you about the hospital and the waiting, and you end up sharing a table until the end of the break.

In everyday life

We tell stories about ourselves too, and “I always mess things up” is usually built from a few bright memories that leave a great deal out. After years together, partners stop checking and start assuming, and “I know what you meant” can do a lot of quiet damage in a long relationship. Children are often labelled early as the difficult one or the easy one, and the label becomes a lens that only sees what fits. With ADHD, attention is pulled by whatever is new or urgent and can lock on hard, so someone may truly not have seen or heard. It helps to get their attention first and to put requests in writing. Common mix-ups

  • “I just see things as they are.” Everyone sees through a lens, and knowing that is where fairness starts.
  • “My memory is a recording.” Memory is rebuilt each time, so two honest people can remember the same event differently.
  • “Giving the benefit of the doubt means ignoring what happened.” It means checking the story before deciding, and if something hurtful keeps happening, that matters.
  • “Their face or tone told me what they meant.” Faces and tones vary a lot between people, cultures and neurotypes, so treat them as clues.

If you want to help someone else with this

  • Start with how they feel. Make sense of the feeling first, and only then ask gently whether there is another way to see it.
  • Offer information. “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.”
  • Check your own guesses out loud, as in “I assumed he was annoyed. I’ll ask.”
  • Be careful with labels. Labels for children, coworkers or family members stick, and then shape what everyone sees.
  • Believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone their experience didn’t happen.

Words to try “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”

When to get more help

Seeing other people’s side is a strength. It should never mean doubting yourself when you know something is wrong. If someone often tells you that what you saw, heard or remember didn’t happen, that you’re “too sensitive” or “imagining it,” and you find yourself unsure of your own mind, take that seriously. It can be part of controlling behaviour. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? can help, privately.

It may also help to talk to a counsellor or other trained professional if you notice you often feel others are against you, find it hard to trust anyone, or keep getting stuck in the same painful stories about yourself. Those patterns are common, understandable and often change with support.

This chapter is general education, not diagnosis or therapy.
