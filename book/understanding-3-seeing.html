How we see each other: perception, bias, and the stories we tell

Simple versionRead the full chapter

Why two people can watch the same moment and come away with two different stories, and how to see others, and yourself, a little more fairly.

In short

  • Seeing is a best guess. Your brain fills in a lot of what you think you see and hear.
  • We fill gaps with stories, and the story often feels like a fact.
  • We judge ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions, and that gap causes a lot of hurt.
  • Asking is kinder and more accurate than trying to read someone’s mind.

Wednesday, 12:15pm

You wave at a colleague across the canteen, tray in one hand. She looks straight through you and walks off. All afternoon you wonder what you did, running back through Monday’s meeting, until at four o’clock she messages to say sorry for not stopping; she’d just had a call about her dad. She never saw you wave.

How feelings work and Stress and the nervous system look inward. This chapter looks outward, at how we see other people, why misunderstandings are so common even between kind and clever people, and what helps. Before I built any of this I spent my career as a government auditor, walking into situations where nobody could tell me straight what had happened. Almost everyone, it turned out, was doing their best with the piece of the picture they could actually see. That’s more or less this chapter.

Some of the tools elsewhere depend on it. Who did what (the Preface to Part Two) exists because everyone sees their own effort more clearly than other people’s, so two honest guesses of “I do about 60%” can add up to well over 100%, and one shared page settles it. Rewind the talk (Part Two, Chapter III) slows down the habit of judging ourselves by our reasons and others by their actions. And the Chat analyzer follows this chapter’s last idea, that asking beats guessing.

  1. Seeing is a best guess.

    Your eyes and ears take in far less than you think, and your brain fills in the rest from what it expects. Most of the time this works well, and fast. It also means that what you see is partly what you expected to see, so two people can watch the same moment and honestly see different things.

    Two parents watch their son in an under-12s match on a freezing Sunday morning. One sees him giving up. The other sees him being careful with the ankle he went over on last week. Same boy, same touchline, same flask of tea. Next time you’re sure what someone meant by a look, think of two other things it could have meant.

    Things to tell yourself “This is what I see, and there may be more to it.” “What else could this be?”

    Dig deeper: perception as a best guess

  2. You see what you’re looking for.

    Attention is like a torch in a dark room. You see what it lights up and can miss what it doesn’t, even when it is right in front of you. If you are looking for proof that someone doesn’t care, you will notice every missed call and none of the little kindnesses.

    A woman decides in her first week that her new manager doesn’t like her. For a month she notices every short email he sends (there are a lot; he writes short emails to everyone). She somehow misses the fact that he gave her the best project on the team. If there’s someone you’re struggling with, write down one kind thing they do each day for a week. It’s an odd exercise, and it tends to change what the torch finds.

    Things to tell yourself “What might I be missing?” “If I were looking for the opposite, what would I see?”

    Dig deeper: the torch of attention

  3. We fill the gaps with stories.

    When we don’t know why someone did something, the mind makes up a reason fast. A late reply becomes anger and a closed door becomes rejection, and the story arrives feeling like a fact. Memory works this way too, because each time we remember something we rebuild it a little, so two people can honestly remember the same evening differently.

    A friend doesn’t reply for two days, and by the Sunday evening you’ve decided she’s fed up with you and drafted, then deleted, a message saying so. She was camping in Snowdonia with no signal, and comes back with photos of a sheep.

    You could say “The story I’m telling myself is that you’re upset with me. Is that right?”

    When something someone did hurts, write down the story you’re telling yourself, then one other story that would also fit.

    Dig deeper: stories and memory

  4. We judge ourselves by our reasons, and others by their actions.

    When you are late, you know why, whether it was the traffic, a phone call or a child who lost a shoe. When someone else is late, you only see the lateness, and it is easy to decide they are careless. We all tend to blame other people’s character for things that are really about their situation.

    A driver cuts in front of you on the ring road and you call him an idiot, not quite under your breath. The next week, rushing to the hospital because your mum has had a fall, you cut someone up yourself, and you know exactly why, and you’d like them to know it too.

    Thoughts you could try “What might be going on for them that I can’t see?” “If I did this, what would my reason be?”

  5. Quick shortcuts can lead us astray.

    The brain uses shortcuts to save time, and many are useful. Some lead to unfair judgements, such as a first impression that colours everything after it, a bad moment that outweighs ten good ones, or a quick guess about someone from their age, accent, looks or group. Everyone has these shortcuts, and the trouble starts when nobody notices them.

    A housemate decides on day one that the new tenant is messy, because of one box left in the hall. Every bit of mess after that counts as proof. Her own mug collection on the landing goes unseen. Pick one person you’ve made your mind up about and look for one thing that doesn’t fit.

    Things to tell yourself “Am I looking for proof of what I already think?” “Would I judge this the same if someone I like did it?”

    Dig deeper: shortcuts and bias

  6. Everyone feels like the one who sees clearly.

    Most of us feel that we see things as they really are and that anyone who disagrees must be missing something, or not thinking straight. The other person feels exactly the same about you, and each of you can see the other’s lens more easily than your own.

    Two adult siblings argue about how their parents raised them, and each insists they are just being realistic, which is true of what each of them lived through.

    Words you could use “I think we’re both seeing this from where we stand. Can you tell me how it looks from yours?”

    In your next disagreement, explain the other person’s view back to them until they agree you’ve got it, and only then give yours. You’ll find it slows everything down, which is rather the point.

    Dig deeper: why we each feel like the realist

  7. “Us” and “them” changes what we see.

    People split into groups quickly, even over tiny things. We tend to see our own group as varied and full of individuals and other groups as all the same, and we give our own side the benefit of the doubt. Spending real time with people from the other group, as equals, is one of the best ways to soften this.

    At one depot the night shift calls the day shift lazy and the day shift calls the night shift sloppy, and has done for as long as anyone can remember. After a joint training day with a bad buffet and a fire drill, people start using each other’s names. It doesn’t fix everything. It does stop the notes on the kettle.

    Things to tell yourself “Would I say this about one of ‘us’?” “Who in that group do I actually know?”

    One small step. Have one real conversation this month with someone from a group you tend to see as “other”, and ask about their day before you ask about their views.

    Dig deeper: us and them

  8. Ask, don’t guess.

    Imagining yourself in someone’s shoes is a kind start, though research suggests it doesn’t make us much more accurate about what they think, while asking does. A simple, curious question like “What was that like for you?” works better than the cleverest guess.

    A grandmother thinks her teenage grandson hates visiting, because he’s always on his phone at her kitchen table. One Sunday she asks. He’s been messaging a friend who is having a hard time, and he likes being at her house because it’s calm, and because of her apple crumble, though he wouldn’t have said so if she hadn’t asked.

    Ways to say it “Can I check what you meant?” “What was going on for you?” “I might have this wrong. How did it look from your side?”

In everyday life

We tell stories about ourselves too, and “I always mess things up” is usually built from a few bright memories that leave a great deal out. After years together, partners tend to stop checking and start assuming. And “I know what you meant” can do a lot of quiet damage in a long relationship. Children are often labelled early as the difficult one or the easy one, and the label becomes a lens that only sees what fits. With ADHD, attention is pulled by whatever is new or urgent and can lock on hard, so someone may truly not have seen or heard. It helps to get their attention first and to put requests in writing. Common mix-ups

Nobody just sees things as they are. Everyone sees through a lens, and knowing that is where fairness starts. Memory isn’t a recording either; it’s rebuilt each time, so two honest people can remember the same evening differently and both be telling the truth. Giving someone the benefit of the doubt doesn’t mean ignoring what happened. It means checking the story before you decide, and if something hurtful keeps happening, that matters. And a face or a tone is a clue to what someone meant. Faces and tones vary a lot between people and between cultures, so a face is a weaker clue than it feels.

If you want to help someone else with this

Start with how they feel. Make sense of the feeling first, and only then ask whether there might be another way to see it. Offer information rather than verdicts: “I know she had a hard week” helps more than “You’re being unfair to her.” Check your own guesses out loud (“I assumed he was annoyed. I’ll ask”), which shows how it’s done. Be careful with labels for children and colleagues, because they stick and then shape what everyone sees. And believe people about their own experience. Seeing other angles never means telling someone that what happened to them didn’t happen.

Words to try “That sounds really hurtful. Do you know what was going on for them?” “It might be worth asking. Want to plan what to say?”

When to get more help

Seeing other people’s side is a strength. It should never mean doubting yourself when you know something is wrong. If someone often tells you that what you saw, heard or remember didn’t happen, that you’re “too sensitive” or “imagining it,” and you find yourself unsure of your own mind, take that seriously. It can be part of controlling behaviour. Signs to look out for and Not safe at home? can help, privately.

It may also help to talk to a counsellor or other trained professional if you notice you often feel others are against you, find it hard to trust anyone, or keep getting stuck in the same painful stories about yourself. Those patterns are common and understandable, and they often change with support.

This chapter is general education, not diagnosis or therapy.
