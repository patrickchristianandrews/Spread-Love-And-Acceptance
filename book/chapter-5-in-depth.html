The Deficit Audit

The fourth Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth about Tuesday. Or you could notice that Tuesday has now happened four times, and ask what it is about Tuesdays.

Like the tools in earlier chapters, the look-back in this chapter checks a shared setup over time. It never judges either person’s character, and it never settles “who owes what” for hours already passed.

The formal name is the Deficit Audit. In accounting, a deficit is a shortfall, and an audit is a careful look back through the books. Here, the “deficit” is a job that keeps falling short, and the “audit” is a friendly monthly look at your notes to find it.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load. (Borrowed from systems thinking, behavioral economics and ledger accounting.)

  • In you: Look at your own routines as a setup you can adjust. Spot the easy defaults, like “I’ll just do it,” that let a gap quietly reopen.
  • Between you and others: Look at the month together, give repeating gaps a clear owner, and keep count so jobs don’t drift back to whoever notices first.

Why a monthly look, on top of the weekly ones

By this point in the book, two rhythms are already running. There’s the 90-second daily check-in (WP-13). And there’s the weekly closing, a short end-of-week catch-up where you tally the week in the Lemonade Stand and take a quick look at your job list (WP-03).

Both are useful because they are small and frequent. But small, frequent checks have a blind spot. A job that slips for one week looks like a fluke. The same job slipping four weeks in a row is a pattern, and patterns are hard to see week by week. They only show up when you step back and look at a whole month.

The monthly look-back is that step back. Instead of asking “How did this week go?”, it asks: which gaps kept reopening, however many times we closed them?

Getting ready

  • Your four weekly WP-01 logs from this month.
  • Your current WP-03 job list.
  • The WP-04 sheet, on paper or on screen.
  • Twenty minutes, at a time you’ve both agreed, when neither of you is rushed, hungry or worn out.

If one of your batteries is high that day (over 0.6 on WP-02), move the look-back. It can wait a day or two.

Step one: gather the month’s notes

Go through the four weekly logs. Write down every job that slipped in any week. A job slipped if it was noticed but not done, or only got done after someone raised it more than once. Tick each week it happened.

One tick is normal life. Two ticks are worth a look. Three or four ticks in one month is a real pattern, not a fluke.

What a gap looks like at this scale

A single missed job is an event. A deficit is a job that keeps producing the same event, month after month, without ever landing on a steady owner. It’s the difference between “the trash didn’t go out on Tuesday” and “the trash have quietly become nobody’s job for three months.” The first is ordinary noise. The second is a gap in the job list that the weekly check-ins keep patching instead of fixing.

This matters because gaps like these are where the unbilled debt from the Preface grows fastest. It doesn’t come from any single missed job. It comes from the same job being argued over from scratch every time it comes back, because it never got a name that stuck.

Step two: sort each repeat

For every job with two or more ticks, decide which of three kinds of gap it is:

Kind of gapWhat it meansWhat to do
Structural gapThere’s no clear owner, or the named owner isn’t the person actually doing it.Update the WP-03 job list in this session, and agree on the change together.
Capacity issueThere’s a named owner, but they’ve been unable to keep up with it.Book an honest, kind conversation about handing it over or changing it. Don’t just quietly promise to try harder.
One-offIt slipped more than once, but for a clear short-term reason, such as illness, travel or a busy month at work.Note it and move on. Don’t make a lasting change because of a short-term cause.

For a capacity conversation, the kind scripts in WP-01 for saying “not right now,” and the fact-feeling-ask shape in WP-09, both help.

A worked example month

Here’s what Sam and Alex’s WP-04 sheet looks like at the end of a month:

School forms signed✓✓✓3Capacity issue
Dishes before bed✓✓2One-off
Plants watered✓1(normal life)
  • The trash are Alex’s on paper, but Sam has done them every week because Alex works late on Tuesdays. The named owner isn’t the person doing it, so it’s a structural gap. They move the trash to Sam and give Alex the Saturday recycling. They update WP-03 on the spot.
  • The school forms have a clear owner, Sam, who is swamped this term. That’s a capacity issue. They book a proper talk for Thursday evening instead of solving it now.
  • The dishes slipped during the two weeks Alex was traveling. That’s a one-off. They note it and change nothing.

Result: one structural gap, one capacity issue, one one-off. They write those three numbers down. The whole thing takes eighteen minutes.

Step three: close the month and keep count

  1. Update WP-03 for every structural gap.
  2. Book the conversation for every capacity issue. The look-back finds the issue. It doesn’t replace the conversation.
  3. Write down how many structural gaps, capacity issues and one-offs you found.

A falling number of structural gaps, month after month, is the clearest sign the whole approach is working. A rising number usually means something bigger has changed, such as a new job, a new baby or a move, and that change may need its own conversation.

The look-back also feeds back into Chapter II. Each structural gap you fix gives a job a clear owner, so next month’s ownership score should rise. REPORT-01 can keep the monthly counts, including how many gaps came back after being fixed the month before. That repeat count is the one to watch most closely.

How to talk about a pattern

When a gap needs a real conversation, use Check-ins for the order: one topic, a good moment, and hearing each other back before answering. Try the Signal Translator on the sentence that names the pattern.

How you say it matters. A pattern said as a verdict (“You never take out the trash”) will get argued with. A pattern said as a repeated fact (“The trash has slipped four weeks running. What’s getting in the way on Tuesdays?”) can get an owner. Keep the month from turning into overload, blame or criticism of the person. The ground rules are how the conversation stays a repair.

A good habit: open every look-back by each naming one thing that went well this month. Turning Toward has more ideas for small, specific thank-yous.

What the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

The monthly look-back, set out as a working sheet in WP-04, does three things:

  1. It finds which jobs are real repeating gaps, not one-off misses.
  2. It decides whether each gap needs a new owner or a conversation about capacity.
  3. It closes the month cleanly, so next month starts from a fixed setup instead of the same unresolved argument.

It is not a monthly performance review of either person. And it never goes back to add up hours that were “owed.” Those hours are gone. Billing for them afterward doesn’t fix anything; it just turns a look-back into a grievance. The only question it asks about the past month is where the setup failed to hold. So the setup, not anyone’s effort or affection, is what changes for next month.

It also doesn’t replace the weekly closing. The monthly look-back only works because the weekly notes keep feeding it real information. Skip the weekly checks, and the monthly one becomes guesswork.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, the look-back turns into a list of complaints. Every tick becomes “you, again.” Old hours get added up. Someone leaves feeling judged, and next month both people quietly find a reason to skip it.

When it goes right, it’s short, a little dull and oddly calming. A couple of jobs get new owners. One talk gets booked. The number of gaps is written down. Next month it’s smaller, and you both notice the difference at home.

Questions people often ask

We missed a few weekly logs. Can we still do the look-back?

Yes. Use what you have, and be honest about the gaps in your notes. Just avoid making big changes based on thin information.

What if we disagree about which kind of gap something is?

Start with the simplest question: does the job have a named owner, and is that person the one actually doing it? If you still disagree, call it a structural gap and look at the job list together. Updating the list is low-risk and easy to undo.

How long should it take?

About twenty minutes. If it runs much longer, you’re probably solving capacity issues in the meeting. Book those as separate talks.

Can someone help us prepare?

Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you word the sentence that names a pattern.

Limits and cautions

  • The look-back finds patterns in jobs. It can’t tell you why someone feels the way they do. That still needs a conversation.
  • Don’t use it in a week when either of you is very stretched. A calm look-back a few days late beats a tense one on time.
  • Agreements made for an old life often need changing. The Ship of Theseus is a good story to keep in mind: a ship rebuilt plank by plank still needs its plans updated.

Closing the books

This is the last chapter, but the program keeps going round. Each month, you look back, change one or two things, and start again. You see the work, stay in tune, check the split can last, know what you’re carrying, agree what fair means, and look back again. The aim was never a perfect ledger. It’s a shared life where nobody is quietly carrying more than the other can see.
