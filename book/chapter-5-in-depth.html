The Deficit Audit

The third Tuesday in a row

The trash again. You could have the same ten-minute back-and-forth about Tuesday. Or you could notice that Tuesday has now happened three times, and ask what it is about Tuesdays.

Like the tools in earlier chapters, the look-back in this chapter checks a shared setup over time. It never judges either person’s character, and it never settles “who owes what” for hours already passed.

The formal name is the Deficit Audit. In accounting, a deficit is a shortfall. Here, the “deficit” is a job that keeps falling short, and the “audit” is a careful, friendly look back at your month’s notes, more like a self-check than an inspection.

Where this fits: the Five Pillars

The whole program rests on five pillars. Each one starts inside you and then shows up between you and other people. This chapter mostly teaches Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person and Pillar V, Notice the quiet incentives, with help from Pillar I, See the whole load. (Borrowed from systems thinking, behavioral economics and ledger accounting.)

  • In you: Look at your own routines as a setup you can adjust. Spot the easy defaults, like “I’ll just do it,” that let a gap quietly reopen.
  • Between you and others: Look at the month together, give repeating gaps a clear owner, and keep count so jobs don’t drift back to whoever notices first.

Why a monthly look, on top of the weekly ones

By this point in the book, two rhythms are already running. There’s the 90-second daily check-in (WP-13). And there’s the weekly catch-up, a short end-of-week talk where you tally the week in the Lemonade Stand (a free page where each person lists their hours) and take a quick look at your job list (WP-03, One owner per job).

Both are useful because they are small and frequent. But small, frequent checks have a blind spot. A job that slips for one week looks like bad luck. The same job slipping week after week is a pattern, and patterns are easy to miss week by week. They show up best when you step back and look at a whole month.

The monthly look-back is that step back. Instead of asking “How did this week go?”, it asks: which gaps kept reopening, however many times we closed them?

Getting ready

  • Your four weekly notes from this month, in WP-01 (the “who did what” log). A few lines a week is all it needs.
  • Your current WP-03 job list.
  • The WP-04 sheet (“What keeps coming back?”), on paper or on screen.
  • Twenty minutes, at a time you’ve both agreed on, when neither of you is rushed, hungry or worn out.

If either of your stress tanks is high that day (0.60 or more on the Battery & Stress Meter (WP-02), which measures how full your stress tank is), move the look-back. It can wait a day or two.

Step one: gather the month’s notes

Go through the four weekly logs. Write down every job that slipped in any week. A job slipped if it was noticed but not done, if it only got done after someone raised it more than once, or if its owner couldn’t do it and someone else quietly covered. Mark an ✗ for each week it happened.

In accounting terms, you are reconciling the plan (WP-03, who should do each job) against the log (WP-01, who did). Every difference is an item to explain.

One ✗ is normal life. Two are worth a look. Three or four in one month is a real pattern, not just bad luck.

What a gap looks like at this scale

A single missed job is an event. A deficit is a job that keeps producing the same event, month after month, without ever landing on a steady owner. It’s the difference between “the trash didn’t go out on Tuesday” and “the trash has quietly become nobody’s job for three months.” The first is ordinary noise. The second is a gap in the job list that the weekly check-ins keep patching instead of fixing.

This matters because gaps like these are where the unbilled debt from the Preface grows fastest. It doesn’t come from any single missed job. It comes from the same job being argued over from scratch every time it comes back, because it never got a name that stuck.

Step two: sort each repeat

For every job with two or more ✗, decide which of three kinds of gap it is. (As in Chapter II, the owner is the person who does the job, start to finish. The second name on the list is only a backstop who notices if it slipped and gently checks in.)

Kind of gapWhat it meansWhat to do
Structural gapThere’s no clear owner, or the named owner isn’t the person actually doing it.Update the WP-03 job list in this session, and agree on the change together.
Capacity issueThere’s a named owner, but they’ve been unable to keep up with it.Set a time for an honest, kind conversation about handing it over or changing it. Don’t just quietly promise to try harder.
One-offIt slipped more than once, but for a clear short-term reason, such as illness, travel or a busy month at work.Note it and move on. Don’t make a lasting change because of a short-term cause.

For a capacity conversation, two workpapers help: WP-01, which besides the log has kind scripts for saying “not right now,” and the fact-feeling-ask shape in WP-09, Say it so it lands.

A worked example month

Here’s what Sam and Alex’s WP-04 sheet looks like at the end of a month:

JobWk 1Wk 2Wk 3Wk 4Total ✗Kind
Trash out (Tuesday night)✗✗✗3Structural gap
School forms signed✗✗✗3Capacity issue
Dishes before bed✗✗2One-off
Plants watered✗1(normal life)

✗ = it slipped that week. A blank means the job went fine.

  • The trash is Alex’s on paper. But Alex works late on Tuesdays, so for three weeks Sam quietly took it out. At the weekly catch-up in Chapter IV, they agreed to try a swap for the rest of the month, and in week 4 Sam took it out with no trouble. The named owner wasn’t the person doing it, so it’s a structural gap. Now they make the trial swap official: Sam owns the trash, and Alex takes the recycling and the weekly grocery order, so the hours stay even. They update WP-03 on the spot.
  • The school forms have a clear owner, Sam, who is swamped this term. That’s a capacity issue. They set a time for a proper talk on Thursday evening instead of solving it now.
  • The dishes slipped during the two weeks Alex was traveling. That’s a one-off. They note it and change nothing.

Result: one structural gap, one capacity issue, one one-off. They write those three numbers down. The whole thing takes eighteen minutes.

Step three: close the month and keep count

  1. Update WP-03 for every structural gap.
  2. Set a time for the conversation for every capacity issue. The look-back finds the issue. It doesn’t replace the conversation.
  3. Write down how many structural gaps, capacity issues and one-offs you found.

A falling number of structural gaps is a good sign, over several months; one month can be a normal swing. A number that keeps rising usually means something bigger has changed, such as a new job, a new baby or a move, and that change may need its own conversation.

The look-back also feeds back into Chapter II. If a gap was a blank row, filling it in raises next month’s ownership score. If the names were filled in but wrong, like the trash, the score holds steady, but now it’s true: the list names who really does each job. Only the look-back catches that kind of gap. REPORT-01 (your progress, week by week) can keep the monthly counts, including how many gaps came back after being fixed the month before. That repeat count is the one to watch most closely.

The quiet months

The look-back is built to find gaps, so it is tempting to skip it when there aren’t any. That’s the month it matters in a different way. Where audits are done well, a control that has always worked is still tested again on a schedule, because working so far is not the same as working now. People are no different. A month with no ✗ can mean the setup is solid. It can also mean nobody has looked closely, or that one person is quietly covering so well that the page looks clean.

On a quiet month, keep the same twenty minutes and add three questions:

  1. What’s going well that we haven’t said out loud?
  2. Is anything small that we’ve stopped mentioning because it didn’t seem worth it?
  3. Does the job list still match life as it is now?

The first question gives thanks a place. The second catches what slow drift hides. The third is the review date for the whole setup. This is the same re-check that the Complacency guide sets out for every kind of relationship and for yourself, and a quiet month is a good time to practice it.

How to talk about a pattern

When a gap needs a real conversation, use Check-ins for the order: one topic, a good moment, and hearing each other back before answering. Try the Signal Translator, which shows how a sentence may land, on the sentence that names the pattern.

How you say it matters. A pattern said as a verdict (“You never take out the trash”) will get argued with. A pattern said as a repeated fact (“The trash has slipped three weeks in a row. What’s getting in the way on Tuesdays?”) can get an owner. Keep the month from turning into overload, blame or criticism of the person. The ground rules are how the conversation stays a repair.

Give context on both sides before you decide anything. A gap that looks like neglect from one side may be a heavy month from the other, and a job that “always gets done” may be getting done at a cost nobody has said out loud. Each person says briefly what the month was like for them, and the other repeats back what they heard. Only then do you decide who owns what. The Complacency guide has words for raising something someone has stopped noticing.

If the talk starts going badly, either of you can call a pause, and the person who paused names a time to come back.

A good habit: open every look-back by each naming one thing that went well this month. Turning Toward has more ideas for small, specific thank-yous.

What the look-back is for, and what it isn’t

The monthly look-back, set out as a working sheet in WP-04, does three things:

  1. It finds which jobs are real repeating gaps, not one-off misses.
  2. It decides whether each gap needs a new owner or a conversation about capacity.
  3. It closes the month cleanly, so next month starts from a fixed setup instead of the same unresolved argument.

It is not a monthly performance review of either person. And it never goes back to add up hours that were “owed.” Past hours can’t be handed back, and billing for them doesn’t fix anything; it just turns a look-back into a grievance. That doesn’t mean pretending they didn’t happen. You can thank someone for what they carried before (“I see how much you’ve been carrying”); you just don’t bill for the past. The only question the look-back asks about the past month is where the setup failed to hold. So the setup, not anyone’s effort or affection, is what changes for next month.

It also doesn’t replace the weekly catch-up. The monthly look-back only works because the weekly notes keep feeding it real information. Skip the weekly checks, and the monthly one becomes guesswork.

Your part, and only your part

In the look-back you can only fix the gaps you own, and offer help with the rest. You can’t make someone keep a rhythm. Keep the rhythms you can keep, notice which ones are kept in return, and let your effort follow: more where it’s shared, less where it isn’t, so the rhythm doesn’t run on one person’s energy.

The idea runs through the whole book: you can only do what’s in your control, and you protect your own energy by matching energy with energy, giving in proportion to what comes back, with room for seasons when someone can’t. Part One covers it in full: your circle of control, and matching energy.

When it goes wrong, and when it goes right

When it goes wrong, the look-back turns into a list of complaints. Every ✗ becomes “you, again.” Old hours get added up. Someone leaves feeling judged, and next month both people quietly find a reason to skip it.

When it goes right, it’s short, a little dull and oddly calming. A couple of jobs get new owners. One talk gets scheduled. The number of gaps is written down. Over the next few months it gets smaller, and you both notice the difference at home.

Questions people often ask

We missed a few weekly logs. Can we still do the look-back?

Yes. Use what you have, and be honest about the gaps in your notes. Just avoid making big changes based on thin information.

What if we disagree about which kind of gap something is?

Start with the simplest question: does the job have a named owner, and is that person the one actually doing it? If you still disagree, call it a structural gap and look at the job list together. Updating the list is low-risk and easy to undo.

How long should it take?

About twenty minutes. If it runs much longer, you’re probably solving capacity issues in the meeting. Schedule those as separate talks.

Can someone help us prepare?

Professor Puddles, the site’s friendly chat host, can help you word the sentence that names a pattern.

Limits and cautions

  • The look-back finds patterns in jobs. It can’t tell you why someone feels the way they do. That still needs a conversation.
  • Don’t use it in a week when either of you is very stretched. A calm look-back a few days late beats a tense one on time.
  • Agreements made for an old life often need changing. The Ship of Theseus is a good story to keep in mind: a ship rebuilt plank by plank is still the same ship, and a home whose agreements get rebuilt is still your home, as long as you keep the plans up to date.
  • These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor, together or on your own.

Closing the books

This is the last chapter, but the program keeps going around. Each month, you look back, change one or two things, and start again. You see the work, stay in tune, check the split can last, know what you’re carrying, agree what fair means, and look back again. The aim was never a perfect ledger. It’s a shared life where nobody is quietly carrying more than the other can see.
