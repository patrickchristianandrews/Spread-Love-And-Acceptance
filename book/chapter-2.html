Part Two: Between us · Chapter II

Is the split working? A fair look at chores at home

Paperback textRead the expanded version

One gentle question: can the way you share the work keep going, or is one of you quietly running on empty?

In the paperbacks: Book 4, Share the Load, Chapter 2.

At a glance

  • Look at the month, not last night, using what you both wrote down.
  • Three things go in, which are how the hours split, whether each job has a name, and how full each person’s stress tank is.
  • One number comes out, from 0 to 1. Start with whichever part pulled it down most.
  • It judges the setup, never a person, and it is where a talk starts.

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about, like the trash schedule and the fish food, the reply to the school, the dentist reminder, and the bit at the bottom of the shopping list that just says “bulbs?”

When the other one says it feels pretty even, they mean it. From inside their own week, it does.

Eleven small jobs a day, seen quickly. On the surface, a split that feels even to one person. Underneath, jobs with no owner. Underneath that, stress tanks at very different levels. The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives. Then the chapter takes the three parts of the score in turn.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    Ask whether the split is working and the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently, which is usually last night’s pans. This chapter asks a calmer question, using what you both wrote down. Ask it again every few months, even when things seem fine, because a split that worked once can quietly go stale.

    Two roommates had one truly terrible week of dishes in May, the kind where you wash a fork in order to eat with it. They were all set for a big fight. Then they looked at the whole month, written down, and it was three ordinary weeks and one exam week. The fight didn’t happen, which was almost a disappointment after all that build-up. They bought paper plates for exam week and left it at that.

  2. Three things go in.

    The first is how evenly the hours are shared. The second is whether every regular job has a clear owner, from the job list in One owner per job. The third is how stressed each of you already is from everything else going on. A workpaper is one of the site’s free fill-in worksheets. You don’t need any of them to start, since rough notes on the back of an envelope do fine.

    You could say “Let’s each bring our own numbers, so neither of us has to guess.”

    Dig deeper: where each number comes from

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    From your week-long logs, work out each person’s share of the hours. Against an even split, an even share scores 1 and one person doing everything scores 0, whichever person it is. If you’ve agreed an uneven split that suits you, perhaps because one of you works longer hours outside the home, compare against that split instead. With three or more of you, compare each person’s share with an even share (100 divided by the number of people) or with the split you agreed. Three partners who log 30 hours of home jobs between them, at 15, 9 and 6, would each have 10 as an even share. The “Who does what?” page, where each person lists their hours, does the arithmetic for you.

    Everyone’s share comes to more than 100% In a classic set of experiments, spouses asked for their own share of joint jobs gave answers that, added together, usually came to more than 100%. Nobody was lying. We simply remember our own contributions better, which is the best argument I know for writing the week down instead of guessing.

    Ross and Sicoly (1979) · Strong: a classic, widely replicated finding

    Log one week each, and put in the small jobs that take under ten minutes. Those are the ones that go missing. They are often the ones that start the fights, too.

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Each job gets two names. The owner does the job from start to finish. The backstop notices if it slipped and checks in gently, which is very different from supervising. It can be the same person, though a second name gives the job a gentle check. Count the jobs that have an owner and divide by all the jobs on the list, and leave the unowned jobs on the list, because they matter most. A decision you make together, such as which school, can be marked “Both (decide together)” and counts as owned. A job that needs doing gets one name.

    Most of what goes wrong in shared work isn’t incompetence. It’s unclear ownership: work that belongs to everyone belongs to no one, and it quietly lands on whoever notices first.

    Two sisters care for their dad. Nobody owned his prescription refills. For two weeks each of them thought the other had ordered it, and each, it turned out, had thought about calling to check and hadn’t, so as not to seem pushy. He ran out on a Sunday. The late-night pharmacy was forty minutes away.

  5. Stress: how full each stress tank is.

    Each of you rates how full your own stress tank is with How much are you carrying?, where a higher score means more stress. Under 0.30 is light, 0.30 up to 0.60 is medium, and 0.60 or more is high. The score uses the average of your two scores, but if either of you is at 0.60 or more, talk about that first, even when the overall result looks fine. A setup that only works when you’re both well rested can’t last, so more stress lowers the result.

    How loaded it feels The Perceived Stress Scale, one of the most used stress questionnaires, asks how unpredictable, uncontrollable and overloaded life has felt lately, and its scores track symptoms of depression and illness. That’s close to what the stress tank asks: not how much happened, but how loaded it feels.

    Cohen, Kamarck and Mermelstein (1983) · Strong: one of the most widely used stress questionnaires

    Doreen is raising her two grandchildren and scores 0.70. Her son, who helps on weekends, scores 0.35. The average looks fine, but Doreen’s 0.70 is the headline, whatever the average says, and her son, looking at the two numbers side by side, went quiet for a long time. Each of you scores your own tank on the same day, before you look at the other’s.

    Dig deeper: the stress score

  6. One number comes out, from 0 to 1.

    A result of 0.70 or above reads as working well, meaning the setup is holding. From 0.40 up to 0.70 it reads as needs a look, because something is drifting, often who owns what, and below 0.40 it reads as needs a rethink, together. The result is called the setup score. It is a simple weighted sum, in which the hours count most, then the owners, then stress, and although it looks like a probability it isn’t one.

    Two friends who run a café together scored 0.55. So nothing was broken, just something drifting, and it took them about four minutes to find it, which was the Monday ordering. Each of them had assumed for months that the other did it, and it had been getting done, more or less, by whoever panicked first on Tuesday morning. Write the date next to your number, so you can compare next month.

    Dig deeper: the formula and what each range means

  7. Start with the biggest shortfall.

    The single number matters less than which of the three parts pulled it down most. The shortfall is how far one part fell below the most it could add, and in the middle band it’s often ownership, which is why the calculator suggests One owner per job first. A separated couple scored 0.62 and found their biggest gap was that nobody owned the school forms. That was the only thing they changed that month, and both of them were quietly relieved it wasn’t something bigger.

    Ways to say it “Which part pulled the number down most? Let’s start there.”

    Dig deeper: reading your result

  8. It judges the setup, never a person.

    When a measure becomes a target Goodhart’s law, from an economist writing about monetary policy, says a number tends to stop telling you anything once people are pushed to hit it. That’s the reason this score is for looking at together and never for winning.

    Goodhart (1975) · Moderate: an economist’s observation, later generalized and widely seen in practice

    The number isn’t a verdict on who’s better or whether the relationship works. It’s where a conversation starts, never how one ends.

    “So I’m the problem,” said Lee, looking at 0.35.

    “No,” said his partner. “The bathroom hasn’t got an owner and you work nights.”

    They started there. Before you begin, agree that either of you can say “pause” and come back later.

    Dig deeper: what the number is not

Your part, and only your part. The score describes the setup you share, but your levers are your own: your jobs, your hours and what you agree to take on. You can propose a fix; you can’t make the other person accept it. If the balance stays one-sided, step your load back toward your share on purpose, and say so kindly. Matching effort with effort protects your stress tank, and your tank is part of the score too. More in Your wiring, weather and words

Sunday evening

Two week-long logs. Two cups of tea. One list has 31 rows and the other has 18, and for a moment neither of you says anything and the fridge hums.

“I honestly didn’t know about half of these,” one of you says.

“Which half?”

Words this chapter uses

A log is a simple list of the jobs you did this week and roughly how long each took. The owner does a job from start to finish, including remembering it, and the backstop notices if a job slipped and checks in gently. Your stress tank is how much stress you’re already carrying from the rest of life. The setup is the way you share the work right now, which can always be changed, and the setup score is the one number from 0 to 1 that sums up the three readings. Invisible jobs count too. Planning meals, remembering birthdays, booking the vet and noticing that the soap is running low all belong in your log.

On your own: is your own load working?

You can ask the same question of your own week. Are you spending more energy than comes back? Add up what you carry, jobs and worries both, and set it beside what refills you. If it runs in the red, change your side, by dropping, delaying, asking or saying no kindly. A limit describes your supply. It says nothing about your worth. After her breakup, Leanne had the children every other week and filled the quiet weeks with favors for friends, including one memorable assemble-it-yourself dresser that took nine hours and a hospital visit (her thumb). Writing it down showed her she never rested (more in On my own).

Words to try “I’d love to help, and I can’t this week. Can I let you know next week?”

One setup, looked at twice

Three people share an apartment above a bakery, and everyone agrees that cleaning matters while nobody owns the bathroom. Their first check scores 0.67, and the biggest shortfall is ownership: eight of the twelve jobs have a name, and the bathroom, the trash, the recycling and the hallway don’t. They give each of those four jobs an owner and a backstop. A month later, with the hours and their stress tanks about the same as before, the score is 0.79. Ownership went from 0.67 to 1.00, and that part counts for 0.35 of the score, so it adds 0.12. The bathroom is better. The recycling is still a sore point, because the one who owns it works late on collection night, and that’s the job they’re arguing about next. Shift work, illness and caring can all make hours look even while sleep or energy is not, and an uneven split agreed on purpose can be the fair one (see Different hours and When one of you is sick or disabled).

Two ways to read this

Redoing a job, two ways

Some people care most that a job got done. Others care how it was done, because things have a system and a cup in the “wrong” place means hunting for it later. If you care that it’s done, a quiet reload of the dishwasher can feel like your work was graded. If you care about the system, saying the reason (“The bowls go at the back so the door shuts”) turns a silent redo into a plan you can share.

Where the fields meet. Politics would say a home is a small government, and a rule lasts when the people who live by it helped make it. That is why the score is filled in together, from both people’s own notes. The roots are ownership and structure, and quiet incentives.

Common mix-ups

A low number doesn’t mean the relationship is failing. It means the current setup needs changing, and setups change all the time. Please don’t guess the numbers. A guessed number is a feeling with a decimal point, so use your written logs. One of you can’t fill it out for both, either. Each person writes their own log and scores their own stress tank. And fair doesn’t have to mean exactly 50/50. Fair is what you both agree to with your eyes open, and an uneven split you chose together can be the fair one. Two kinds of fair looks at this.

Small children and a new baby

Small children bring a flood of new jobs, and most of them are thinking jobs, from the daycare bag and the shots to the party invitations, the next size of clothes and the nights. That’s why the mental load (see the work nobody sees) often grows after a baby arrives, usually without anyone deciding it. Log the children’s jobs too, with the thinking time, and count the nights. Hand over whole jobs, like clothes and sizes or the doctor and the shots, so the new owner does the noticing too, in their own way. If a baby is on the way, write your first job list together before the birth, plan the nights, and agree to look again at six weeks, at three months and before either of you goes back to work. In the first weeks, share by energy more than by hours, so whoever was up in the night gets the rest.

Hollie and Gareth have Alfie, who is three, and Poppy, who is eight months, and Gareth is a nurse on shifts. Their logged minutes came out nearly even, yet Hollie did almost three-quarters of the jobs nobody asked for. They left the minutes alone and moved one whole job, the children’s clothes, to Gareth. In the first two weeks Alfie went to daycare in a T-shirt in October. Hollie said nothing, heroically.

You could say “Could you own the daycare bag from now on, including noticing when we’re low on diapers? I won’t check it.”

If only one of you wants to try this

That’s common, and you can still start. Log your own week, score your own tank, and then share one small thing you noticed, without asking the other person to agree with your numbers. A dad noticed he was spending five hours a week driving his sixteen-year-old to training and back. He said so, without any accusation in it, and they agreed on a bus pass for two days a week. His son grumbled for two weeks and then started to enjoy the bus, where nobody asked him how training went. Share one finding at a time, then let it rest for a few days.

If the other person refuses to talk about the split at all, ever, that isn’t a setup problem, and no score will fix it.
