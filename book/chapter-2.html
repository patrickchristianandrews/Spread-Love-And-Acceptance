Is the split working?

Simple version 🌱 Still growingRead the full chapter

One gentle question: can the way you share the work keep going, or is one of you quietly running on empty?

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about. Nobody is lying when they say it feels even. From inside each person’s own week, it does.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    When people ask “Is this working?”, the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently. This chapter asks a calmer question, using what you both wrote down.

    Dig deeper: what the check is for

  2. Three things go in.

    How evenly the hours are shared. Whether every regular job has a clear owner (WP-03). And how stressed each of you already is from everything else going on.

    Dig deeper: where each number comes from

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    From your week-long logs, work out each person’s share of the hours. An even split scores 1. One person doing everything scores 0. It doesn’t matter which person is doing more.

    Dig deeper: the balance score

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Count the regular jobs that have both names filled in, then divide by all the jobs on the list. Leave jobs nobody owns on the list. They are the ones that matter most.

    Dig deeper: the ownership score

  5. Stress: how full each battery is.

    Each of you scores your own battery with WP-02. A setup that only works when you’re both well rested isn’t one that can last, so more stress lowers the result.

    Dig deeper: the stress score

  6. One number comes out, from 0 to 1.

    0.7 or above: the setup is holding. From 0.4 up to 0.7: something is drifting, often who owns what. Below 0.4: the setup needs a kind rethink.

    Dig deeper: the formula and what each range means

  7. Start with the weakest part.

    The single number matters less than which of the three parts pulled it down. That part tells you where one small change would help most.

    Dig deeper: reading your result

  8. It judges the setup, never a person.

    The number isn’t a verdict on who’s better or whether the relationship works. It’s where a conversation starts, never how one ends.

    Dig deeper: what the number is not

Sunday evening

Two week-long logs and two cups of tea. One list has 31 rows. The other has 18. Nobody says anything for a moment. Then one of you says, “I honestly didn’t know about half of these.”

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: See your own share honestly, from what you wrote down rather than from memory. See the way you share work as a setup you can redesign, not a flaw in you.
  • Between you and others: Put both people’s numbers on one page, and read the result as a note about the setup, never a verdict on a person. Clear owners replace blame.

In real life

  • Partners: After a new baby arrives, the hours tip heavily one way without anyone deciding it. The check shows it before resentment does.
  • Family: Two siblings share care for a parent. One lives nearby and does most of the visits. Writing down the hours helps them rebalance in other ways, like paperwork and phone calls.
  • Roommates: Everyone agrees cleaning matters, but nobody owns the bathroom. The ownership score shows the gap straight away.
  • Coworkers: Two people share one role. The same three questions still work: how the hours split, who owns what, and how stretched each person is.

Common mix-ups

  • “A low number means the relationship is failing.” It only means the current setup needs changing. Setups change all the time.
  • “A high number means nobody can complain.” A good score never silences anyone. If something feels wrong, say so.
  • “We can just guess the numbers.” A guessed number is just a mood with a decimal point. Use your written logs.
  • “Taking unowned jobs off the list will help.” It raises the number and changes nothing at home.
  • “One of us can fill it in for both.” Each person writes their own log and scores their own battery.

Try this: Each of you logs one week in WP-01, Who did what, first. The number only means something if you each wrote your own.

Next, fill in the job list in WP-03 together, including any jobs nobody owns yet.

Then each of you scores your own battery in WP-02, on the same day.

Put the three numbers into the calculator. Look first at the weakest part, pick one small change, and check again in a month.
