Is the split working? A fair look at chores at home

One gentle question: can the way you share the work keep going, or is one of you quietly running on empty?

Five Pillars: This chapter mostly teaches Pillar I, See the whole load and Pillar II, Fix the setup, not the person, with help from Pillar III, Read your state first. What are the Five Pillars?

Any given week

One of you is quietly doing eleven small things a day that the other has never had to think about. When the other one says it feels even, they mean it. From inside each person’s own week, it does.

  1. Look at the month, not last night.

    When people ask “Is this working?”, the answer usually comes from whatever happened most recently. This chapter asks a calmer question, using what you both wrote down.

    Dig deeper: what the check is for

  2. Three things go in.

    How evenly the hours are shared. Whether every regular job has a clear owner (the job list, WP-03). And how stressed each of you already is from everything else going on.

    Dig deeper: where each number comes from

  3. Balance: how the hours split.

    From your week-long logs, work out each person’s share of the hours. An even split scores 1. One person doing everything scores 0. It doesn’t matter which person is doing more. If you agreed an uneven split that suits you (say, one of you works longer hours outside the home), compare against that split instead.

    Three or more of you? Compare each person’s share with an even share (100 ÷ number of people), or with a split you agreed. The Lemonade Stand, a free page where each person lists their hours, does the arithmetic.

    Dig deeper: the balance score

  4. Ownership: does each job have a name?

    Each job gets two names: the owner, who does the job start to finish, and a backstop, who notices if it slipped and gently checks in (never a supervisor). It can be the same person, though a second name gives the job a gentle check. Count the jobs that have both names filled in, then divide by all the jobs on the list. Leave jobs nobody owns on the list. They are the ones that matter most.

    Dig deeper: the ownership score

  5. Stress: how full each stress tank is.

    Each of you rates how full your own stress tank is with the Battery & Stress Meter (WP-02). A higher score means more stress. Under 0.30 is low, 0.30 up to 0.60 is moderate, and 0.60 or more is high. If either of you is at 0.60 or more, treat that as the headline, whatever the average says. A setup that only works when you’re both well rested isn’t one that can last, so more stress lowers the result.

    Dig deeper: the stress score

  6. One number comes out, from 0 to 1.

    0.7 or above: the setup is holding. From 0.4 up to 0.7: something is drifting, often who owns what. Below 0.4: the setup needs a kind rethink. (The book’s formal name, P(Solvency), looks like a probability, but it isn’t one. It’s a simple weighted sum.)

    Dig deeper: the formula and what each range means

  7. Start with the biggest shortfall.

    The single number matters less than which of the three parts pulled it down most. In the middle band that is often ownership, which is why the calculator suggests WP-03 first. Once you’ve both seen it, what you do next is a choice you make together.

    Dig deeper: reading your result

  8. It judges the setup, never a person.

    The number isn’t a verdict on who’s better or whether the relationship works. It’s where a conversation starts, never how one ends. These tools only work when each person feels free to say no, to ask for a pause, or to keep their own notes private. If talking about the split ever feels unsafe, or one of you feels you can’t say no, stop using the tools and talk with a trained counselor, together or on your own.

    Dig deeper: what the number is not

Sunday evening

Two week-long logs and two cups of tea. One list has 31 rows. The other has 18. Nobody says anything for a moment. Then one of you says, “I honestly didn’t know about half of these.”

Inside you, and between you

  • In you: See your own share honestly, from what you wrote down rather than from memory. See the way you share work as a setup you can redesign, not a flaw in you.
  • Between you and others: Put both people’s numbers on one page, and read the result as a note about the setup, never a verdict on a person. Clear owners leave less room for blame.

The fields behind this chapter

This chapter draws on 6 of the author’s nine fields (see the Preface or the About page). The first few:

  • Finance (Pillar I): Solvency: like a small shop asking whether it can keep paying its bills, month after month. The chapter reads the setup that way, never as a verdict on the people.
  • Business (Pillar II): Ownership clarity: RACI from project work, and the maker-and-checker habit from internal controls. Every regular job gets a person who does it and a backstop who notices if it slipped.
  • Neurobiology (Pillar III): Autonomic saturation: the book’s name for a quick self-rating of the stress you’re already carrying. It borrows the idea that stress builds up in the body, but it doesn’t measure your body.
  • Economics (Pillar I): Limited capacity and the safety margin.

Two fields, one idea: Business + Economics. Unowned work falls to whoever notices first, every time. That’s what turns a fixable imbalance into a repeating one, so the check counts owners, not just the hours.

In real life

  • Partners: After a new baby arrives, the hours tip heavily one way without anyone deciding it. The check can show it before resentment builds.
  • Family: Two siblings share care for a parent. One lives nearby and does most of the visits. Writing down the hours helps them rebalance in other ways, like paperwork and phone calls.
  • Roommates: Everyone agrees cleaning matters, but nobody owns the bathroom. The ownership score shows the gap straight away.
  • Coworkers: Two people share one role. The same three questions still work: how the hours split, who owns what, and how stretched each person is.

Common mix-ups

  • “A low number means the relationship is failing.” It only means the current setup needs changing. Setups change all the time.
  • “A high number means nobody can complain.” A good score never silences anyone. If something feels wrong, say so.
  • “We can just guess the numbers.” A guessed number is really just a feeling with a decimal point. Use your written logs.
  • “Taking unowned jobs off the list will help.” It raises the number and changes nothing at home.
  • “One of us can fill it in for both.” Each person writes their own log and scores their own stress tank.

Try this: Each of you logs one week in WP-01, Who did what, first. The number only means something if you each wrote your own.

Next, fill in the job list in WP-03, the RACI Treaty, together, including any jobs nobody owns yet.

Then each of you scores how full your own stress tank is in WP-02, the Battery & Stress Meter, on the same day.

Put the three numbers into the calculator. Look first at the biggest shortfall, pick one small change together, and check again in a month.
